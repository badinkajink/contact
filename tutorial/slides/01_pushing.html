Tool #1: arrows (vectors)

  • A vector is an arrow: it has a length and a direction.
  • We write it with components: $\vf = (f_x, f_y)$ means “go $f_x$ to the right and $f_y$ up”.
  • Its length comes from Pythagoras: $$\norm{\vf} = \sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2}$$
  • Example: $\vf = (3, 2)$ has length $\sqrt{13} \approx 3.61$.

Convention: bold letters are vectors, plain letters are numbers.

Adding and scaling arrows

  • Add tip to tail: put $\vb$'s tail on $\va$'s tip. The sum runs from the start to the finish.
  • With numbers, add component by component: $(2,\ 0.5) + (0.8,\ 1.6) = (2.8,\ 2.1)$.
  • Scale: $2\va$ is twice as long; $-\va$ points the opposite way.
  • A unit vector has length 1 and wears a hat: $\nhat$. It only says “which way”.

Forces are arrows

  • A force has a size and a direction, so it is a vector. Sizes are measured in newtons (N).
  • Gravity pulls a mass $m$ straight down with force $mg$, where $g \approx 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$.
  • Several forces on one object add like arrows. Only the total, the net force, decides how the object moves.
massweight $mg$
small apple, 0.1 kg0.98 N
1 L of water, 1 kg9.81 N
box of books, 10 kg98.1 N

Rule of thumb: 1 N is about the weight of a small apple.

Newton's rule (the only one we need)

$$\sum \vf = m\,\va \qquad \text{(net force = mass} \times \text{acceleration)}$$
  • If all the forces on an object add up to zero, it does not speed up, slow down, or turn. If it was sitting still, it stays still.
  • If they don't add to zero, it accelerates in the direction of the leftover force.
  • Most of this series lives in the first case (statics): can the contacts find forces that exactly cancel everything else?
Key idea. “Holding” an object means the contact forces on it cancel every other force on it.

A box resting on the floor

  • Two forces act on the box: gravity $mg$ pulling down, and the floor pushing up with $N$.
  • The box is still, so they cancel: $N = mg$. For a 10 kg box, $N = 98.1$ N.
  • $N$ is called the normal force (“normal” means perpendicular to the surface).
  • Puzzle: how does the floor know to push with exactly 98.1 N?
  • It squishes a tiny bit, like a very stiff spring, until it pushes back enough. Rigid-body models skip the squish: the floor pushes with whatever is needed.
“Whatever is needed” is the key modeling move. Contact forces are not given by a formula of positions and velocities; they are unknowns, chosen so that the rules of contact hold. Contact solvers (tutorial/05_contact_solvers.py) are machines for finding those unknowns.

The floor can push, but never pull

  • $N \ge 0$. A floor can push; it cannot grab. Lift the box and $N$ drops to zero, not below.
  • The box can't sink into the floor either, so the gap is never negative: $\text{gap} \ge 0.$
  • At every moment at least one of them is zero: touching and maybe pushing, or apart and not pushing.
$$\text{gap} \ge 0,\quad N \ge 0,\quad \text{gap}\times N = 0$$

This “one or the other” rule is called complementarity. Simulators live and die by it; see 05_contact_solvers.py.

Zoom in: a point contact

  • The simplest model of touching: a single contact point $\vp$ where two objects meet.
  • The contact normal $\nhat$: a unit arrow perpendicular to the surface, pointing into the object being pushed.
  • The tangent $\that$: a unit arrow along the surface.
  • Any force the floor applies at $\vp$ is some mix of the two.

Splitting a contact force in two

$$\vf = f_n\,\nhat + f_t\,\that$$
  • $f_n$, the normal part: how hard the surfaces press together.
  • $f_t$, the tangential part: how hard they drag sideways. This is friction.
  • Flat floor: $\nhat = (0, 1)$ and $\that = (1, 0)$, so the force $\vf = (3, 2)$ has $f_t = 3$ and $f_n = 2$.

Tool #2: the dot product

  • $\va \cdot \vb = a_x b_x + a_y b_y$. The answer is a number, not an arrow.
  • Geometric meaning: $\va\cdot\vb = \norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between them.
  • If $\hat{\mathbf b}$ has length 1, then $\va\cdot\hat{\mathbf b}$ is the length of $\va$'s shadow on $\hat{\mathbf b}$: “how much of $\va$ points along $\hat{\mathbf b}$”.
  • So on any surface, flat or tilted: $\ f_n = \vf\cdot\nhat,\ \ f_t = \vf\cdot\that$.

Refresher: $\cos 0^\circ = 1$, $\cos 90^\circ = 0$, $\cos 180^\circ = -1$. A positive dot product means the angle is less than $90^\circ$.

Experiment: push a heavy box

  • Push gently sideways with force $P$. Nothing moves.
  • So something pushes back exactly as hard: friction from the floor, $F = P$.
  • Push harder. Still nothing: friction matches you again…
  • …until suddenly the box breaks free and slides.
Key idea. Static friction is “whatever is needed”, like the normal force, but only up to a limit.

Coulomb's law of friction

$$\abs{f_t} \;\le\; \mu\, f_n$$
  • The sideways force can be anything up to $\mu$ times the pressing force.
  • $\mu$ (“mu”) is the coefficient of friction. It has no units.
  • Press twice as hard and the friction budget $[-\mu f_n,\ \mu f_n]$ doubles.

Leonardo da Vinci wrote it in his notebooks around 1500. Amontons rediscovered it in 1699, and Coulomb studied it carefully in 1781. Engineers call it Coulomb friction.

The absolute value is there because friction can point either way along the surface: left or right in this 2D picture. In 3D the absolute value becomes the length of a 2D sideways vector (Part 2).

Real values depend on dirt, moisture, wear, and temperature. Fingertips vary a lot with how sweaty they are.

If it matters, measure it; we'll see how in a few slides.

Simulators need a number anyway: MuJoCo's default is $\mu = 1$.

Worked example: will it slide?

Setup. A 10 kg box on a floor with $\mu = 0.5$.

  • Normal force: $N = mg = 98.1$ N. Friction budget: $\mu N = 0.5 \times 98.1 = 49.05$ N.
  • Push with 30 N: $30 < 49.05$, so friction matches the push (30 N). It stays put.
  • Push with 60 N: $60 > 49.05$, so friction can't keep up. It slides.
  • Once sliding, friction is usually a bit weaker. With a kinetic coefficient $\mu_k = 0.4$: $F = 0.4 \times 98.1 = 39.2$ N. Net force $60 - 39.2 = 20.8$ N, so the acceleration is $a = 20.8 / 10 = 2.08\ \text{m/s}^2$.

Friction force vs. push

  • Sticking: friction rises one-for-one with the push.
  • Peak: $\mu_s N$ (the static limit, $\mu_s = 0.5$).
  • Sliding: friction drops to $\mu_k N$ ($\mu_k = 0.4$). That drop is why stuck things lurch when they finally move, and why chalk squeaks.

Most simulators use one $\mu$ for both. So do we, from here on.

Surprise: contact area doesn't matter

  • A brick lying flat and a brick standing on its end need the same push to slide.
  • Why? Surfaces are rough. Zoomed in, they touch only at a few tiny bumps.
  • Press harder and the bumps flatten, so the real contact area grows in proportion to the load. Friction follows the real area, so it follows the load (Bowden & Tabor, 1950).

Coulomb's law is a model, not a law of nature

  • It is an approximation: simple, and good enough for most engineering.
  • Where it bends:
    • soft materials (rubber, skin, fingertips): friction grows more slowly than the load;
    • sticky things (tape, gecko feet): they can pull, so $f_n < 0$ happens;
    • lubricated or wet surfaces, very high speeds, heat.
  • $\mu$ is not a property of one material. It belongs to a pair of surfaces, plus whatever is between them.
We use Coulomb's model because it captures the one essential fact: sideways grip is limited, and the limit grows with how hard you press.

When it slides, which way does friction point?

Opposite the sliding, at full strength:

$$f_t = -\mu\, f_n\, \operatorname{sign}(v_t)$$
  • $v_t$ is the sliding velocity along the surface; $\operatorname{sign}(v_t)$ is $+1$ or $-1$.
  • Friction never speeds things up relative to the floor. It always removes energy (turning motion into heat).

Three things a contact can be doing

modewhat you seeforce rule
separatedgap $> 0$$f_n = 0,\ f_t = 0$
stickingtouching, $v_t = 0$$f_n \ge 0,\ \abs{f_t} \le \mu f_n$
slidingtouching, $v_t \ne 0$$f_n \ge 0,\ f_t = -\mu f_n \operatorname{sign}(v_t)$
  • Every contact is in exactly one mode at each instant.
  • A simulator with $n$ contacts must work out which of the $3^n$ combinations is happening. With 10 contacts that is 59,049 combinations. This is why contact solvers are hard.

Part 1b

Drawing Coulomb's law: the friction cone

Turn the inequality into a picture. The picture turns out to be much easier to think with.

Plot every force the floor could make

  • Each possible contact force is a point $(f_t, f_n)$ in this picture.
  • Rule 1, $f_n \ge 0$: only the top half.
  • Rule 2, $\abs{f_t} \le \mu f_n$: between the lines $f_t = \pm\mu f_n$.
  • Both rules together: a wedge.

That's it. That wedge is the friction cone.

The friction cone

$$\K_\mu = \{\, (f_t, f_n) \;:\; f_n \ge 0,\ \abs{f_t} \le \mu f_n \,\}$$
  • The set of all forces the contact is able to supply.
  • Why “cone”? If a force is allowed, so is every positive multiple of it. Press 3× harder and 3× the friction is fine. So the set is made of rays from the origin.
  • In 2D it's a wedge. In 3D (Part 2) it's an ice-cream cone.

How wide is the cone? $\ \alpha = \arctan\mu$

  • Trig: $\tan\theta = \dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$.
  • On the cone's edge, $f_t / f_n = \mu$. So the edge makes an angle $\alpha$ with the normal, where $\tan\alpha = \mu$:
$$\alpha = \arctan\mu \qquad \text{(the friction angle)}$$
$\mu$0.10.30.512
$\alpha$5.7°16.7°26.6°45°63.4°

The whole cone opens $2\alpha$ wide.

Extremes

Inside, on the edge, outside

  • Inside: the contact can supply it. If this force is what's needed, the contact sticks.
  • On the edge: friction is maxed out. Sliding, or about to.
  • Outside: impossible. If the rest of the world needs this force, the contact can't deliver it, and the object slides.
Think of the cone as the contact's menu. Statics asks: “is what we need on the menu?”

The cone test, written with angles

A force is inside the cone exactly when its angle to the normal is at most $\alpha$:

$$\angle(\vf, \nhat) \le \alpha \quad\iff\quad \vf\cdot\nhat \;\ge\; \norm{\vf}\cos\alpha$$
  • Why: if $\theta$ is the angle to the normal, then $f_n = \norm{\vf}\cos\theta$ and $\abs{f_t} = \norm{\vf}\sin\theta$. So $$\abs{f_t} \le \mu f_n \iff \tan\theta \le \mu \iff \theta \le \alpha.$$
  • Grasp checks in robotics papers are usually written in this angle form. Part 4 takes one apart.

Try it: drag the force

Drag the white handle. Things to try:

  • Find the edge of the cone. What angle is it at?
  • Set $\mu = 0$. What forces are left?
  • Point the force downward (a pull).

The tilting-board experiment

  • Put a block on a board and tilt the board slowly, to angle $\theta$.
  • Gravity splits into a part along the board, $mg\sin\theta$, and a part into the board, $mg\cos\theta$.
  • The block sticks while $mg\sin\theta \le \mu\,mg\cos\theta$, that is, while $\tan\theta \le \mu$.
  • So it starts to slide when $\theta$ passes $\arctan\mu = \alpha$.
The friction angle is literally the tilt at which things start to slide. This is how $\mu$ is measured.

Same experiment, cone picture


  • To hold the block still, the board must push with exactly $-$(gravity): straight up, size $mg$.
  • The board's cone tilts with the board.
  • “Straight up” is inside the tilted cone exactly when $\theta \le \alpha$.
  • The mass cancels: heavy or light, it slips at the same angle.

Worked examples

A phone on a tilted book. Rubbery case on cardboard, $\mu \approx 0.4$: $\alpha = \arctan 0.4 \approx 21.8^\circ$. Tilted 15° it stays; tilted 25° it slides.
A car on an icy hill. Tires on ice, $\mu \approx 0.1$: $\alpha = \arctan 0.1 \approx 5.7^\circ$. That's a 10% grade (a rise of 10 m per 100 m). Any steeper and a parked car slides.
A pile of dry sand. Its sides stop at about 30–35° (the “angle of repose”). The grains' friction angle sets the slope: $\mu \approx \tan 33^\circ \approx 0.65$.

Check yourself

  1. With $\mu = 0.25$, what is the steepest ramp a box can rest on?
    answer
    $\arctan 0.25 \approx 14.0^\circ$.
  2. Double the box's weight. Does the slip angle change?
    answer
    No. Both sides of $mg\sin\theta \le \mu\,mg\cos\theta$ double.
  3. A contact force has $f_n = 10$ N and $f_t = 6$ N, with $\mu = 0.5$. Can the contact stick?
    answer
    No. The budget is $\mu f_n = 5$ N $< 6$ N. Its angle, $\arctan 0.6 \approx 31^\circ$, is outside $\alpha \approx 26.6^\circ$.
  4. You push the 10 kg box ($\mu = 0.5$) with 60 N, aimed 30° below horizontal. Does it slide?
    answer
    Sideways: $60\cos 30^\circ = 52.0$ N. Pressing: $N = 98.1 + 60\sin 30^\circ = 128.1$ N, so the budget is $64.05$ N. It does not slide. Pushing down makes it harder!

Summary

  • Forces are vectors. A contact force splits into a normal part $f_n$ and a tangential part $f_t$ (dot products with $\nhat$ and $\that$).
  • Floors push, never pull: $f_n \ge 0$.
  • Coulomb: $\abs{f_t} \le \mu f_n$. When sliding, $\abs{f_t} = \mu f_n$, opposite the slip.
  • As a picture: a wedge (cone) of half-angle $\alpha = \arctan\mu$ around the normal. Inside = stick, edge = slide, outside = impossible.
  • Tilt test: things start to slip at $\theta = \alpha$, whatever their weight.
