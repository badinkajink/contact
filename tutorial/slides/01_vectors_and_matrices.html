Part I

Vectors, span, and norms

Arrows and lists of numbers, the combinations of a few vectors, and three ways to measure the size of a vector.

Vectors as arrows and as lists of numbers

  • The arrow from the origin to the point $(3, 2)$ is the vector $\vv = (3, 2)$: 3 units right, 2 units up.
  • The same vector is a list of two numbers, its components $v_1 = 3$ and $v_2 = 2$, stacked in a column: $\vv = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
  • Length (Pythagoras): $\norm{\vv} = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2} = \sqrt{13} \approx 3.606$.
  • $\R^n$ is the set of all lists of $n$ real numbers. A planar block's velocity $(v_x, v_y, \omega)$ is in $\R^3$; the joint speeds of a 7-joint arm are in $\R^7$.
A vector is two things at once: an arrow with a length and a direction, and an ordered list of numbers. Bold lowercase letters ($\vv$, $\vu$, $\vw$) are vectors; plain italic letters are numbers; the $i$-th entry of $\vv$ is $v_i$. Vectors are columns unless said otherwise, which matters once a matrix multiplies them (slide matrix-vector-product). In $\R^2$ and $\R^3$ the arrow can be drawn. In $\R^7$ it cannot, but addition, scaling, length and the dot product still work entry by entry, which is why the decks treat them as operations on lists. The length of a vector in $\R^n$ is $\sqrt{v_1^2 + \dots + v_n^2}$: Pythagoras applied $n-1$ times, one new right angle per extra entry. Deck 11 (Forces and Coulomb friction, “Tool #1”) introduces forces as arrows more slowly; this deck is the reference. The tex (lines 1107-1108) uses the same column convention and writes matrices in bold capitals; the decks write matrices as italic capitals ($A$, $M$).

Vector addition and scalar multiplication

  • Add component by component: $\vu + \vw = (u_1 + w_1,\ u_2 + w_2)$. With $\vu = (1, 1)$ and $\vw = (-1, 3)$: $\vu + \vw = (0, 4)$.
  • Tip to tail: put the tail of $\vw$ on the tip of $\vu$. The sum runs from the start of $\vu$ to the tip of $\vw$.
  • Multiply by a number $c$ (a scalar): $c\vu = (c u_1,\ c u_2)$. $2\vu = (2, 2)$, and $-\vw = (1, -3)$ points the opposite way.
Addition and scaling are the only two operations a vector space needs, and they obey the rules of ordinary arithmetic: $\vu + \vw = \vw + \vu$; $(\vu + \vw) + \vx = \vu + (\vw + \vx)$; the zero vector $\vzero = (0, 0)$ has $\vu + \vzero = \vu$; $\vu + (-\vu) = \vzero$; $c(\vu + \vw) = c\vu + c\vw$; $(c + d)\vu = c\vu + d\vu$. Subtraction is addition of $-1$ times a vector: $\vu - \vw = \vu + (-1)\vw = (2, -2)$ for the running pair, which is the arrow from the tip of $\vw$ to the tip of $\vu$. In the figure the dashed lines complete the parallelogram with sides $\vu$ and $\vw$; its diagonal is $\vu + \vw$ whichever vector goes first, which is the picture of $\vu + \vw = \vw + \vu$. A scalar $c$ between 0 and 1 shrinks the arrow, $c > 1$ stretches it, and $c < 0$ flips it through the origin. Forces add this way (deck 11: the net force on a box is the sum of the arrows acting on it).

Linear combinations of two vectors

  • A linear combination of $\vu$ and $\vw$ is $c_1\vu + c_2\vw$ for numbers $c_1$, $c_2$.
  • $2\vu + \vw = (2, 2) + (-1, 3) = (1, 5)$.
  • $(0, 4)$ is reached with $c_1 = c_2 = 1$.
  • $(3, 1)$ needs $c_1 - c_2 = 3$ and $c_1 + 3c_2 = 1$. Subtract the first equation from the second: $4c_2 = -2$, so $c_2 = -0.5$ and $c_1 = 2.5$.

A linear combination uses only the two vector operations: scale each vector, then add. The same definition works for any number of vectors in any $\R^n$: $c_1\vv_1 + c_2\vv_2 + \dots + c_k\vv_k$. Finding the coefficients that reach a given point is the first linear system of the series: for $(3, 1)$ the two components give two equations in the two unknowns $c_1, c_2$, and subtracting one equation from the other eliminates $c_1$. Check: $2.5(1, 1) - 0.5(-1, 3) = (2.5 + 0.5,\ 2.5 - 1.5) = (3, 1)$. Part III of this deck solves such systems for any number of unknowns by Gaussian elimination. The function d01_coords solves the $2\times 2$ case with Cramer's rule, and the slide basis-and-coordinates shows that every point of the plane is reached by exactly one pair $(c_1, c_2)$.

The span of a set of vectors

  • The span of $\vv_1, \dots, \vv_k$ is the set of all their linear combinations.
  • The span of one nonzero vector is a line through $\vzero$.
  • Two vectors in $\R^2$ that are not parallel span the whole plane. Two parallel vectors, such as $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 2)$, span a line.
  • In $\R^3$, $\Span\{(1, 0, 1), (0, 1, 1)\}$ is the plane $z = x + y$ through the origin: $x(1, 0, 1) + y(0, 1, 1) = (x,\ y,\ x + y)$.
The dots in the figure are the combinations with whole-number coefficients; the span also contains every combination with fractional coefficients, which fills the gaps. A span always contains $\vzero$ (take every coefficient 0), and it is closed under addition and scaling: the sum of two combinations is a combination. A set with these properties is called a subspace (slide column-space). For the plane in $\R^3$: every combination $x(1, 0, 1) + y(0, 1, 1)$ has third entry equal to the sum of the first two, and every point with $z = x + y$ is the combination with coefficients $x$ and $y$. So a point such as $(0, 0, 1)$, whose third entry is not the sum of the first two, is outside the span. This plane returns three times in the deck: as the column space of the matrix $M$, as the image of the $3\times 2$ matrix $A_{23}^\T$, and as the row space of $A_{23}$. Drag $\vw$ onto the line of $\vu$ (the handle snaps to multiples of 0.25) to watch the dots collapse onto a line.

Linear independence

  • $\vv_1, \dots, \vv_k$ are linearly independent when $c_1\vv_1 + \dots + c_k\vv_k = \vzero$ only for $c_1 = \dots = c_k = 0$. Otherwise they are dependent: one of them is a combination of the others.
  • $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 4)$ are dependent: $2(1, 2) - (2, 4) = \vzero$.
  • $\vv_1 = (1, 0, 1)$, $\vv_2 = (0, 1, 1)$, $\vv_3 = (1, 1, 2 + h)$ are independent exactly when $h \ne 0$. At $h = 0$: $\vv_1 + \vv_2 - \vv_3 = \vzero$.
  • More than $n$ vectors in $\R^n$ are always dependent.
To test independence, solve $c_1\vv_1 + c_2\vv_2 + c_3\vv_3 = \vzero$. The three components give $c_1 + c_3 = 0$, $c_2 + c_3 = 0$ and $c_1 + c_2 + (2 + h)c_3 = 0$. The first two give $c_1 = c_2 = -c_3$; the third becomes $-2c_3 + (2 + h)c_3 = h\,c_3 = 0$. For $h \ne 0$ this forces $c_3 = 0$ and then $c_1 = c_2 = 0$: independent. For $h = 0$ any $c_3$ works, for example $(c_1, c_2, c_3) = (1, 1, -1)$: dependent. The readout also shows the $3\times 3$ determinant of $[\vv_1\ \vv_2\ \vv_3]$, the signed volume of the box with these three edges; it equals $h$, and a zero volume means the three arrows lie in one plane. The $2\times 2$ determinant is built on slide determinant-area; the $3\times 3$ one is not needed until deck 02. The rule “more than $n$ vectors in $\R^n$ are dependent” is proved on slide rank-nullity. Hou and Mason require the rows of their velocity command $C$ to be independent of each other and of the constraint rows $N$ (Hou 2019, Sec. IV-A).

Basis and coordinates

  • A basis of $\R^n$ is a set of independent vectors that spans $\R^n$. Every basis of $\R^n$ has $n$ vectors; $n$ is the dimension.
  • The standard basis $\ve_1 = (1, 0)$, $\ve_2 = (0, 1)$: $(x_1, x_2) = x_1\ve_1 + x_2\ve_2$.
  • $\vu, \vw$ are also a basis of $\R^2$. The coordinates of $\vx$ in this basis are the $c_1, c_2$ with $c_1\vu + c_2\vw = \vx$. For $\vx = (1, 5)$: $(2, 1)$.
  • Coordinates are unique: $c_1\vu + c_2\vw = d_1\vu + d_2\vw$ gives $(c_1 - d_1)\vu + (c_2 - d_2)\vw = \vzero$, so $c_1 = d_1$ and $c_2 = d_2$.
Spanning guarantees that every $\vx$ has coordinates; independence guarantees that they are unique (the last bullet uses the definition of independence with the coefficients $c_1 - d_1$ and $c_2 - d_2$). Why every basis of $\R^n$ has exactly $n$ vectors: more than $n$ vectors are dependent (slide rank-nullity), and fewer than $n$ cannot span, because a matrix with $k < n$ columns has at most $k$ pivots and its column space is smaller than $\R^n$. The same word applies to subspaces: a basis of a subspace is an independent set that spans it, and the dimension of the subspace is the size of any basis. The plane $z = x + y$ in $\R^3$ has the basis $(1, 0, 1)$, $(0, 1, 1)$ and dimension 2. Computing coordinates is solving a linear system; slide identity-and-inverse computes the coordinates $(2, 1)$ of $(1, 5)$ a second time with the inverse matrix.

Dot product, length, and angle

  • $\va\cdot\vb = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + \dots + a_n b_n$, a number.
  • Length: $\norm{\va} = \sqrt{\va\cdot\va}$.
  • Angle $\theta$ between $\va$ and $\vb$: $\va\cdot\vb = \norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}\cos\theta$ (derived on the next slide).
  • $\va = (3, 4)$, $\vb = (4, 0)$: $\va\cdot\vb = 12$, $\norm{\va} = 5$, $\norm{\vb} = 4$, $\cos\theta = 12/20 = 0.6$, $\theta = 53.13^\circ$.
  • Perpendicular (orthogonal) means $\va\cdot\vb = 0$: $(3, 4)\cdot(4, -3) = 12 - 12 = 0$.
The dot product multiplies matching entries and adds. Its rules: $\va\cdot\vb = \vb\cdot\va$; $(c\va)\cdot\vb = c(\va\cdot\vb)$; $\va\cdot(\vb + \vc) = \va\cdot\vb + \va\cdot\vc$; $\va\cdot\va \ge 0$, with equality only for $\va = \vzero$. The sign tells the angle: positive below $90^\circ$, zero at $90^\circ$, negative above. Dividing by $\norm{\vb}$ gives the signed length of the shadow of $\va$ on the line of $\vb$: here $12/4 = 3$, the $x$-component of $\va$, because $\vb$ lies on the $x$-axis. Deck 02 turns the shadow into the projection of a vector onto a line and onto a subspace. Deck 11 (“Tool #2”, Forces and Coulomb friction) uses the dot product to split a contact force into its normal part $f_n = \vf\cdot\nhat$ and tangential part $f_t = \vf\cdot\that$. In the running pair, $\vu\cdot\vw = -1 + 3 = 2$, $\norm{\vu} = \sqrt 2$, $\norm{\vw} = \sqrt{10}$, so $\cos\theta = 2/\sqrt{20} = 0.447$ and $\theta = 63.43^\circ$.

Derivation of the angle formula from the law of cosines

  1. Law of cosines for the triangle: $$\norm{\va - \vb}^2 = \norm{\va}^2 + \norm{\vb}^2 - 2\norm{\va}\norm{\vb}\cos\theta.$$
  2. Expand the left side with the dot-product rules: $$\norm{\va - \vb}^2 = (\va - \vb)\cdot(\va - \vb) = \norm{\va}^2 - 2\,\va\cdot\vb + \norm{\vb}^2.$$
  3. $\norm{\va}^2$ and $\norm{\vb}^2$ appear on both sides and cancel: $-2\,\va\cdot\vb = -2\norm{\va}\norm{\vb}\cos\theta$.
  4. Divide by $-2$: $\ \va\cdot\vb = \norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}\cos\theta$.
$\abs{\cos\theta} \le 1$, so $\abs{\va\cdot\vb} \le \norm{\va}\,\norm{\vb}$ (the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality).

In $\R^3$: $(1, 0, 1)\cdot(0, 1, 1) = 1$ and both lengths are $\sqrt 2$, so $\cos\theta = 1/2$ and $\theta = 60^\circ$.

The law of cosines, $c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\theta$, generalizes Pythagoras to triangles without a right angle; at $\theta = 90^\circ$ the last term vanishes. Step 2 uses the distributive rule twice: $(\va - \vb)\cdot(\va - \vb) = \va\cdot\va - \va\cdot\vb - \vb\cdot\va + \vb\cdot\vb$, and $\vb\cdot\va = \va\cdot\vb$. In $\R^n$ with $n > 3$ there is no triangle to draw, so the formula becomes the definition of the angle: $\cos\theta = \va\cdot\vb / (\norm{\va}\norm{\vb})$. This is a valid cosine only if the right side lies in $[-1, 1]$, which Cauchy-Schwarz guarantees. A proof that needs no picture: for every number $t$, $\norm{\va - t\vb}^2 \ge 0$, that is $\va\cdot\va - 2t\,\va\cdot\vb + t^2\,\vb\cdot\vb \ge 0$. Choose $t = \va\cdot\vb / \vb\cdot\vb$; the inequality becomes $\norm{\va}^2 - (\va\cdot\vb)^2/\norm{\vb}^2 \ge 0$, which rearranges to $(\va\cdot\vb)^2 \le \norm{\va}^2\norm{\vb}^2$. The $t$ chosen here is the shadow length of the previous slide divided by $\norm{\vb}$; deck 02 reuses it for projection.

The 1-, 2- and $\infty$-norms

A norm measures the size of a vector. Three norms appear in this series:

normformula$(3, -4)$
1-norm$\norm{\vx}_1 = \abs{x_1} + \dots + \abs{x_n}$7
2-norm$\norm{\vx}_2 = \sqrt{x_1^2 + \dots + x_n^2}$5
$\infty$-norm$\norm{\vx}_\infty = \max_i \abs{x_i}$4

The $p$-norm for $p \ge 1$: $\norm{\vx}_p = \bigl(\sum_i \abs{x_i}^p\bigr)^{1/p}$. For $(3, -4)$ it is 4.498 at $p = 3$ and 4.048 at $p = 8$. The $\infty$-norm is its limit as $p \to \infty$.

Used later: the 2-norm in least squares (deck 02) and the circular friction cone (deck 12); the $\infty$-norm in actuator limits $\abs{u_i} \le u_{\max}$ (decks 07-08).

A norm satisfies, for all vectors $\vx, \vy$ and numbers $c$:

  1. $\norm{\vx} \ge 0$, and $\norm{\vx} = 0$ only for $\vx = \vzero$;
  2. $\norm{c\vx} = \abs{c}\,\norm{\vx}$;
  3. $\norm{\vx + \vy} \le \norm{\vx} + \norm{\vy}$ (triangle inequality).
Not a norm: the formula with $p = 1/2$ breaks rule 3. $\norm{(1, 1)}_{1/2} = (1 + 1)^2 = 4$, but $\norm{(1, 0)}_{1/2} + \norm{(0, 1)}_{1/2} = 1 + 1 = 2$.
The 2-norm is the length of the previous slides. The 1-norm adds the sizes of the entries (the distance a taxi drives on a square street grid); the $\infty$-norm keeps only the largest entry. Each has its uses in the series: the 2-norm in least squares (deck 02), in the circular friction cone $\norm{\vf_t}_2 \le \mu f_n$ (deck 12) and in quadratic costs (decks 05-09); the $\infty$-norm in box constraints such as actuator limits $\norm{\vu}_\infty \le u_{\max}$ (decks 07-08); the 1-norm in costs that should drive many entries exactly to zero. The triangle inequality for the 1-norm follows from $\abs{x_i + y_i} \le \abs{x_i} + \abs{y_i}$ entry by entry, and for the 2-norm from Cauchy-Schwarz: $\norm{\vx + \vy}^2 = \norm{\vx}^2 + 2\,\vx\cdot\vy + \norm{\vy}^2 \le (\norm{\vx} + \norm{\vy})^2$. The $p$-norm values come from d01_pnorm, which refuses $p < 1$. The tex (line 1113) stated the $p$-norm without the condition $p \ge 1$ and listed $p = \infty$ as a case of the formula; both are corrected there now.

Unit balls of the 1-, 2- and $\infty$-norms

  • The unit ball of a norm is the set of $\vx$ with $\norm{\vx} \le 1$.
  • 1-norm: a diamond with corners $(\pm 1, 0)$ and $(0, \pm 1)$. 2-norm: the unit disk. $\infty$-norm: the square $-1 \le x_1, x_2 \le 1$.
  • For every $\vx$: $\norm{\vx}_\infty \le \norm{\vx}_2 \le \norm{\vx}_1$. So the diamond lies inside the disk, and the disk inside the square.
A larger norm value means a smaller unit ball: the 1-norm is the largest of the three, so its ball, the diamond, is the smallest. Proof of the two inequalities. First, $\norm{\vx}_\infty^2 = \max_i x_i^2 \le x_1^2 + \dots + x_n^2 = \norm{\vx}_2^2$. Second, $\norm{\vx}_1^2 = (\abs{x_1} + \dots + \abs{x_n})^2$ expands into $x_1^2 + \dots + x_n^2$ plus the cross terms $2\abs{x_i}\abs{x_j}$, which are never negative, so $\norm{\vx}_1^2 \ge \norm{\vx}_2^2$. At $\vx = (3, -4)$ the three values are 4, 5 and 7. Unit balls reappear as constraint sets: $\norm{\vu}_\infty \le u_{\max}$ is a box of actuator limits (decks 07-08), and $\norm{\vf_t}_2 \le \mu f_n$ is the disk of friction forces that becomes the circular friction cone (deck 12). For $p < 1$ the curve $\norm{\vx}_p = 1$ bends inward between the axes, so the “ball” is not convex; that is the picture of the failed triangle inequality of the previous slide.

Part II

Matrices as linear maps

A matrix is a table of numbers and a function that moves vectors. Products, transposes, inverses and determinants all follow from the second view.

A matrix and its product with a vector

  • An $m \times n$ matrix is a table of numbers with $m$ rows and $n$ columns. The entry $a_{ij}$ sits in row $i$ and column $j$.
  • The running matrix has the columns $\vu = (1, 1)$ and $\vw = (-1, 3)$: $$A = \begin{bmatrix} \vu & \vw \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}.$$
  • Shape rule: an $m\times n$ matrix times a vector in $\R^n$ gives a vector in $\R^m$.

Column view. $A\vx$ is the linear combination of the columns of $A$ with the entries of $\vx$ as coefficients:

$$A\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Row view. Entry $i$ of $A\vx$ is (row $i$ of $A$)$\,\cdot\,\vx$: $(1, -1)\cdot(2, 1) = 1$ and $(1, 3)\cdot(2, 1) = 5$.

Both views compute $(A\vx)_i = a_{i1}x_1 + a_{i2}x_2 + \dots + a_{in}x_n$.

The two views perform the same multiplications and additions in a different order, and each answers a different question. The column view answers “which vectors can $A\vx$ reach?”: exactly the linear combinations of the columns, which is the column space of Part III. The row view answers “which $\vx$ satisfy these equations?”: each row of $A$ dotted with $\vx$ gives one equation, which is the row picture and the null space of Part III. The formula $(A\vx)_i = \sum_j a_{ij}x_j$ is the row view written out; reading it down a column instead of across a row gives the column view. The shape rule says the number of columns of $A$ must equal the length of $\vx$; a $2\times 3$ matrix accepts vectors in $\R^3$ and returns vectors in $\R^2$ (slide wide-matrix-flattens). The product $A(2, 1) = (1, 5)$ is the linear combination $2\vu + \vw$ of slide linear-combinations, written with a matrix.

A $2\times 2$ matrix as a map of the plane

  • $A$ sends each point $\vx$ of the plane to $A\vx$.
  • Grid lines stay straight, parallel and evenly spaced. The origin stays put.
  • $A\ve_1$ (red) is column 1 and $A\ve_2$ (green) is column 2.


The figure applies $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$ to every line of the square grid. Because $A(\vx + t\vy) = A\vx + t\,A\vy$, a line through $\vx$ with direction $\vy$ maps to a line through $A\vx$ with direction $A\vy$; lines with the same direction stay parallel, and equally spaced lines stay equally spaced. The whole picture is fixed by where the two unit arrows go, which are the two columns. The presets and their determinants (slide determinant-area): identity 1; rotation by $30^\circ$, $\begin{bmatrix} 0.866 & -0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.866 \end{bmatrix}$, 1; shear $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, 1; stretch $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}$, 1; projection $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, 0; reflection $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$, $-1$; running $A$, 4. The projection flattens the plane onto the $x$-axis: every point of a vertical line lands on one point, a first example of a nonzero null space (slide null-space).

Linear maps and their matrices

  • A map $T$ from $\R^n$ to $\R^m$ is linear when $T(\vx + \vy) = T(\vx) + T(\vy)$ and $T(c\vx) = c\,T(\vx)$ for all vectors $\vx, \vy$ and numbers $c$.
  • Every matrix map $\vx \mapsto A\vx$ is linear: in the column view, $A(\vx + \vy)$ uses the coefficients $x_j + y_j$, which splits into $A\vx + A\vy$.

Every linear map is a matrix map, and column $j$ of its matrix is $T(\ve_j)$:

  1. Write $\vx = x_1\ve_1 + x_2\ve_2 + \dots + x_n\ve_n$.
  2. Apply linearity: $T(\vx) = x_1 T(\ve_1) + x_2 T(\ve_2) + \dots + x_n T(\ve_n)$.
  3. This is the column view of the product $\begin{bmatrix} T(\ve_1) & T(\ve_2) & \cdots & T(\ve_n) \end{bmatrix}\vx$.
Rotation by $90^\circ$: $T(\ve_1) = (0, 1)$ and $T(\ve_2) = (-1, 0)$, so $R = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. The shift $T(\vx) = \vx + (1, 0)$ is not linear: $T(\vzero) = (1, 0)$, but a linear map sends $\vzero$ to $\vzero$.
Linearity is the property that makes a map predictable from a few samples: once the images of the $n$ basis vectors are known, the image of every vector follows by scaling and adding. The three steps are the proof; step 1 uses the standard basis of slide basis-and-coordinates, step 2 uses both linearity rules, and step 3 is the column view of slide matrix-vector-product. A linear map always sends $\vzero$ to $\vzero$, because $T(\vzero) = T(0\cdot\vzero) = 0\cdot T(\vzero) = \vzero$; the shift fails this test. Deck 03 uses this slide in reverse: a smooth nonlinear map is close to a linear map near any point, and the matrix of that linear map, the Jacobian, has as column $j$ the rate of change of the map along $\ve_j$ (the partial derivatives). Deck 17 builds rotation matrices in $\R^3$ the same way: column $j$ is where the rotation sends $\ve_j$.

Matrix multiplication as composition of maps

  • “First $A$, then $B$” is $\vx \mapsto B(A\vx)$. Its matrix is the product $BA$; the right factor acts first.
  • Column $j$ of $BA$ is $B$ times column $j$ of $A$, so $(BA)_{ij}$ = (row $i$ of $B$)$\,\cdot\,$(column $j$ of $A$).
  • Shapes: $(m\times n)(n\times p)$ is $m\times p$. $(AB)C = A(BC)$ always; $AB = BA$ rarely.
$$S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\ \text{shear},\quad R = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\ \text{rotation}$$ $$RS = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \ne SR = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
Why column $j$ of $BA$ is $B\va_j$: the product $BA$ must send $\ve_j$ to $B(A\ve_j) = B\va_j$, where $\va_j$ is column $j$ of $A$, and column $j$ of a matrix is where it sends $\ve_j$ (slide linearity). Entry $i$ of $B\va_j$ is row $i$ of $B$ dotted with $\va_j$, which gives the entry rule. Worked out: $RS = \begin{bmatrix} 0\cdot 1 + (-1)\cdot 0 & 0\cdot 1 + (-1)\cdot 1 \\ 1\cdot 1 + 0\cdot 0 & 1\cdot 1 + 0\cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Associativity holds because both sides are the same composite map “first $C$, then $B$, then $A$”. The transpose

  • The transpose $A^\T$ swaps rows and columns: $(A^\T)_{ij} = a_{ji}$. An $m \times n$ matrix becomes $n \times m$. $$A_{23} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix},\quad A_{23}^\T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$
  • A dot product is a matrix product: $\va\cdot\vb = \va^\T\vb$, a $1\times n$ row times an $n\times 1$ column.
  • $(AB)^\T = B^\T A^\T$. A matrix with $A^\T = A$ is symmetric.

Moving a matrix to the other side of a dot product transposes it:

$$(A\vx)\cdot\vy = \vx\cdot(A^\T\vy).$$

Proof: $(A\vx)\cdot\vy = (A\vx)^\T\vy = \vx^\T A^\T\vy = \vx\cdot(A^\T\vy)$.

$\vx = (1, 2, 3)$, $\vy = (1, -1)$: $A_{23}\vx = (4, 5)$ and $(4, 5)\cdot(1, -1) = -1$; $A_{23}^\T\vy = (1, -1, 0)$ and $(1, 2, 3)\cdot(1, -1, 0) = -1$.
The rule $(AB)^\T = B^\T A^\T$ reverses the order: entry $(i, j)$ of $(AB)^\T$ is entry $(j, i)$ of $AB$, which is row $j$ of $A$ dotted with column $i$ of $B$, which is row $i$ of $B^\T$ dotted with column $j$ of $A^\T$. The proof on the slide applies it to the product of $A$ and the one-column matrix $\vx$. The identity $(A\vx)\cdot\vy = \vx\cdot(A^\T\vy)$ is how forces move between coordinate systems. In deck 17 a Jacobian $J$ maps joint velocities $\vv$ to a contact-point velocity $J\vv$; the power of a contact force $\vlambda$ is $(J\vv)\cdot\vlambda = \vv\cdot(J^\T\vlambda)$, so the joint torques that do the same work are $\vtau = J^\T\vlambda$. Symmetric matrices, such as $A^\T A$ and the inertia matrix $M$ of the tex (line 1123), are the subject of deck 02: $(A^\T A)^\T = A^\T (A^\T)^\T = A^\T A$.

The identity matrix and the inverse

  • The identity $I$ has 1 on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere: $I\vx = \vx$ for every $\vx$, and $AI = IA = A$.
  • The inverse $A^{-1}$ undoes $A$: $A^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = I$. Then $A\vx = \vb$ has the one solution $\vx = A^{-1}\vb$.
  • $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$: the last map is undone first.
  • Only square matrices have inverses. A $2\times 2$ matrix has one exactly when $ad - bc \ne 0$.
$$\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$$
$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ has $ad - bc = 3 + 1 = 4$, so $$A^{-1} = \tfrac14\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix},\quad A^{-1}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac14\begin{bmatrix} 3 + 5 \\ -1 + 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Check: $\tfrac14\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \tfrac14\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = I$.

$A^{-1}(1, 5) = (2, 1)$ are the coordinates of $(1, 5)$ in the basis $\vu, \vw$ (slide basis-and-coordinates): the inverse of a matrix whose columns are a basis converts a vector into its coordinates in that basis. The $2\times 2$ formula can be checked by multiplying: $\begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} ad - bc & 0 \\ 0 & ad - bc \end{bmatrix}$, so dividing by $ad - bc$ gives $I$. When $ad - bc = 0$ no inverse exists: the map squashes the plane onto a line (slide determinant-area), and two different points that land on the same point cannot both be recovered. The reversed order in $(AB)^{-1}$ follows from $(B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB) = B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B = B^{-1}B = I$. A non-square matrix has no inverse; deck 02 builds the pseudoinverse, which picks the shortest solution when there are many and the best approximate solution when there are none.

The $2\times 2$ determinant as an area scale factor

  • $\det A = ad - bc$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$.
  • $\abs{\det A}$ is the area of the image of the unit square. $A$ multiplies every area by this factor.
  • $\det A < 0$: the image is mirrored (the turn from $A\ve_1$ to $A\ve_2$ is clockwise).
  • $\det A = 0$: the columns are parallel, the plane is squashed onto a line, and $A$ has no inverse.
  • Running $A$: $\det A = 1\cdot 3 - (-1)\cdot 1 = 4$.
The unit square has corners $\vzero$, $\ve_1$, $\ve_1 + \ve_2$, $\ve_2$; its image has corners $\vzero$, $A\ve_1$, $A\ve_1 + A\ve_2$, $A\ve_2$, a parallelogram on the two columns. The next slide derives its area $ad - bc$. Every other region is a limit of small squares, each scaled by the same factor, so every area is multiplied by $\abs{\det A}$. The sign records orientation: for the identity the turn from $\ve_1$ to $\ve_2$ is counter-clockwise and $\det I = 1$; a reflection reverses it and has $\det = -1$. Two rules used later: $\det(AB) = \det A\,\det B$ (the areas are scaled twice), and $\det A \ne 0$ exactly when $A$ has an inverse. The $n \times n$ determinant is the signed volume scale factor in $\R^n$; slide linear-independence met it as the box volume $h$. The drag handles snap to multiples of 0.25 so that parallel columns, and $\det A = 0$, can be hit exactly.

Derivation of the parallelogram area $ad - bc$

  1. Box: $(a + b)(c + d) = 4 \cdot 3 = 12$.
  2. Two red triangles with legs $a$, $c$: together $ac = 3$.
  3. Two blue triangles with legs $b$, $d$: together $bd = 2$.
  4. Two green rectangles $b \times c$: together $2bc = 2$.
$(a + b)(c + d) - ac - bd - 2bc = ad - bc$. Here $12 - 3 - 2 - 2 = 5 = 3\cdot 2 - 1\cdot 1$.
Expanding the box area: $(a + b)(c + d) = ac + ad + bc + bd$. Subtracting $ac + bd + 2bc$ leaves $ad - bc$. The picture assumes both columns point into the first quadrant with $(a, c)$ below $(b, d)$ and $b \le a$, $c \le d$; the other arrangements cut the box differently and give the same formula up to sign, which is the orientation of the previous slide. The notebook function d01_parallelogram_area(3, 1, 1, 2) returns the box 12, the pieces 3, 2 and 2, and the area 5, equal to $ad - bc$. A second derivation uses shears. Sliding one side of a parallelogram along the direction of the other side keeps its base and height, so it keeps its area. Replace $(b, d)$ by $(b, d) - \tfrac{b}{a}(a, c) = (0,\ d - \tfrac{bc}{a})$, then replace $(a, c)$ by $(a, 0)$ the same way: the result is a rectangle of width $a$ and height $d - bc/a$, with area $ad - bc$ (for $a \ne 0$). A $2\times 3$ matrix flattens space onto the plane

  • $A_{23} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ maps $\R^3$ to $\R^2$: $(x, y, z) \mapsto (x + z,\ y + z)$. The unit cube lands on a hexagon.
  • Every point of the line $(2, 1, 0) + t(-1, -1, 1)$ lands on $(2, 1)$, because $A_{23}(-1, -1, 1) = \vzero$.
The columns of $A_{23}$ are the images of the three unit arrows: $\ve_1 \mapsto (1, 0)$, $\ve_2 \mapsto (0, 1)$ and $\ve_3 \mapsto (1, 1)$. The eight corners of the unit cube map to seven distinct points, $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$, $(1, 1)$ (twice: from $(1, 1, 0)$ and from $(0, 0, 1)$), $(2, 1)$, $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 2)$, whose outline is the hexagon. A map from $\R^3$ onto a plane must lose one direction; here it loses $(-1, -1, 1)$, and every line with that direction collapses to a single point. The set of vectors that land on $\vzero$ is the null space of slide null-space. The red line in the left panel is the set of solutions of $A_{23}\vx = (2, 1)$; slide solution-set returns to it, and its point at $t = 1$, $(1, 0, 1)$, is the shortest solution. In robotics a wide matrix appears whenever more joints move than the task measures: a 7-joint arm has a $6\times 7$ Jacobian from joint speeds to hand velocity, which always leaves a line of joint motions that the hand does not feel.

A $3\times 2$ matrix embeds the plane in space

  • $B = A_{23}^\T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ maps $\R^2$ to $\R^3$: $(s_1, s_2) \mapsto (s_1,\ s_2,\ s_1 + s_2)$.
  • The image is the plane $z = x + y$. Different $\vs$ land on different points.
  • $(0, 0, 1)$ is off the plane, so $B\vs = (0, 0, 1)$ has no solution.

Drag $\vs$ in the left panel.

The image of $B$ is the span of its two columns $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$, the plane of slide span. $B$ loses nothing: if $B\vs = B\vs'$ then the first two entries give $s_1 = s_1'$ and $s_2 = s_2'$. What it cannot do is reach most of $\R^3$. The point $(0, 0, 1)$ (marked with a cross) has $z - x - y = 1$, and every point of the image has $z - x - y = 0$, so no $\vs$ reaches it. A tall system such as $B\vs = (0, 0, 1)$, with more equations than unknowns, usually has no solution; deck 02 replaces it by the least-squares problem of finding the point of the plane closest to the target. Tall and wide matrices are transposes of each other here: $A_{23}$ flattens $\R^3$ onto $\R^2$, and its transpose $B$ lifts $\R^2$ into a plane in $\R^3$. The plane that $B$ reaches is the row space of $A_{23}$, the set of vectors perpendicular to $A_{23}$'s lost direction $(-1, -1, 1)$ (slide row-space-and-rank).

Row picture and column picture of a $2\times 2$ system

  • Column picture: $x\vu + y\vw = \vb$. The same numbers are the coefficients that reach $\vb = (1, 5)$: $2\vu + 1\vw = \vb$.

The system $x - y = 1$, $x + 3y = 5$ is $A\vx = \vb$ with the running matrix $A = [\vu\ \vw]$ and $\vb = (1, 5)$. The two pictures read the same equations in two directions. Across the rows: row $i$ of $A$ dotted with $\vx$ must equal $b_i$, one line per equation in the plane of the unknowns $(x, y)$. Down the columns: $A\vx$ is the combination $x\vu + y\vw$, and the question is which coefficients reach $\vb$ in the plane of the outputs. With $m$ equations in $n$ unknowns, the row picture has $m$ lines, planes or hyperplanes in $\R^n$, and the column picture has $n$ arrows in $\R^m$. Because $\det A = 4 \ne 0$, every $\vb$ has exactly one solution, $\vx = A^{-1}\vb$; dragging $\vb$ moves both lines and never makes them parallel. Replacing an equation by an inequality, row $i$ of $A$ dotted with $\vx$ at most $b_i$, turns its line into a half-plane; a stack of such rows, $A\vz \le \vb$, describes the feasible regions of the linear programs in deck 07.

One solution, no solution, or infinitely many solutions

  • $\det\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & k \end{bmatrix} = k - 1$.
  • $k \ne 1$: the lines cross once. One solution; at $k = 3$, $c = 4$ it is $(1, 1)$.
  • $k = 1$, $c \ne 2$: parallel lines. No solution: the system is inconsistent.
  • $k = 1$, $c = 2$: one line written twice. Infinitely many solutions.
A system is consistent when it has at least one solution and inconsistent when it has none. For two equations in two unknowns the three cases are visible: two lines cross once, never, or everywhere. No linear system has exactly two solutions. If $\vx$ and $\vy$ both solve $A\vx = \vb$, then so does every point $\vx + s(\vy - \vx)$ of the line through them, because $A(\vx + s(\vy - \vx)) = \vb + s(\vb - \vb) = \vb$. Worked case $k = 3$, $c = 4$: subtract the first equation from the second to get $2y = 2$, so $y = 1$ and $x = 1$. At $k = 1$ the same subtraction gives $0 = c - 2$, which is true for $c = 2$ (the second equation adds nothing) and false otherwise. The readout prints the ranks of $A$ and of $[A \mid \vb]$; slide consistency turns their comparison into a test that works for any size. For the infinite case the readout shows the shortest solution, which deck 02 computes with the pseudoinverse.

Gaussian elimination on a $3\times 3$ system

$x + y + z = 6$, $\ 2x + 3y + z = 11$, $\ x - y + 2z = 5$, written as the augmented matrix $[A \mid \vb]$:

$$\left[\begin{array}{rrr|r} 1 & 1 & 1 & 6 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 & 11 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 & 5 \end{array}\right]$$

$R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$, $\ R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - R_1$

$$\left[\begin{array}{rrr|r} 1 & 1 & 1 & 6 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 0 & -2 & 1 & -1 \end{array}\right]$$

$R_3 \leftarrow R_3 + 2R_2$

$$\left[\begin{array}{rrr|r} 1 & 1 & 1 & 6 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 & -3 \end{array}\right]$$

Back substitution, bottom row first:

  1. $-z = -3$, so $z = 3$.
  2. $y - z = -1$, so $y = 2$.
  3. $x + y + z = 6$, so $x = 1$.
Each row operation is undone by another one (swap back; divide by the factor; subtract the multiple added), so no operation changes the solution set. Check: $(1, 2, 3)$ satisfies all three original equations.
Elimination uses three row operations: swap two rows, multiply a row by a nonzero number, and add a multiple of one row to another. A row operation on the augmented matrix is the same operation on the equations, so any $\vx$ that satisfied the old equations satisfies the new ones; because each operation can be reversed, the converse holds too. Forward elimination clears the entries below each pivot, column by column, until the matrix is upper triangular; back substitution then solves from the bottom row up. Check of the answer: $1 + 2 + 3 = 6$, $2 + 6 + 3 = 11$, $1 - 2 + 6 = 5$. Gauss-Jordan elimination also clears the entries above each pivot and scales each pivot to 1, which replaces back substitution by more row operations; d01_rref_steps and the stepper on the next slide perform its seven operations on this system and end with the column $(1, 2, 3)$ next to the identity matrix. Numerical codes add partial pivoting (swapping the largest entry into the pivot position) to control rounding error; decks 02 and 06 use library solvers that do this.

Reduced row echelon form and pivots

  • The pivot of a row is its first nonzero entry. In echelon form each pivot sits to the right of the pivot above it, and zero rows are at the bottom.
  • Reduced row echelon form (RREF): every pivot is 1 and is the only nonzero entry of its column.
  • Columns with a pivot are pivot columns; the others are free columns. The number of pivots is the rank.
  • Every matrix has exactly one RREF.
The stepper runs Gauss-Jordan elimination with the hand rule: in each column, take the first row at or below the current one with a nonzero entry, swap it up, scale the pivot to 1, and subtract multiples of the pivot row from every other row. The yellow cell is the pivot in use and the gray row is the row the operation changed; at the last step every pivot is outlined. For $A_{34} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 & 1 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$ the operations are $R_2 - 2R_1$, $R_3 - 3R_1$, $R_3 - R_2$. Column 2 gets no pivot, because after the first step its entries below row 1 are all zero, so $x_2$ is free; the pivots land in columns 1 and 3 and the rank is 2. Different sequences of row operations reach the same RREF, which is why the rank and the pivot columns belong to the matrix and not to the method. The "table E" option is the equilibrium system of slide four-legged-table; it needs a swap in column 2. The same rule is Num.rref in lib/num.js, checked against d01_rref_steps by the twin tests.

Consistent and inconsistent systems

  • Elimination can end with a row $[0\ \cdots\ 0 \mid c]$. With $c \ne 0$ that row reads $0 = c$, and the system has no solution.
  • Such a row adds a pivot to $[A \mid \vb]$ that $A$ does not have.
$A\vx = \vb$ is consistent exactly when $\rank A = \rank\,[A \mid \vb]$.

A block on a table cannot move down: $v_y = 0$. A command to move down at 0.1 m/s adds $v_y = -0.1$:

$$\left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -0.1 \end{array}\right] \to \left[\begin{array}{rr|r} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -0.1 \end{array}\right]$$

$M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ has third row equal to the sum of the first two. $R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - R_1 - R_2$ on $[M \mid \vb]$ with $\vb = (1, 2, b_3)$:

$$\left[\begin{array}{rrr|c} 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & b_3 - 3 \end{array}\right]$$
  • $b_3 = 3$: $\rank M = \rank\,[M \mid \vb] = 2$. Consistent.
  • $b_3 = 4$: the last row says $0 = 1$; $\rank M = 2$, $\rank\,[M \mid \vb] = 3$. Inconsistent.
The rank test works because elimination brings $[A \mid \vb]$ to echelon form without changing either rank. If the last column has no pivot, every pivot sits in the $A$ part, the two ranks agree, and back substitution produces a solution (choose any values for the free variables). If the last column has a pivot, that pivot row is $[0\ \cdots\ 0 \mid 1]$ after scaling, the equation $0 = 1$ has no solution, and $\rank\,[A \mid \vb]$ exceeds $\rank A$ by one. For $M$, subtracting rows 1 and 2 from row 3 gives $b_3 - 1 - 2$ in the last column. The block example is Hou and Mason's infeasible case (2019, Fig. 1, right): a velocity command that pushes into a table contradicts the table's constraint. In the matrix the two rows $(0, 1)$ are identical, so $\rank A = 1$, while $\rank\,[A \mid \vb] = 2$. Deck 02 shows a second failure: rows that are nearly parallel make a consistent system fragile, so that a small error in $\vb$ produces a large change in $\vx$.

The column space

  • A subspace contains $\vzero$ and is closed under addition and scaling: a line or plane through the origin, $\{\vzero\}$, or the whole space.
  • The column space $\Col(A) = \{A\vx\}$ is the span of the columns: a subspace of $\R^m$.
  • $A\vx = \vb$ is consistent exactly when $\vb \in \Col(A)$.
  • $\Col(M)$ is the plane $z = x + y$: $(1, 2, b_3)$ is on it only for $b_3 = 3$.
$M\vx$ is the combination $x_1\vv_1 + x_2\vv_2 + x_3(\vv_1 + \vv_2)$ of its columns, so the set of all outputs is the span of the columns. The third column adds nothing: $\Col(M) = \Span\{\vv_1, \vv_2\}$, the plane $z = x + y$ of slide span. A basis of $\Col(A)$ is the set of pivot columns of $A$ itself. Row operations change the columns (the RREF of $M$ has columns in the plane $z = 0$), but they keep every relation among them: $A\vx = \vzero$ exactly when $R\vx = \vzero$. In the RREF the pivot columns are independent and every free column is a combination of them, so the same holds for the columns of $A$ in the same positions. For $M$ the pivot columns are 1 and 2, the basis is $\vv_1, \vv_2$, and $\dim\Col(M) = 2 = \rank M$. The null space

  • The null space $\Null(A) = \{\vx : A\vx = \vzero\}$ is a subspace of $\R^n$: if $A\vx = A\vy = \vzero$, then $A(\vx + c\vy) = \vzero$.
  • $A_{23}\vx = \vzero$ says $x + z = 0$ and $y + z = 0$, so $\Null(A_{23})$ is the line $t(-1, -1, 1)$.
  • Each row of $A$ dotted with a null vector gives 0: the rows are perpendicular to $\Null(A)$.
  • A contact that allows no motion along its normal gives $J_c\vv = \vzero$; the allowed velocities are $\Null(J_c)$ (decks 18, 21).
The null space collects the inputs that a matrix sends to zero: the directions the map loses. For $A_{23}$ these are the points of the line through $\vzero$ in direction $(-1, -1, 1)$, the same direction that slide wide-matrix-flattens saw collapse onto a single point. The closure proof on the slide uses linearity: $A(\vx + c\vy) = A\vx + cA\vy = \vzero + c\vzero$. Entry $i$ of $A\vx$ is row $i$ dotted with $\vx$, so $A\vx = \vzero$ says that $\vx$ is perpendicular to every row, and then to every combination of rows. In the figure the red line meets the yellow plane of the rows at a right angle; deck 02 proves that the null space and the row space are orthogonal complements. The matrix $M$ has the same null space as $A_{23}$: its first two rows are those of $A_{23}$, and its third row is their sum, which adds no new condition. In decks 18 and 21 the constraint Jacobian plays the role of $A$, and the velocities that keep every contact closed form its null space.

  4. Every null vector is $\vx = x_2\vn_1 + x_4\vn_2$: $\vn_1, \vn_2$ are a basis and $\dim\Null(A_{34}) = 2$.
Check: $A_{34}\vn_1 = (-2 + 2,\ -4 + 4,\ -6 + 6) = \vzero$ and $A_{34}\vn_2 = (-1 + 1,\ -2 - 2 + 4,\ -3 - 2 + 5) = \vzero$.
$A\vx = \vzero$ and $R\vx = \vzero$ have the same solutions, because row operations do not change solution sets, so the null space can be read off the RREF. Each nonzero row of $R$ expresses one pivot variable in terms of the free variables; the free variables can take any values. The vectors $\vn_1$ and $\vn_2$ are called the special solutions. They are independent: in entry 2 only $\vn_1$ is nonzero, and in entry 4 only $\vn_2$ is, so $c_1\vn_1 + c_2\vn_2 = \vzero$ forces $c_1 = c_2 = 0$. They span the null space, because any null vector $\vx$ equals $x_2\vn_1 + x_4\vn_2$: both sides have the same free entries, and the pivot entries are fixed by the free ones. The same recipe works for any matrix: one special solution per free column. These bases are not orthonormal; Num.nullspace in lib/num.js returns an orthonormal basis of the same subspace, the convention Hou and Mason use, and deck 02 builds it.

The row space and the rank

  • The row space $\Row(A)$ is the span of the rows of $A$, a subspace of $\R^n$. It equals $\Col(A^\T)$.
  • A row operation replaces a row by a combination of rows and can be undone, so it does not change the row space.
  • A basis of $\Row(A_{34})$: the nonzero rows of $R$, $(1, 2, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 0, 1, 2)$.
  • A basis of $\Col(A_{34})$: columns 1 and 3 of $A_{34}$, $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$.
$\dim\Row(A) = \dim\Col(A) = $ number of pivots $= \rank A$. For $A_{34}$ all three equal 2.

Rows are perpendicular to null vectors: $(1, 2, 0, 1)\cdot(-2, 1, 0, 0) = 0$, $(1, 2, 0, 1)\cdot(-1, 0, -2, 1) = 0$, $(0, 0, 1, 2)\cdot(-2, 1, 0, 0) = 0$, $(0, 0, 1, 2)\cdot(-1, 0, -2, 1) = 0$.

The nonzero rows of the RREF are independent: each has a 1 in a pivot column where the other rows have 0. They span the row space, which row operations do not change, so they are a basis, and $\dim\Row(A)$ is the number of pivots. The column space has the pivot columns of $A$ as a basis (slide column-space), so $\dim\Col(A)$ is the same number. That common number is the rank, and $\rank A = \rank A^\T$ follows. A fourth subspace completes the set: the left null space $\Null(A^\T)$, the combinations of rows that give zero. For $A_{34}$ it is spanned by $(-1, -1, 1)$, because row 3 equals row 1 plus row 2; its dimension is $m - \rank A = 1$. d01_four_subspaces returns bases of all four. The dot products on the slide illustrate the fact of slide null-space: every row, hence every vector of the row space, is perpendicular to every null vector. Deck 02 proves the stronger statement that the row space contains every vector perpendicular to the null space, and Hou and Mason's rank tests for goal inclusion (2021, eq. 11-13) rest on it.

The rank-nullity theorem

For an $m\times n$ matrix, $\rank A + \dim\Null(A) = n$.
  1. Each of the $n$ columns of the RREF is a pivot column or a free column.
  2. There are $\rank A$ pivot columns, so $n - \rank A$ free columns.
  3. Each free column gives one special solution. These are a basis of $\Null(A)$ (previous slide), so $\dim\Null(A) = n - \rank A$.

A wide matrix ($n > m$) has $\rank A \le m < n$, so its null space contains a nonzero vector. Hence more than $m$ vectors in $\R^m$ are dependent.

The theorem splits the $n$ input directions of a map into two counts: the rank counts the independent directions that reach the output, and the nullity $\dim\Null(A)$ counts the directions that are lost. The proof counts columns of the RREF: every column either holds a pivot or it does not, and the special solutions of slide null-space-basis are one per free column. The rank is at most the number of rows and at most the number of columns, because each pivot uses one of each. The consequence at the bottom proves the claim of slide linear-independence: put $k > m$ vectors of $\R^m$ as the columns of an $m\times k$ matrix; its null space contains some $\vc \ne \vzero$, and $c_1\vv_1 + \dots + c_k\vv_k = \vzero$ is a dependency. Hou and Mason's maximum number of velocity commands, $n - \rank N = \dim\Null(N)$ (2019, eq. 9), is this theorem applied to the constraint matrix $N$.

Solution set of $A\vx = \vb$ as a particular solution plus the null space

If $A\vx_p = \vb$, the solutions of $A\vx = \vb$ are exactly the vectors $\vx_p + \vn$ with $\vn \in \Null(A)$.
  • Each is a solution: $A(\vx_p + \vn) = \vb + \vzero = \vb$.
  • Each solution $\vx$ has this form: $A(\vx - \vx_p) = \vb - \vb = \vzero$.
  • $A_{23}\vx = (2, 1)$: $\vx_p = (2, 1, 0)$, so $\vx = (2, 1, 0) + t(-1, -1, 1)$. At $t = 1$, $(1, 0, 1)$ has length $\sqrt 2 = 1.414$, the shortest.

The particular solution $\vx_p$ comes from the RREF of $[A \mid \vb]$ with every free variable set to 0: for $A_{23}$ the free variable is $z$, and $z = 0$ gives $x = 2$, $y = 1$. Any other particular solution works as well, since two particular solutions differ by a null vector. The solution set is a copy of the null space shifted by $\vx_p$: a line parallel to the gray one, which the sliders $b_1$ and $b_2$ move sideways. The length of $\vx(t)$ is $\sqrt{(b_1 - t)^2 + (b_2 - t)^2 + t^2}$; setting the derivative of its square to zero gives $t = (b_1 + b_2)/3$, which is $t = 1$ for $\vb = (2, 1)$, with length $\sqrt 2$ against $\sqrt 5 = 2.236$ at $t = 0$. The shortest solution is the one perpendicular to the null space: $(1, 0, 1)\cdot(-1, -1, 1) = 0$. Leg forces of a four-legged table

  • A 100 N load sits at the centre of a square table with legs at $(\pm 0.5, \pm 0.5)$ m. The unknowns are the four leg forces $f_1, \dots, f_4$ (N, up).
  • Equilibrium: the forces sum to 100 N, and the moments about the $x$ and $y$ axes vanish: $$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 & -0.5 & -0.5 \\ 0.5 & -0.5 & -0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}\vf = \begin{bmatrix} 100 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
  • $\rank E = 3$, so $\dim\Null(E) = 1$: $\vf = 25(1, 1, 1, 1) + t(1, -1, 1, -1)$ N.
  • Legs push only for $\abs{t} \le 25$ N. Statics cannot fix $t$: the table is statically indeterminate.
Legs 1 to 4 stand at $(0.5, 0.5)$, $(-0.5, 0.5)$, $(-0.5, -0.5)$ and $(0.5, -0.5)$ m. A vertical force $f_i$ at $(x_i, y_i)$ has moment $y_i f_i$ about the $x$ axis and $-x_i f_i$ about the $y$ axis, so the second and third rows of $E$ hold the $y_i$ and the $x_i$. Four unknowns and three independent equations leave one free direction, $(1, -1, 1, -1)$: the diagonal pairs trade load, and $E(1, -1, 1, -1) = \vzero$ because the pairs balance in every row. The particular solution $25(1, 1, 1, 1)$ is the even split. With $t = 10$: $\vf = (35, 15, 35, 15)$ N, and $E\vf = (100, 0, 0)$. A leg can push but not pull, so the physical solutions are $-25 \le t \le 25$; at the ends two diagonal legs carry everything and the table rocks. Which $t$ occurs depends on leg lengths and floor stiffness, which rigid-body statics does not model. Homogeneous and non-homogeneous systems

  • Homogeneous: $A\vx = \vzero$. Always consistent ($\vx = \vzero$ works); the solution set is $\Null(A)$, a subspace.
  • Non-homogeneous: $A\vx = \vb$ with $\vb \ne \vzero$. The solution set is empty or $\vx_p + \Null(A)$, which does not contain $\vzero$.

Rank $r$, $n$ unknowns: $r = n$ gives at most one solution; $r < n$ gives none or a family with $n - r$ free parameters.

A homogeneous system always has the solution $\vx = \vzero$, so the only question is whether it has others; it does exactly when $\rank A < n$. A non-homogeneous system can fail to have any solution (slide consistency); when it has one, it has as many as the homogeneous system, shifted. So the homogeneous system decides how many solutions there are and the right-hand side decides whether there are any. The solution set of a non-homogeneous system is not a subspace: it misses $\vzero$, and the sum of two solutions solves $A\vx = 2\vb$ instead. Such a shifted subspace is called an affine set. Hou and Mason use both kinds. Their natural constraints are homogeneous, $N\vv = \vzero$, and their goals and velocity commands are non-homogeneous, $G\vv = \vb_G$ and $C\vv = \vb_C$ (2019, Sec. IV-A). They first make the homogeneous systems $[N; C]\vv = \vzero$ and $[N; G]\vv = \vzero$ share their solutions and then choose $\vb_C$ from one particular solution, which is this slide's theorem used twice.

$G = I$ allows only $\vv^*$, and $\vv^*$ satisfies every goal $G\vv = G\vv^*$: the identity is the strictest goal.
Hou and Mason (2019, Sec. III-B) state the goal of each control step as an affine constraint on the generalized velocity. The goal can be a whole desired velocity, in which case $G = I$, or it can involve only some entries, which leaves the others free for the controller to choose. In the example the block should slide right at 0.1 m/s. Asking only for $v_x = 0.1$ leaves $v_y$ and $\omega$ free: a plane. Asking also for $\omega = 0$ leaves a line along $v_y$. Asking for all three leaves the one point $\vv^*$. Each added independent row raises the rank by one and removes one dimension, by rank-nullity, and each solution set sits inside the one before it. The identity has the largest possible rank, $n$, so its solution set has dimension 0: it pins every entry of the velocity. In a system with $n$ velocity entries, $G = I$ therefore asks the controller for the most, and slide velocity-command-count shows that it needs the largest number of velocity commands. The select box highlights one goal; the figure always draws all three sets. In the select boxes a semicolon separates rows: $[1\ 0\ 0;\ 0\ 0\ 1]$ is the $2\times 3$ matrix of the table.

A contact constraint stacked with a velocity goal

  • The table stops the block from moving down: $N\vv = \vzero$ with $N = [0\ 1\ 0]$.
  • Contact and goal together: $\begin{bmatrix} N \\ G \end{bmatrix}\vv = \begin{bmatrix} \vzero \\ \vb_G \end{bmatrix}$.
  • Consistent exactly when the stacked matrix and its augmented matrix have equal rank. Then $\dim\mathrm{Sol}(N \,\&\, G) = 3 - \rank\begin{bmatrix} N \\ G \end{bmatrix}$.
  • $G = I$ with $b_y < 0$ asks the block to move into the table: inconsistent.
Hou and Mason write the contact as a natural constraint $N\vv = \vzero$, where $N = J_\Phi\Omega$ comes from differentiating the holonomic constraint $\Phi(q) = 0$ (2019, eq. 3; deck 03 derives it). Here the block keeps contact with the table, so its vertical velocity is zero. The velocities that meet both the contact and the goal solve the stacked system, written $\mathrm{Sol}(N \,\&\, G)$ in the paper. The four goals in the select box give four cases, all computed by d01_stacked_ranks. $G = [1\ 0\ 0]$: ranks 1 and 2, a line ($\omega$ free). $G = [1\ 0\ 0;\ 0\ 0\ 1]$: rank 3, the point $(0.1, 0, 0)$. $G = I$: rank 3; consistent only for $b_y = 0$, because the augmented matrix has rank 4 otherwise. $G = [0\ 1\ 0]$ with $b_y = 0$ repeats the contact: the stacked rank stays 1 and the solution set is the whole plane; with $b_y \ne 0$ the two planes are parallel and the system is inconsistent. No velocity satisfies an inconsistent system, and Hou and Mason (2019, Fig. 1, right) call such a system infeasible. The notation $[N; G]$ means $N$ stacked above $G$.

The number of velocity commands from two ranks

The controller adds $n_{av}$ velocity commands $C\vv = \vb_C$. Requirement (Hou 2019, eq. 7): $\mathrm{Sol}(N \,\&\, C) \subseteq \mathrm{Sol}(N \,\&\, G)$.

  1. A set inside another has no more dimensions: $n - \rank\begin{bmatrix} N \\ C \end{bmatrix} \le n - \rank\begin{bmatrix} N \\ G \end{bmatrix}$.
  2. Stacked rows add at most their own number to the rank: $\rank\begin{bmatrix} N \\ C \end{bmatrix} \le \rank N + n_{av}$.
$n_{av} \ge \rank\begin{bmatrix} N \\ G \end{bmatrix} - \rank N$ (Hou 2019, eq. 8).

The two steps combine: step 1 gives $\rank[N; C] \ge \rank[N; G]$, and step 2 gives $\rank[N; C] \le \rank N + n_{av}$, so $\rank N + n_{av} \ge \rank[N; G]$. Step 1 uses rank-nullity: both solution sets are shifted copies of null spaces, of dimension $n$ minus the rank of the stacked matrix, and a subspace inside another cannot have more independent directions. Step 2 holds because the rows of $[N; C]$ are the rows of $N$ together with the $n_{av}$ rows of $C$, so a basis of its row space needs at most $\rank N + n_{av}$ vectors. For the block ($n = 3$, $\rank N = 1$): the goal $v_x = 0.1$ needs one command; the goal $[1\ 0\ 0;\ 0\ 0\ 1]$ and the full goal $G = I$ need two; the goal $v_y = 0$ needs none, because the table already enforces it. Hou and Mason choose exactly the minimum, $n_{av} = \rank[N; G] - \rank N$ (eq. 10), which leaves the remaining directions free for force control.
