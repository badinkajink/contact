From yes/no to how much

  • Force closure says: the grasp can resist any disturbance, if the fingers squeeze hard enough.
  • Real fingers have limited strength. A grasp whose segment barely fits inside the cones needs an enormous squeeze to resist a small nudge.
  • So we want a number: how big a disturbance can the grasp resist, per unit of finger effort?

The recipe has three steps: make the cones finite, build the set of reachable wrenches, and measure how far its boundary is from the origin.

Step 1: make the cones finite (again)

  • Computers like finite lists. Replace each cone by $k$ edge directions $\vd_{ij}$, each with normal part 1 (an inscribed pyramid, Part 2).
  • Each edge gives a primitive wrench $$\vw_{ij} = (\vd_{ij},\ \vp_i \times \vd_{ij}).$$
  • A grasp is now just a cloud of points: $k$ per finger, in $\R^3$ (flat) or $\R^6$ (solid).

In the plane the cone has exactly 2 edges, so nothing is lost. In 3D, the choice of $k$ matters. We'll see how much.

Step 2: force closure = origin inside the hull

  • Tool #7: the convex hull of some points is the smallest convex shape containing them: a rubber band around them in 2D, shrink-wrap in 3D and up.
  • The primitive wrenches positively span everything iff the origin is strictly inside their convex hull.
  • If it isn't, the hull has a face with the origin on the wrong side, and that face's direction is the escape direction from Part 4.

Convex hulls in any dimension: the Qhull library (scipy.spatial.ConvexHull).

Or: force closure as a linear program

Part 4's recipe, in code: full rank, plus a strictly positive squeeze.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

def force_closure(W):
    """W: one primitive wrench per column (3 x m in 2D, 6 x m in 3D)."""
    d, m = W.shape
    if np.linalg.matrix_rank(W) < d:
        return False                     # some wrench direction is unreachable
    # Look for weights lambda_j >= 1 with W @ lambda = 0: a strict squeeze.
    res = linprog(np.zeros(m), A_eq=W, b_eq=np.zeros(d), bounds=[(1, None)] * m)
    return res.status == 0               # feasible means force closure

Why $\ge 1$ and not $> 0$? Any strictly positive solution can be scaled up until every weight is at least 1. The same function, with tests, is in 06_force_closure.py.

Step 3: what can the grasp do with limited effort?

  • Total finger force at most 1 (“$L_1$”): the reachable wrenches are the convex hull of the primitive wrenches.
  • Each finger at most 1 (“$L_\infty$”): every finger contributes its own fan, and the fans add up, giving a bigger set.
  • Either set is called the grasp wrench space (GWS).

C. Ferrari and J. Canny, “Planning optimal grasps”, ICRA 1992.

The Ferrari–Canny quality $\varepsilon$

$\varepsilon$ = radius of the largest ball, centered at the origin, that fits inside the grasp wrench space. Equivalently: the distance from the origin to the nearest face of the hull.

  • Meaning: every disturbance of size up to $\varepsilon$ can be resisted with total finger force at most 1.
  • The nearest face points at the grasp's weakest direction.
  • $\varepsilon > 0 \iff$ force closure. Bigger is better.

A catch: forces and torques have different units

  • A wrench is (newtons, newton-meters). Its “size” adds apples to oranges.
  • Usual fix: divide torques by a length $\lambda$, such as the object's radius: $$\vw = \big(\vf,\ \vtau / \lambda\big).$$
  • Then $\varepsilon$ depends on $\lambda$. Double the object and keep $\lambda$ fixed, and the ranking of grasps can change. It is a modeling choice, not physics.
  • Mirtich & Canny avoid the issue by scoring forces and torques separately (Part 5).

Try it: quality of an off-center pinch


Box of width $w = 2$, torques divided by $\lambda = 1$. Quality falls smoothly to zero exactly where Part 4 said force closure ends: $d = \mu w$.

False positives are the dangerous kind

Part 5's ball: three fingers at latitude $\varphi$, $\mu = 0.5$. True answer: force closure iff $\varphi < 26.6^\circ$. What do 4-sided pyramids say?

pyramid, turned so that…certifies up to
diamond, corner toward the grip plane26.6°
diamond, side toward the grip plane19.5°
box, side toward the grip plane26.6°
box, corner toward the grip plane35.3°
  • The inscribed diamond can reject good grasps (19.5°–26.6°): a false negative, safe but wasteful.
  • The circumscribed box can accept a grasp at 30°, which really slips: a false positive. In planning, that's a dropped object.
  • Even the pyramid's rotation about the normal changes the answer.
For safety checks, use the true cone, or an inscribed pyramid with many sides.

Case study: the pinch filter

Putting Parts 2–6 together on one real test.

Reading the pinch filter with everything we know

For friction coefficient $\mu$, feasibility requires $\cos(\angle(-\nhat_0, \nhat_1)) \ge \cos(2\arctan\mu)$.
  1. For a sphere, it is exactly Nguyen's condition (Part 4): normals through the center make the angle test and the segment test the same.
  2. With hard point contacts in 3D, no two-finger pinch is force closure (Part 5): the thumb–index axis twist is free. For a ball that twist only spins it in place, but it matters when reorienting it. With soft fingertips (MuJoCo condim $\ge 4$) the test is exact.
  3. “$\ge$” admits zero margin. At equality the contact is on the edge of slipping.

A fair one-line description: a necessary condition for antipodal force closure that is exact for spheres under a soft-finger contact model. Add a margin, e.g. use $0.8\mu$, and rank the survivors with $\varepsilon$.

Other ways to score a grasp

  • Task-oriented (Li & Sastry, 1988): only resist the disturbances you expect, such as gravity plus small bumps, not the worst direction.
  • Grasp-map measures: e.g. the smallest singular value of the matrix that maps finger forces to wrenches, or the volume of the grasp wrench space.
  • Position tolerance: how far each finger can slide and keep force closure. Nguyen (1988) constructs independent contact regions for exactly this.
  • Hand-dependent: can the joints actually produce the needed forces?

Survey: M. Roa and R. Suárez, “Grasp quality measures: review and performance”, Autonomous Robots 38, 2015.

What this series skipped

  • Motion: sliding and rolling on purpose (pushing, in-hand manipulation), and the contact solvers that simulate it: 05_contact_solvers.py.
  • Patches and soft materials: pressure distributions, the limit surface, rubbery fingertips.
  • Uncertainty: you never know $\mu$ or the object's shape exactly. Margins are not optional.
  • Curvature: with curved surfaces, fewer frictionless fingers can immobilize an object than the counting argument says (Rimon & Burdick, 1998).

Check yourself

  1. Why does $\varepsilon$ need a length scale $\lambda$?
    answer
    Wrenches mix newtons and newton-meters; a distance in wrench space only makes sense after putting torques in force units.
  2. A circumscribed 4-sided pyramid says “force closure”; an inscribed one says “not”. What do you conclude?
    answer
    Nothing yet: the truth is in between. Use more sides or the true cone.
  3. The LP finds a strictly positive squeeze, but the primitive wrenches have rank 5 in 3D. Force closure?
    answer
    No. Some wrench direction is unreachable, like the twist about a two-finger grasp axis.
