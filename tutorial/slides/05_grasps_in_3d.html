What changes in 3D

  • Forces have 3 numbers. Torques have 3 numbers too: you can twist about any axis, not just “in the page”.
  • So a wrench has 6 numbers, and force closure asks the contact wrench cone to be all of $\R^6$.
  • What doesn't change: the recipe from Part 4.
    Force closure $\iff$ (1) ignoring friction limits, the contacts can make every wrench, and (2) a squeeze exists strictly inside all the cones.
  • This part: why two ordinary fingers are never enough, how fingertip pads fix it, and how three fingers squeeze.

Tool #6: the cross product

The 3D torque of force $\vf$ applied at $\vr$ (from the pivot):

$$\vtau = \vr \times \vf = \begin{pmatrix} r_y f_z - r_z f_y \\ r_z f_x - r_x f_z \\ r_x f_y - r_y f_x \end{pmatrix}$$
  • Its direction is the axis of the twist (right-hand rule: curl your fingers from $\vr$ to $\vf$; your thumb points along $\vtau$).
  • Its length is force × lever arm, as before.
  • The last row is Part 3's 2D torque: a flat world only twists about the $z$ axis.

Wrenches and counting in 3D

  • A contact force $\vf$ at point $\vp$ makes the wrench $\vw = (\vf,\ \vp \times \vf) \in \R^6$.
  • To positively span $\R^6$ we need at least $6 + 1 = 7$ edge wrenches.
  • Frictionless fingers give 1 edge each: at least 7 frictionless fingers. For most shapes 7 is also enough (Markenscoff, Ni & Papadimitriou, 1990).
  • Shapes of revolution (balls, cylinders, bottles) can't be held by frictionless fingers at all: nothing resists spinning about the axis.
  • Frictional fingers give a whole cone of edges each, so far fewer fingers can work. How few?

“Soft finger” is the torsional friction from Part 2: a rubbery pad touching over a small patch. The names come from Murray, Li & Sastry (1994), Ch. 5.

Two hard fingers can't hold anything

  • Draw the line through the two contact points: the grasp axis.
  • Any force applied at a point on a line has zero torque about that line. Both contacts sit on the grasp axis.
  • So no finger forces, however large, can resist a twist about the axis. Pinch a pencil at its two ends and it spins.

In numbers: all the edge wrenches of two hard fingers lie in a 5-dimensional slice of $\R^6$. Checked in 06_force_closure.py.

Soft fingers fix it

  • A fingertip pad resists twisting about its own normal: $\abs{\tau_n} \le \gamma f_n$.
  • In a good pinch the normals lie along the grasp axis, so the pads resist exactly the twist that was missing.
Two soft-finger contacts form a force-closure grasp if and only if the segment joining them lies strictly inside both friction cones.
  • That's Nguyen's condition again, now in 3D. Part 4's box, disk, and sphere results carry over unchanged.
  • Real robot fingertips are soft pads, which is why pinch grasps work in practice. In simulation, use condim="4" or pinches can slowly spin.

Finding antipodal pairs (Chen & Burdick, 1993)

  • Antipodal pair: two surface points whose normals point straight along the line between them, in opposite directions.
  • Idea: a “grasp energy” that grows with the distance between the fingers, like a rubber band pulling them together.
  • Slide either finger along the surface: if the distance doesn't change (to first order), the chord must be perpendicular to the surface there. So critical points of the distance are antipodal pairs.
  • Find them with optimization, on 2D curves and 3D surfaces.

I.-M. Chen and J. W. Burdick, “Finding antipodal point grasps on irregularly shaped objects”, IEEE T-RA 9(4), 1993.

Which pair is best? (Mirtich & Canny, 1994)

  • Rank two-finger grasps of a flat object in two stages:
    1. resist pushes through the grip: best when the normals are exactly opposite;
    2. among those, resist twisting. Friction at the two ends makes a couple of size $\mu f d$, where $d$ is the finger spacing. Longer is better.
  • Winner: the longest chord, the object's diameter. Its endpoints are automatically antipodal.
  • In the language of the last slide: the best pinch is the global maximum of Chen & Burdick's distance function. Two papers, one function.

B. Mirtich and J. Canny, “Easily computable optimum grasps in 2-D and 3-D”, ICRA 1994.

Part 5a

Three hard fingers

Three plain fingertips can hold a 3D object. How they squeeze is the key.

Three balanced forces lie in one plane

Suppose three finger forces squeeze: total force 0 and total torque 0.

  • Take torques about the line through $\vp_1$ and $\vp_2$. Forces 1 and 2 are applied on that line: zero torque about it.
  • So force 3 must have zero torque about it too: its line of action must meet the line $\vp_1\vp_2$ (or run parallel to it).
  • That puts $\vf_3$ in the plane through $\vp_1, \vp_2, \vp_3$. The same goes for $\vf_1$ and $\vf_2$.

All squeezes live in the grip plane.

…and their lines meet at one point

  • Inside the grip plane, let lines 1 and 2 cross at a point $\vq$.
  • Take torques about $\vq$: forces 1 and 2 give zero. So force 3 must too: its line passes through $\vq$.
  • Unless lines 1 and 2 are parallel, in which case all three are parallel.
Three forces in balance are coplanar and concurrent (or parallel). Statics textbooks call this the three-force theorem.

The three-finger test

Three hard fingers, not all on one line, give force closure iff they can squeeze strictly inside their cones. By the last two slides, that squeeze:

  1. lies in the grip plane, so each cone must reach into the grip plane: each normal must be tilted less than $\alpha$ out of it;
  2. has three lines of action through one point $\vq$ (or parallel). So look for a meeting point $\vq$ that every finger can push along.
  • Usual case (fingers around a convex object): $\vq$ inside the finger triangle, every finger pushing toward it.
  • Parallel case: a thumb on one side of a flat plate, two fingers on the other.

Three fingers in a line never work: nothing resists twisting about that line.

Example: three fingers on a ball

  • Fingers 120° apart around a circle at latitude $\varphi$ (angle above the equator). The grip plane is that circle's plane.
  • Each normal points at the ball's center, tilted $\varphi$ out of the grip plane. The cone reaches the plane iff $\varphi < \alpha$.
  • Then the in-plane pushes all point at the circle's center, inside the triangle.
$$\text{force closure} \iff \tan\varphi < \mu$$

$\mu = 0.5$: grab below $26.6^\circ$. Grab higher and the ball squirts out downward.

Why three fingers are enough (Mirtich & Canny)

  • With friction and rounded fingertips, Mirtich & Canny show that any smooth 3D object can be held by 3 fingers, and any flat object by 2. That's one fewer than the earlier bounds (4 and 3).
  • The construction: find three surface points whose normals lie in one plane, 120° apart, meeting at one point.
  • Squeezing along those normals is a squeeze straight down the middle of every cone, so it works for any $\mu > 0$.

Rounded fingertips matter: on a sharp edge or corner of a polyhedron, a round fingertip can push in a whole range of directions, as if the corner were slightly rounded.

The best three-finger grasp (Mirtich & Canny)

  1. Resist pushes in the grip plane: normals 120° apart (an “equilateral grasp”).
  2. Then resist twisting about the plane's normal. With each finger pressing with $f$: $$m_{\max} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,f\,(\mu\, s_o - s_i)$$ $s_o$: side of the outer triangle (lines through the contacts, perpendicular to the normals). $s_i$: side of the inner triangle formed by the normals' lines.

Best: the largest outer triangle; then the normals meet at one point ($s_i = 0$). In 3D: the largest equilateral prism that fits around the object.

Check yourself

  1. Two hard fingers pinch a pencil at its ends. Which disturbance can't they resist?
    answer
    A twist about the pencil's own axis, the line through both contacts.
  2. Three fingers on a ball at latitude 30°, $\mu = 0.5$. Force closure?
    answer
    No: $\tan 30^\circ = 0.577 > 0.5$.
  3. Three fingers whose contact points lie on one straight line. Force closure?
    answer
    Never: all their forces have zero torque about that line.
  4. Three fingers on a ball's equator at 0°, 60°, 120°, $\mu = 0.5$. Force closure?
    answer
    No. The normals meet at the center, outside the finger triangle. It takes $\mu > \tan 30^\circ \approx 0.58$ to bend the pushes enough.

Summary

  • 3D wrenches have 6 numbers; torque is $\vr \times \vf$.
  • Two hard fingers can never close a grasp (twist about the grasp axis). Two soft fingers can: Nguyen's condition again. Antipodal pairs are critical points of the finger distance.
  • Three balanced forces are coplanar and concurrent. So three hard fingers need cones that reach into the grip plane and a common meeting point.
  • Ball example: force closure iff $\tan\varphi < \mu$.
  • Mirtich & Canny: 3 fingers suffice for any smooth solid; the best grasp is equilateral with concurrent normals.
