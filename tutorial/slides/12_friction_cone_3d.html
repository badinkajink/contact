Where we left off

  • A contact force splits into a pressing part $f_n$ and a sideways part $f_t$.
  • Coulomb: $\abs{f_t} \le \mu f_n$, with $f_n \ge 0$.
  • In the $(f_t, f_n)$ plane: a wedge of half-angle $\alpha = \arctan\mu$.
  • Inside = can stick. Edge = sliding. Outside = impossible.

That was a side view. A real floor has two sideways directions.

Two sideways directions

  • At a contact point on a floor we need three unit arrows: the normal $\nhat$ and two tangents $\that_1, \that_2$, all at right angles. This is the contact frame.
  • A contact force is $\vf = f_n\,\nhat + f_{t1}\,\that_1 + f_{t2}\,\that_2$.
  • The sideways part $\vf_t = (f_{t1}, f_{t2})$ is itself a little 2D vector lying flat in the floor. Friction can point anywhere in that plane.

Coulomb's law in 3D

Same rule as before, but “how much sideways” is now the length of $\vf_t$:

$$\norm{\vf_t} = \sqrt{f_{t1}^2 + f_{t2}^2} \;\le\; \mu\, f_n$$
Example. $f_n = 10$ N, $\vf_t = (3, 4)$ N, $\mu = 0.6$. The sideways length is $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$ N. The budget is $0.6 \times 10 = 6$ N. $5 \le 6$, so the contact can stick.
  • Notice: each component alone is small ($3 \le 6$ and $4 \le 6$). What counts is their combined length. Keep that in mind; it matters for pyramids later.

What shape is that? Slice it.

  • Fix the pressing force $f_n$. The allowed sideways forces are all $\vf_t$ with $\norm{\vf_t} \le \mu f_n$: a disk of radius $\mu f_n$.
  • $f_n = 1$: disk of radius $\mu$.
  • $f_n = 2$: radius $2\mu$.
  • $f_n = 3$: radius $3\mu$.
  • Stack all the disks: an ice-cream cone, tip at zero force.

The Coulomb friction cone

$$\K_\mu = \{\, \vf = (f_n, \vf_t) \;:\; f_n \ge 0,\ \norm{\vf_t} \le \mu f_n \,\}$$
  • Half-angle $\alpha = \arctan\mu$ around the normal, as before.
  • Cut it through its axis (the gray plane) and you get Part 1's wedge. Every such cut gives the same wedge.
  • Other names: second-order cone, Lorentz cone. Optimization people say “SOC”.

Why a circle?

  • The circle says the floor doesn't care which way you drag: the budget is the same in every sideways direction.
  • That property is called isotropy (Greek: “same in every direction”).
  • Most everyday surfaces are close to isotropic. Some are not; we'll get to those.

One slice tells the whole story

  • A cone is made of rays from the tip. So the cone is completely described by its slice at $f_n = 1$, the unit slice.
  • A force $(f_n, \vf_t)$ with $f_n > 0$ is in the cone exactly when $\vf_t / f_n$ is in the unit slice.
  • From here on we mostly draw unit slices: flat 2D pictures of 3D cones.

Part 2a

Sliding in 3D: which way does friction point?

In 1D the answer was “backwards”. In 3D there's a neat rule that also explains everything that follows.

Power: the dot product again

  • A force $\vf$ acting on something moving with velocity $\vv$ does work at a rate $$P = \vf \cdot \vv \qquad \text{(power, in watts)}$$
  • $P > 0$: the force speeds it up (adds energy). $P < 0$: the force slows it down (removes energy, turning it into heat).
  • Friction always has $P \le 0$. The question is: of all the forces in the slice, which one does friction pick?
Example. $\vv = (2, 0)$ m/s. The force $(-3, 0)$ N gives $P = -6$ W. The force $(0, 3)$ N gives $P = 0$: sideways forces do no work.

Maximum dissipation

Sliding friction picks the force in the slice that removes energy fastest: the most negative $\vf_t \cdot \vv$.

  • Picture: slide a line perpendicular to $\vv$ backwards across the slice. The last point it touches is the friction force.
  • For a disk that point is straight behind: $$\vf_t = -\mu f_n \frac{\vv}{\norm{\vv}}$$

This is the maximum dissipation principle (Moreau). It gives back Part 1's rule for a circle, and the right answer for every other shape too.

Part 2b

Elliptic cones: when direction matters

Some surfaces grip harder one way than the other.

Anisotropic friction

Anisotropic: different grip in different directions.

  • ice skates and sled runners;
  • grooved surfaces: tire treads, knurled handles, ridged robot fingertips;
  • wood along vs. across the grain, brushed metal, corduroy.

Even stranger: fish-scale ski bases and snake scales grip backward but glide forward. No symmetric shape can model that; we won't try.

The elliptic cone

Give each tangent direction its own coefficient:

$$\left(\frac{f_{t1}}{\mu_1}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{f_{t2}}{\mu_2}\right)^2 \le f_n^2$$
  • The unit slice is an ellipse with half-widths $\mu_1$ and $\mu_2$.
  • $\mu_1 = \mu_2$ gives back the circle.
  • This is exactly MuJoCo's cone="elliptic". The two tangential coefficients may differ; MuJoCo's docs suggest skating as a use.

An ellipse is a stretched circle

  • Rescale the sideways axes: $g_1 = f_{t1}/\mu_1$ and $g_2 = f_{t2}/\mu_2$. The rule becomes $g_1^2 + g_2^2 \le f_n^2$: an ordinary circular cone with $\mu = 1$.
  • So every fact about the circular cone carries over to the elliptic one, after a stretch.

Sliding on an elliptic surface

Same rule: the last point touched by a line perpendicular to $\vv$. On an ellipse that point is generally not straight behind:

$$\vf_t = -f_n\,\frac{(\mu_1^2 v_1,\ \mu_2^2 v_2)}{\sqrt{\mu_1^2 v_1^2 + \mu_2^2 v_2^2}}$$
Example. $\mu_1 = 0.9$ (across the blade), $\mu_2 = 0.3$ (along it), sliding at 45°. Friction points only 6.3° off the $-\that_1$ axis, 39° away from $-\vv$. It mostly kills the sideways motion, so the skate keeps gliding along its blade.

Only the circle always pushes straight back.

Part 2c

Pyramids: cones with flat faces

Many simulators and planners replace the round cone with a pyramid. What does that cost?

Why would anyone approximate a circle?

  • The circle rule has squares in it: $f_{t1}^2 + f_{t2}^2 \le \mu^2 f_n^2$. It is nonlinear.
  • Many classic solvers only handle linear inequalities (things like $a x + b y \le c$): linear programs (LP) and linear complementarity problems (LCP), solved by Lemke's method or projected Gauss–Seidel. Engines like ODE and Bullet are built on them.
  • Replace the round cone by a pyramid with flat faces, and every constraint becomes linear. This is the classic LCP contact model (Stewart & Trinkle, 1996).
  • Modern solvers handle the round cone directly (MuJoCo's elliptic mode, Drake, conic optimizers). But pyramids are everywhere: MuJoCo's default is still pyramidal.

Tool: linear inequalities are flat walls

  • One linear inequality, like $x + 2y \le 2$, keeps one side of a straight line: a half-plane.
  • Several at once keep the region inside all the walls: a polygon.
  • On a cone's unit slice, $k$ walls give a $k$-sided polygon. Back in 3D, that's a pyramid with $k$ flat faces.

Two ways to build a pyramid

  • Inside out: pick $k$ edge directions $\vd_j$ on the cone. Allowed forces are the non-negative mixes $$\vf = \beta_1 \vd_1 + \dots + \beta_k \vd_k,\quad \beta_j \ge 0.$$ The corners sit on the circle: inscribed, a bit too small.
  • Outside in: put $k$ flat walls touching the cone. The sides touch the circle: circumscribed, a bit too big.

The two famous four-sided pyramids

Diamond (inscribed). Edges $\nhat \pm \mu\that_1$, $\nhat \pm \mu\that_2$, so $\ \abs{f_{t1}} + \abs{f_{t2}} \le \mu f_n$.
Used by the 4-direction LCP and by MuJoCo's pyramidal cones.

Box (circumscribed). Clamp each direction on its own: $\ \abs{f_{t1}} \le \mu f_n$ and $\abs{f_{t2}} \le \mu f_n$.
ODE's “friction pyramid”; the usual trick in per-constraint solvers.

Watch out: both get called “the friction pyramid”. Always check which one a paper or engine means.

Cost 1: the budget depends on direction

Along the axes, all three agree. At 45° they don't:

modelmax friction at 45°
circle (true)$1.00\,\mu f_n$
diamond$0.71\,\mu f_n$
box$1.41\,\mu f_n$
Example. Push the 10 kg box ($\mu = 0.5$) sideways at 45° to the $\that_1$ axis. True breakaway force: 49.1 N. The diamond model says 34.7 N; the box model says 69.4 N.

A pyramid turns an isotropic floor into an anisotropic one.

Cost 2: sliding friction points the wrong way

  • Maximum dissipation on a polygon: the last point touched is (almost always) a corner.
  • Diamond, sliding at 30°: friction points straight along $-\that_1$, a full 30° away from $-\vv$.
  • Friction snaps to a handful of directions. It only points exactly backwards when you slide along a corner direction or exactly across a side.

Cost 3: sliding paths bend

Pucks launched at 2 m/s in 16 directions on a floor with $\mu = 0.3$, until friction stops them. Top view, 1 m grid.

True Coulomb friction: straight spokes. Pyramids: spokes that curve toward a few favorite directions.

Why the paths bend, in slow motion

  1. Sliding at 30°, friction is the corner $(-\mu m g, 0)$. Only $v_1$ shrinks; $v_2$ keeps going. The direction swings toward 45°.
  2. At 45° the last-touch line lies along a whole side. Friction sits in the middle of it, exactly opposite $\vv$, with size $\mu m g/\sqrt2$.
  3. The puck then slides straight along the diagonal until it stops.
Paths are pulled toward the directions across the sides: the diagonals for the diamond, the axes for the box.

Do more sides fix it?

  • For hard (rigid) contact, yes, gradually: with $k = 8$ the budget error is below 8% and the bending is milder. But paths still bend toward $k$ favorite directions.
  • It costs: a $k$-sided pyramid has $k$ unknowns per contact instead of 3.
  • MuJoCo uses $2(\text{condim} - 1)$ edges, 4 for an ordinary frictional contact. Its docs say more edges would be pointless: the solver would get slower than the elliptic one.
  • Subtle: in soft-contact simulators like MuJoCo, pyramidal and elliptic models give slightly different motion even when they describe the same set of forces, because the softness is measured differently. MuJoCo's docs call the elliptic model “more principled and more consistent with physical intuition”.

Part 2d

Twisting: soft fingers

A point can't resist a twist. A fingertip pad can.

Torsional friction

  • A rubbery fingertip touches over a small patch, not a point.
  • Twisting the object about the normal drags the patch in a circle; friction over the patch resists with a torque: $\abs{\tau_n} \le \gamma\, f_n$.
  • $\gamma$ has units of length, roughly the patch size.
  • Together: a cone in four numbers, $$\frac{f_{t1}^2}{\mu^2} + \frac{f_{t2}^2}{\mu^2} + \frac{\tau_n^2}{\gamma^2} \le f_n^2.$$

MuJoCo: condim="4" adds exactly this, and its docs say it “can substantially improve the stability of simulated grasping”. The default torsional coefficient is 0.005 m. Part 5 shows why grasps need it.

Extra credit: the dual cone

The dual cone collects every direction that makes an angle of at most 90° with every force in the cone:

$$\K^* = \{\, \vv : \vv \cdot \vf \ge 0 \text{ for all } \vf \in \K \,\}$$
  • For the Coulomb cone, $\K_\mu^* = \K_{1/\mu}$. Narrow cone, wide dual; $\mu = 1$ is its own dual.
  • Why care: in the convex contact models inside MuJoCo and Drake, contact velocities live in the dual cone. See 05_contact_solvers.py.

Check yourself

  1. $f_n = 10$ N, $\vf_t = (5, 5)$ N, $\mu = 0.6$. Inside the Coulomb cone?
    answer
    No: $\norm{\vf_t} = 7.07 > 6$.
  2. Same force. Does the diamond pyramid allow it? The box?
    answer
    Diamond: $5 + 5 = 10 > 6$, no. Box: $5 \le 6$ and $5 \le 6$, yes. The box wrongly allows a force the real floor can't supply.
  3. How many sides must an inscribed pyramid have for less than 1% error?
    answer
    $1 - \cos(\pi/k) < 0.01$ first holds at $k = 23$ (0.93%). With $k = 22$ it's 1.02%.
  4. On the skate surface ($\mu_1 = 0.9$, $\mu_2 = 0.3$), a puck slides along $\that_2$ exactly. Which way does friction point?
    answer
    Straight back, $-\that_2$, with size $0.3 f_n$. Along a principal axis the ellipse's last-touch point is directly behind.

Summary

  • In 3D, Coulomb's rule bounds the length of the sideways force: a round cone with unit slice a disk of radius $\mu$.
  • Sliding friction is the slice's last-touch point against the velocity (maximum dissipation). For a disk, straight back.
  • Elliptic cones model direction-dependent grip; they are stretched circles, and their friction is not straight back.
  • Pyramids make the math linear but make the floor anisotropic: wrong budgets (up to 29% short or 41% over for $k = 4$), wrong friction directions, bent sliding paths.
  • Fingertip pads add torsional friction: a fourth number in the cone.
