From floors to fingers

  • A fingertip is just a contact: it pushes, never pulls, and its friction is limited by a cone around its normal.
  • A grasp is a set of fingertip contacts on one object.
  • The difference from a floor: fingers can push from several sides.

In this part everything is flat (2D): think of pinching a coin lying on a table, seen from above. Part 5 goes to 3D.

What does “holding” mean?

  • As the hand moves, gravity points in different directions relative to the object.
  • Things bump into it. The hand accelerates it.
  • We want a grasp that can cancel any disturbance: whatever force and torque the world applies, the fingers can pick forces inside their cones that cancel it.
Force closure: for every disturbance wrench, the fingers can find forces inside their friction cones whose total wrench cancels it.

For now, fingers may squeeze as hard as needed. Part 6 asks how hard.

Force closure, in symbols

Contacts at points $\vp_1, \dots, \vp_k$ with friction cones $\K_1, \dots, \K_k$. Each contact force $\vf_i$ makes the wrench $(\vf_i,\ \vp_i \times \vf_i)$, where in 2D $\vp \times \vf = p_x f_y - p_y f_x$.

$$\text{force closure} \iff \Big\{ \sum_{i} (\vf_i,\ \vp_i \times \vf_i) \;:\; \vf_i \in \K_i \Big\} = \text{all of } \R^3$$
  • In Part 3's words: the contact wrench cone is everything.
  • $\R^3$ just means “all lists of 3 numbers”: every $(f_x, f_y, \tau)$.

Warm-up in 1D: a bead on a wire

  • One finger pushing right can resist being pushed left, and nothing else.
  • Mixes of $\{+1\}$ with non-negative weights: only $[0, \infty)$, half the line.
  • Add a finger pushing left: mixes of $\{+1, -1\}$ give every number.

The whole story of force closure is already here: contacts only push, so you need them to push from enough sides.

Tool #5: positive combinations

  • A positive combination of vectors $\vd_1, \dots, \vd_m$ is $\beta_1\vd_1 + \dots + \beta_m\vd_m$ with every $\beta_j \ge 0$.
  • The vectors positively span a space if every vector in it is such a combination.
  • In the plane you need at least 3 vectors, spread out so they don't all fit in one half-plane.
  • In general: in $d$ dimensions you need at least $d + 1$.

The escape-direction test

The vectors fail to positively span exactly when there is a direction $\vu$ making an angle of $90^\circ$ or more with all of them:

$$\vu \cdot \vd_j \le 0 \quad \text{for every } j.$$
  • Physically: push the object along $\vu$ and no combination of contact forces pushes back. The object escapes along $\vu$.
  • Equivalently: the origin is not strictly inside the convex hull of the vectors.

Mathematicians call this Farkas' lemma. The failure certificate is itself physical: it tells you how the object gets away.

Counting edges

  • Planar wrenches have 3 numbers, so we need at least 4 edge wrenches that positively span $\R^3$.
  • Frictionless contact: its cone is a single ray, 1 edge. So you need at least 4 frictionless fingers to hold a flat object.
  • Frictional contact: its cone has 2 edges. So 2 fingers might be enough.
Watch out: counting is necessary, not sufficient. Four edges pointing badly still fail. Where the fingers go matters.

Frictionless fingers: the bar of soap

  • Two wet fingers on a bar of soap can only push along their normals.
  • A tiny sideways nudge: nothing resists it. A slight misalignment: the two pushes make a twist, and the soap spins and shoots out.
  • With no friction, a disk can never be held, by any number of fingers: every normal passes through the center, so nothing can resist spinning.

Two fingers with friction

  • Each finger's cone has two edges: 4 edge wrenches in total, just enough to count.
  • Left: fingers directly across from each other. Holds, whatever the disturbance.
  • Right: same box, same $\mu$, fingers offset. A twist makes it spin out.

What exactly separates the two?

Squeezing: internal forces

  • Push the two fingers toward each other along the line joining them, equally hard.
  • Net force: zero. Net torque: zero (both forces lie on the same line).
  • The object feels nothing overall, but both contacts press harder, so both friction budgets grow.
  • Force combinations with zero net wrench are called internal forces.

For two fingers this is the only kind: zero net force needs equal and opposite forces, and zero net torque then forces them onto the line through both contacts.

Nguyen's theorem (1988)

Two frictional point contacts on a planar object form a force-closure grasp if and only if the segment joining the contact points lies strictly inside both friction cones (pointing into the object at each end).
  • Left: the segment is inside both cones. Force closure.
  • Right: the segment leaves the cones. Not force closure.

Such a grasp is often called antipodal. V.-D. Nguyen, “Constructing force-closure grasps”, IJRR 7(3), 1988.

Why it works: squeeze harder

  1. Given a disturbance, first find finger forces that cancel it ignoring the cones. Always possible: 4 unknowns, 3 equations.
  2. Now add a squeeze $s\,\vd$ at finger 1 and $-s\,\vd$ at finger 2. The total wrench doesn't change.
  3. For big $s$, each finger's force points almost along the segment, which is strictly inside its cone. So eventually both forces are inside.

Why it's needed

Suppose the grasp is force closure. Then:

  1. Pick any forces $\vf^{0}$ strictly inside the cones (say, straight along the normals). They make some wrench $\vw_0$.
  2. Force closure lets us cancel $\vw_0$ with allowed forces $\vf'$.
  3. Add them: $\vf^{0} + \vf'$ is still strictly inside the cones (inside + allowed = inside), and its total wrench is $\vw_0 - \vw_0 = 0$.
  4. So a squeeze strictly inside both cones exists. For two fingers every squeeze lies along the segment. So the segment is strictly inside both cones.

The general recipe (any number of fingers)

A grasp is force closure if and only if
  1. ignoring friction limits, the contacts can make every wrench, and
  2. there is a squeeze (a zero-total-wrench choice of forces) strictly inside all the cones.
  • The two arguments on the last two slides prove this in general, not just for two fingers.
  • Nguyen's theorem is this recipe plus one geometric fact: two fingers can only squeeze along the line between them.
  • Part 5 reuses the recipe in 3D, where three fingers squeeze in more interesting ways.

See Murray, Li & Sastry, A Mathematical Introduction to Robotic Manipulation (1994), Ch. 5.

Part 4a

Examples

Three shapes, three formulas you can do in your head.

Example 1: pinching a box off-center

  • Box width $w$. Fingers on opposite faces, at heights differing by $d$.
  • The normals are exactly opposite, but the segment tilts by $\beta$ with $\tan\beta = d / w$.
  • Force closure $\iff \beta < \alpha \iff$ $$d < \mu\, w.$$
Numbers. $w = 6$ cm. With $\mu = 0.5$ the fingers may be up to 3 cm apart in height; with $\mu = 0.2$, only 1.2 cm.

Example 2: a disk

  • On a disk every inward normal points at the center.
  • Put the fingers $\delta$ of arc away from exactly opposite. The triangle formed by the center and the two contacts is isosceles, so the segment makes the same angle $\delta/2$ with both normals.
  • Force closure $\iff \delta/2 < \alpha \iff \delta < 2\arctan\mu$.
Numbers. $\mu = 0.4$: up to $43.6^\circ$ of misplacement. $\mu = 0.1$: only $11.4^\circ$. Slippery objects need precise fingers.

Example 3: the watermelon seed

  • A wedge whose faces meet at angle $2\beta$, squeezed on its faces at equal heights.
  • The segment between the fingers makes angle $\beta$ with each face's normal.
  • Force closure $\iff \beta < \alpha$.
  • If the wedge is too pointy ($\beta > \alpha$), squeezing harder only pushes it harder out toward its wide end. That's the squirting watermelon seed.

Part 4b

A tempting shortcut, and when it is exact

Grasp filters often check only the two normals. What does that buy you?

A normal-angle test from a real grasp filter

Antipodal grasp feasibility: contact normals satisfy a friction-cone force-closure test. For friction coefficient $\mu$, feasibility requires $\cos(\angle(-\nhat_0, \nhat_1)) \ge \cos(2\arctan\mu)$.
  • $\nhat_0, \nhat_1$ are the outward surface normals at the two contacts. For a perfect pinch they are exactly opposite, so $\angle(-\nhat_0, \nhat_1) = 0$.
  • The test says: the normals may be at most $2\alpha$ away from exactly opposite.
  • It never looks at where the contacts are. Nguyen's condition does.

(From a thumb–index pinch filter for grasping spheres.)

The shortcut is necessary

  • Suppose Nguyen's condition holds. Then the segment direction $\vd$ is within $\alpha$ of the inward normal $-\nhat_0$ at one end,
  • and $-\vd$ is within $\alpha$ of $-\nhat_1$ at the other, i.e. $\vd$ is within $\alpha$ of $\nhat_1$.
  • Going from $-\nhat_0$ to $\vd$ to $\nhat_1$, the angles add up to at most $2\alpha$. So $$\angle(-\nhat_0, \nhat_1) \le 2\alpha.$$

Every force-closure pinch passes the test. But…

…it is not sufficient

  • The off-center box pinch: normals exactly opposite, so the test passes for any $\mu$.
  • But with $d > \mu w$ the segment leaves both cones. Not force closure: squeezing makes the box spin between the fingers.
  • The shortcut can't tell these apart because it ignores contact positions.

…but on a sphere it is exact

  • On a circle or sphere every normal passes through the center.
  • Center + two contacts: an isosceles triangle. The segment makes the same angle $\beta$ with both normals, and $\angle(-\nhat_0, \nhat_1) = 2\beta$.
  • So “$\angle \le 2\alpha$” is the same as “$\beta \le \alpha$”: Nguyen's condition exactly.
For spheres the shortcut is the force-closure test, except exactly at equality, where the grasp is on the verge of slipping.

Making the shortcut safe

  • Keep a margin. Use $\mu_{\text{design}} < \mu$ (say $0.7\mu$–$0.8\mu$), or require $\angle(-\nhat_0, \nhat_1) \le 2\alpha - \text{margin}$. Equality means zero tolerance.
  • Non-spherical objects: check Nguyen's condition with the actual contact points, not just the normals.
  • In 3D, two point contacts can't resist twisting about the finger-to-finger line. True force closure needs soft fingertips (torsional friction). That's Part 5.

For a sphere, that last twist only spins the ball in place, which rarely matters for holding it. But it does matter for calling the grasp “force closure”.

Check yourself

  1. Box, $w = 4$ cm, $\mu = 0.3$, fingers offset by 1.5 cm. Force closure?
    answer
    No: needs $d < \mu w = 1.2$ cm.
  2. Disk, $\mu = 1$. How far from exactly opposite can the fingers be?
    answer
    Up to $2\arctan 1 = 90^\circ$ of arc.
  3. Two frictionless fingers exactly opposite on a disk. Force closure?
    answer
    No. Nothing resists sideways pushes or spinning.
  4. On a sphere, can the normal-angle test pass while Nguyen's condition fails?
    answer
    Only at exact equality ($\beta = \alpha$), where the strict inequality fails.

Summary

  • Force closure: the contacts can cancel any disturbance wrench.
  • Contacts only push, so what matters is positive spanning: $d+1$ edges at least, and no escape direction.
  • General recipe: full rank + a squeeze strictly inside all cones.
  • Two fingers in the plane (Nguyen): the segment between them must lie strictly inside both cones. Box: $d < \mu w$. Disk: misplacement $< 2\arctan\mu$. Wedge: half-angle $< \arctan\mu$.
  • The normal-angle test is necessary; for spheres it's also sufficient.
