Real contact is a patch

  • A box touches the floor over its whole bottom face, with pressure spread out in some unknown way.
  • Model it with a few points: the two bottom corners in a side view, the four corners in 3D.
  • Why is that enough? Any spread of pushing (never pulling) pressure over a flat face has the same total push and the same turning effect as some pushing forces at its corners.

So corner contacts lose nothing for statics, as long as the face is flat.

Tool #3: torque, the turning effect of a force

  • Push a door near the hinge: it barely moves. Push at the handle: it swings.
  • Torque measures how hard a force twists something around a pivot: $$\tau = (\text{force}) \times (\text{lever arm})$$
  • The lever arm is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the force's line.
  • Units: newton-meters (N·m).

Torque from components

With $\vr$ the arrow from the pivot to where the force acts:

$$\tau = r_x f_y - r_y f_x$$
  • Positive = counterclockwise, negative = clockwise.
  • Example: $\vf = (0, -10)$ N at $\vr = (2, 0)$ m gives $\tau = 2 \times (-10) - 0 = -20$ N·m, a clockwise twist.
  • A force whose line passes through the pivot has zero torque about it. We'll use this trick a lot.

Equilibrium, upgraded

For something that can turn, sitting still needs two things:

  1. the forces add up to zero, and
  2. the torques add up to zero.

In a flat (2D) world that's three equations: left-right, up-down, and spin.

If the forces already add to zero, the total torque is the same about every pivot, so you may pick whichever pivot makes the algebra easiest.

A box on the floor, side view

  • Unknowns: $N_A, N_B$ (pressing) and $F_A, F_B$ (friction) at corners $A$ and $B$, width $w$ apart.
  • Torque about $A$: $N_B\,w - mg\,\tfrac{w}{2} = 0$, so $N_B = \tfrac12 mg$. Then $N_A = \tfrac12 mg$ too.
  • Left-right: $F_A + F_B = 0$. Any equal-and-opposite pair works: the corners could be quietly squeezing the floor between them.
Four unknowns, three equations. The friction split is statically indeterminate: rigid-body statics cannot decide it.

Now push it

Push with $P$ at height $h$. Torques about the front corner $B$ (the frictions pass through it, so they drop out):

$$N_A = \tfrac12 mg - \frac{P h}{w}, \qquad N_B = \tfrac12 mg + \frac{P h}{w}$$
  • Pushing shifts weight onto the front corner.
  • Up-down still gives $N_A + N_B = mg$; left-right gives $F_A + F_B = P$.

Two ways to fail: slide or tip

  • Slide when the push beats the total friction budget: $P > \mu (N_A + N_B) = \mu\,mg$.
  • Tip when the back corner lifts, $N_A = 0$: $\ P = mg\,\dfrac{w}{2h}$.
  • Whichever limit is smaller happens first. It tips first when $h > \dfrac{w}{2\mu}$.
Worked example. A tall cabinet, $w = 0.4$ m, $\mu = 0.5$, so the threshold is $w/(2\mu) = 0.4$ m.
  • Push at $h = 1.0$ m: tips at $0.2\,mg$, slides at $0.5\,mg$. It tips.
  • Push at $h = 0.3$ m: tips at $0.67\,mg$, slides at $0.5\,mg$. It slides.
Moral: to slide furniture, push low.

Where does the floor push? The center of pressure

  • Replace $N_A$ and $N_B$ by one force $N_A + N_B$ acting at the center of pressure $$x_{\text{cop}} = \frac{N_A x_A + N_B x_B}{N_A + N_B}.$$
  • It's a weighted average of the corners, so it always sits between them.
  • Pushing harder slides it toward the front corner. Tipping starts when it reaches the edge.

The support polygon

  • In 3D the center of pressure must lie inside the convex hull of the contact points: the smallest convex region containing them (a rubber band stretched around them).
  • This region is the support polygon.
  • A standing robot is balanced (statically) only if the vertical line through its center of mass passes through its support polygon.
  • Walking robots use a moving version of this idea, the zero-moment point (ZMP).

Part 3a

Four corners: more unknowns than equations

Why four-legged tables wobble and three-legged stools never do.

Counting equations in 3D

  • A box resting on four corners. Unknowns: $N_1, N_2, N_3, N_4$.
  • Equations:
    1. up-down forces: $N_1 + N_2 + N_3 + N_4 = mg$
    2. no tipping left-right (torque about one horizontal axis)
    3. no tipping forward-back (torque about the other)
  • Four unknowns, three equations: infinitely many answers. Every one of them is a perfectly good rigid-body solution.

Engineers call this statically indeterminate (or hyperstatic). It is the normal situation in grasping, walking, and stacking.

Try it: the wobbly table

Weight in the middle. Every $s$ from $-mg/4$ to $mg/4$ satisfies all three equations:

$$\mathbf{N} = \tfrac{mg}{4}(1,1,1,1) + s\,(1,-1,1,-1)$$

Diagonal pairs trade load. At the ends, two legs carry everything and the table rocks on them.

Three legs never wobble

  • Three legs: three unknowns, three equations, exactly one answer (as long as the legs aren't in a line).
  • Three points always touch an uneven floor. A fourth leg has to be exactly the right length, or it hangs in the air.
  • That's why stools, camera tripods, and surveying tripods have three legs.
Which of the infinitely many four-leg answers really happens depends on tiny differences in leg length and how the floor and legs squish. Rigid statics can't see either.

What do simulators do about it?

  • A simulator must report some contact forces, so it has to break the tie.
  • Hard-contact solvers (LCP, projected Gauss–Seidel): they return whichever valid answer the iterations happen to land on. The split can depend on contact order and on the previous time step, and can jump around.
  • Soft / regularized solvers (MuJoCo, Drake): a little built-in squishiness makes the answer unique, typically the most even, smallest forces.
Lesson: when contacts are redundant, trust the totals (net force, net torque, center of pressure), not the individual contact forces.

See the tutorial's discussion of PGS and “internal forces”, and 05_contact_solvers.py.

Sliding a four-corner box: good news

  • If the box slides without turning, all four corners have the same velocity $\vv$.
  • Corner $i$ gets friction $-\mu N_i\,\hat{\vv}$. Adding them up: $$\textstyle\sum_i -\mu N_i \hat{\vv} = -\mu\,(\sum_i N_i)\,\hat{\vv} = -\mu\,mg\,\hat{\vv}$$
  • The unknown split doesn't matter! The box decelerates at $\mu g$, like a single puck.
  • With pyramids, it inherits the puck's bent paths too, four times over.

…but turning is different

  • If the box turns while it slides, each corner moves differently, so the frictions point different ways.
  • Now the total friction force and torque do depend on how the weight is split. Rigid statics can't tell you that split.
  • Robot pushing handles this with the limit surface (Goyal, Ruina & Papadopoulos, 1991), assuming a pressure distribution. See Mason, Mechanics of Robotic Manipulation (2001).

Part 3b

Wrenches: forces and torques together

One vector that holds everything a contact can do to an object.

Tool #4: the wrench

A wrench bundles a force and the torque it makes about a chosen reference point:

$$\vw = (f_x,\ f_y,\ \tau) \qquad\text{with}\qquad \tau = p_x f_y - p_y f_x$$
  • A contact force $\vf$ at point $\vp$ produces the wrench $(\vf,\ \tau)$ on the object.
  • To combine several contacts, add their wrenches.
  • Equilibrium (both conditions at once) is just: total wrench = 0.
  • In 2D a wrench has 3 numbers. In 3D it will have 6 (Part 5).

The name comes from the tool: a wrench pushes and twists at the same time.

Every contact has a wrench menu

  • A contact's force must lie in its cone: all non-negative mixes of the cone's two edges $\vd_1, \vd_2$.
  • So its wrench is a non-negative mix of two edge wrenches: $\vw_j = (\vd_j,\ \tau(\vd_j))$.
  • Several contacts together can make any non-negative mix of all their edge wrenches: $$\text{CWC} = \{\, \beta_1 \vw_1 + \beta_2 \vw_2 + \dots,\ \ \beta_j \ge 0 \,\}$$
  • This set is the contact wrench cone: every wrench the contacts, together, are able to apply.

The floor's wrench menu

Corners at $x = \mp w/2$, torque about the bottom middle. The four edge wrenches are $$(\pm\mu,\ 1,\ \pm w/2).$$

Their non-negative mixes are exactly the wrenches with

$$f_y \ge 0,\qquad \abs{f_x} \le \mu f_y,\qquad \abs{\tau} \le \tfrac{w}{2} f_y$$
  • friction walls: how much sideways;
  • tipping walls: how far off-center it can push.

Slide-or-tip in one line

  • What must the floor supply? Minus everything else. The push $(P, 0)$ at $(-w/2, h)$ makes the wrench $(P, 0, -hP)$; gravity makes $(0, -mg, 0)$. So the floor must supply $$\vw_{\text{needed}} = (-P,\ mg,\ hP).$$
  • Is it on the menu?
    • friction wall: $P \le \mu\, mg$  → otherwise it slides;
    • tipping wall: $hP \le \tfrac{w}{2}\, mg$  → otherwise it tips.
Key idea. Statics with any number of contacts is one question: is the needed wrench inside the contact wrench cone?

Why this sets up grasping

  • The floor's menu only has $f_y \ge 0$. It can't stop you lifting the box, and it can't hold the box upside down.
  • Fingers can touch from several sides. Then the menu can include every direction.
  • When the contact wrench cone is everything, the contacts can resist any disturbance. That property is called force closure. It's Part 4.

Check yourself

  1. A bookshelf, $w = 0.3$ m, pushed at $h = 1.5$ m, $\mu = 0.4$. Slide or tip?
    answer
    Tip: it tips at $0.3/(2 \times 1.5) = 0.1\,mg$, long before sliding at $0.4\,mg$.
  2. Below what height must you push to slide it instead?
    answer
    $h < w/(2\mu) = 0.3/0.8 = 0.375$ m.
  3. A box slides straight on four corners. Does the unknown load split change how far it slides?
    answer
    No. Total friction is $\mu\,mg$ whatever the split, as long as it doesn't turn.
  4. Why can a simulator report different corner forces for the same resting box on two runs?
    answer
    The problem is statically indeterminate: many splits are valid, and a hard-contact solver may land on any of them.

Summary

  • Torque $\tau = r_x f_y - r_y f_x$. Equilibrium needs zero net force and zero net torque.
  • A pushed box shifts weight to its front corner. It slides if $P > \mu mg$, and tips if $P > mg\,w/(2h)$.
  • The floor pushes at a center of pressure, always inside the support polygon.
  • More contacts than equations: the force split is undetermined. Trust totals.
  • A wrench is (force, torque). Every contact set has a contact wrench cone; statics asks if the needed wrench is inside it.
