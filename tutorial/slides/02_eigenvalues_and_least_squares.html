Part 1

Projections and orthonormal bases

The closest point of a line or a plane to a given vector, perpendicular errors, and bases in which coordinates are dot products.

Projection of a vector onto a line

Recap (deck 01): $\va\cdot\vb = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2$, $\norm{\va}^2 = \va\cdot\va$, and $\va \perp \vb$ exactly when $\va\cdot\vb = 0$.
  • The projection of $\vb$ onto the line through $\vzero$ and $\va$ is the point $\vp = \hat x\,\va$ of the line closest to $\vb$.
  • $\hat x = \dfrac{\va\cdot\vb}{\va\cdot\va}$, and the error $\ve = \vb - \vp$ is perpendicular to $\va$.
  • $\va = (2, 1)$, $\vb = (1, 3)$: $\hat x = 5/5 = 1$, $\vp = (2, 1)$, $\ve = (-1, 2)$, $\ve\cdot\va = -2 + 2 = 0$. Distance $\norm{\ve} = \sqrt 5 = 2.236$.
Projection answers the question “which point of this line is closest to $\vb$?”. The answer is the foot of the perpendicular from $\vb$: the error $\ve$ meets the line at a right angle, which the figure marks. Why the perpendicular foot is closest: take any other point $\vq$ of the line. Then $\vb - \vq = \ve + (\vp - \vq)$, where $\vp - \vq$ runs along the line and $\ve$ is perpendicular to it, so by Pythagoras $\norm{\vb - \vq}^2 = \norm{\ve}^2 + \norm{\vp - \vq}^2 \ge \norm{\ve}^2$. The number $\hat x$ is the signed shadow length of deck 01 divided by $\norm{\va}$; it is negative when $\va$ and $\vb$ make an angle above $90^\circ$. Projection returns throughout the deck: onto planes (slide projection-subspace), in Gram-Schmidt, and in least squares, where the tex (line 1250) describes $A\vz^\star$ as the orthogonal projection of $\vb$ onto the column space. The handles snap to multiples of 0.25; the readout comes from EigenLS.projectLine, the twin of d02_projection_onto_line.

Derivation of the projection formula

  1. The point of the line: $\vp = \hat x\,\va$ for some number $\hat x$.
  2. Its error: $\ve = \vb - \hat x\,\va$.
  3. Closest means perpendicular: $\va\cdot(\vb - \hat x\,\va) = 0$.
  4. Expand and solve: $\va\cdot\vb - \hat x\,(\va\cdot\va) = 0$, so $\hat x = \dfrac{\va\cdot\vb}{\va\cdot\va}$.
$\vp = \dfrac{\va\va^\T}{\va^\T\va}\,\vb = P\vb$. For $\va = (2, 1)$: $P = \tfrac15\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.8 & 0.4 \\ 0.4 & 0.2 \end{bmatrix}$, with $P^2 = P$ and $P^\T = P$.

Check: $P(1, 3) = (0.8 + 1.2,\ 0.4 + 0.6) = (2, 1)$.

Step 3 uses the Pythagoras argument of the previous slide: among all points of the line, the one whose error is perpendicular to the line is the closest. Step 4 uses the dot-product rules $\va\cdot(\vb - \hat x\va) = \va\cdot\vb - \hat x\,\va\cdot\va$ and needs $\va \ne \vzero$. The projection matrix comes from rewriting $\hat x\,\va$ with the dot product as a matrix product: $\hat x\,\va = \va\,\hat x = \va\,\dfrac{\va^\T\vb}{\va^\T\va} = \dfrac{\va\va^\T}{\va^\T\va}\vb$. Here $\va\va^\T$ is a column times a row, a $2\times 2$ matrix of rank one. $P^2 = P$ because $P^2 = \va(\va^\T\va)\va^\T/(\va^\T\va)^2 = \va\va^\T/(\va^\T\va)$: projecting a point that is already on the line leaves it where it is. $P^\T = P$ because $(\va\va^\T)^\T = \va\va^\T$. The two properties together characterize orthogonal projections. The matrix $P$ has eigenvalues 1 (along $\va$) and 0 (along the perpendicular direction), which Part 2 explains.

Coordinates in an orthonormal basis

  • $\vq_1, \dots, \vq_k$ are orthonormal when $\vq_i\cdot\vq_j = 0$ for $i \ne j$ and $\norm{\vq_i} = 1$.
  • Coordinates need no linear solve: in $\vx = c_1\vq_1 + c_2\vq_2$, $c_i = \vq_i\cdot\vx$.
  • Dot both sides with $\vq_1$: $\vq_1\cdot\vx = c_1(\vq_1\cdot\vq_1) + c_2(\vq_1\cdot\vq_2) = c_1$.
  • $\vq_1 = (0.6, 0.8)$, $\vq_2 = (-0.8, 0.6)$, $\vx = (2, 1)$: $c_1 = 1.2 + 0.8 = 2$, $c_2 = -1.6 + 0.6 = -1$.
  • As columns of $Q = [\vq_1\ \vq_2]$: $Q^\T Q = I$, so $Q^{-1} = Q^\T$ and $\vc = Q^\T\vx$.
In a general basis the coordinates of $\vx$ solve a linear system (deck 01, basis-and-coordinates). In an orthonormal basis the system decouples: entry $(i, j)$ of $Q^\T Q$ is $\vq_i\cdot\vq_j$, which is 1 on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere, so $Q^\T Q = I$ and the inverse of $Q$ is its transpose. A square matrix with orthonormal columns is called orthogonal; it is a rotation or a reflection, and it keeps lengths and angles: $\norm{Q\vy}^2 = \vy^\T Q^\T Q\vy = \norm{\vy}^2$. Check of the example: $2\vq_1 - \vq_2 = (1.2 + 0.8,\ 1.6 - 0.6) = (2, 1)$. Hou and Mason store orthonormal bases as the ROWS of a matrix: their $\operatorname{Null}(A)$ and $\operatorname{Row}(A)$ (2021, Sec. V) and the control-axis matrix $T$ of slide drawer-control-axes are matrices with orthonormal rows, for which $QQ^\T = I$ and the coordinates are $Q\vx$. lib/num.js and the notebook use the same row convention.

Projection onto a plane through the origin

  • With an orthonormal basis $\vq_1, \dots, \vq_k$ of a subspace, the projection of $\vb$ is $\vp = (\vq_1\cdot\vb)\,\vq_1 + \dots + (\vq_k\cdot\vb)\,\vq_k$, and $\ve = \vb - \vp$ is perpendicular to every $\vq_i$.
  • Plane $x + y + z = 0$: $\vq_1 = (1, -1, 0)/\sqrt2$, $\vq_2 = (1, 1, -2)/\sqrt6$. $\vb = (1, 2, 3)$ gives $\vq_1\cdot\vb = -0.7071$, $\vq_2\cdot\vb = -1.2247$.
  • $\vp = -\tfrac12(1, -1, 0) - \tfrac12(1, 1, -2) = (-1, 0, 1)$, $\ve = (2, 2, 2)$.
Only for orthonormal bases. For any other basis, use the normal equations (slide normal-equations).
The formula projects $\vb$ onto each basis vector separately and adds the pieces. It works because the $\vq_i$ are perpendicular to each other: the error $\ve = \vb - \sum_i (\vq_i\cdot\vb)\vq_i$ has $\vq_j\cdot\ve = \vq_j\cdot\vb - \vq_j\cdot\vb = 0$ for every $j$, since all the cross terms $\vq_j\cdot\vq_i$ vanish. With a non-orthonormal basis the cross terms stay, and the coordinates come from a linear system instead (the normal equations of Part 4). With the basis vectors as the columns of $Q$, the projection is $\vp = QQ^\T\vb$, so the projection matrix is $P = QQ^\T$; here $P = I - \tfrac13\vone\vone^\T$, which removes the component along the unit normal $(1, 1, 1)/\sqrt3$. Check: $\vb\cdot(1, 1, 1)/3 = 2$, so the normal component is $(2, 2, 2)$ and $\vp = (1, 2, 3) - (2, 2, 2) = (-1, 0, 1)$, the same answer. Where the basis vectors came from is the next slide. Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization and the QR factorization

  1. Normalize the first vector: $\vq_1 = \va_1/\norm{\va_1} = (3, 1)/\sqrt{10} = (0.9487, 0.3162)$.
  2. Remove the $\vq_1$ part of $\va_2$: $\vw_2 = \va_2 - (\vq_1\cdot\va_2)\,\vq_1 = (2, 2) - 0.8\,(3, 1) = (-0.4, 1.2)$.
  3. Normalize: $\vq_2 = \vw_2/\norm{\vw_2} = (-0.3162, 0.9487)$.
$A = [\va_1\ \va_2] = QR$, $Q = [\vq_1\ \vq_2]$, $R = \begin{bmatrix} \norm{\va_1} & \vq_1\cdot\va_2 \\ 0 & \norm{\vw_2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3.1623 & 2.5298 \\ 0 & 1.2649 \end{bmatrix}$.
Gram-Schmidt turns independent vectors $\va_1, \dots, \va_k$ into orthonormal vectors $\vq_1, \dots, \vq_k$ with the same span, one vector at a time: subtract from $\va_j$ its projections onto the $\vq$'s already built (slide projection-subspace), then scale the remainder $\vw_j$ to length 1. Here $\vq_1\cdot\va_2 = (6 + 2)/\sqrt{10} = 2.5298$ and $(\vq_1\cdot\va_2)\vq_1 = 0.8(3, 1) = (2.4, 0.8)$. Reading the steps backwards gives the factorization: $\va_1 = \norm{\va_1}\vq_1$ and $\va_2 = (\vq_1\cdot\va_2)\vq_1 + \norm{\vw_2}\vq_2$, so the columns of $A$ are combinations of the columns of $Q$ with the coefficients in the upper triangular $R$. A three-dimensional case, $\va_1 = (1, 1, 0)$, $\va_2 = (1, 0, 1)$: $R = \begin{bmatrix} 1.4142 & 0.7071 \\ 0 & 1.2247 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\vq_2 = (1, -1, 2)/\sqrt6$. When $\va_2$ is parallel to $\va_1$, $\vw_2 = \vzero$ and there is no $\vq_2$: Gram-Schmidt also detects dependence. Libraries compute QR with Householder reflections, which lose less accuracy in floating point; Num.qr does so.

Row space and null space as orthogonal complements

  • $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. $A\vv = \vzero$ says (row 1)$\cdot\vv = 0$ and (row 2)$\cdot\vv = 0$.
  • So every vector of $\Null(A)$ is perpendicular to every row, and to every combination of rows: $\Null(A) \perp \Row(A)$.
  • Dimensions: $2 + 1 = 3$ (rank-nullity), so every $\vv$ splits in exactly one way as $\vv = \vv_{\rm row} + \vv_{\rm null}$.
  • $\vn = (1, -1, -1)/\sqrt3$ spans $\Null(A)$. For $\vv = (1, 2, 3)$: $\vv_{\rm null} = (\vn\cdot\vv)\,\vn = (-\tfrac43, \tfrac43, \tfrac43)$ and $\vv_{\rm row} = (\tfrac73, \tfrac23, \tfrac53) = \tfrac23(1, 1, 0) + \tfrac53(1, 0, 1)$.
Two subspaces are orthogonal complements in $\R^n$ when every vector of one is perpendicular to every vector of the other and together they span $\R^n$. The first property is the row picture of $A\vv = \vzero$. The second follows from the dimensions: an orthonormal basis of $\Row(A)$ ($r$ vectors) together with one of $\Null(A)$ ($n - r$ vectors, by rank-nullity in deck 01) gives $n$ orthonormal vectors, which are independent and so a basis of $\R^n$. The split is unique: if $\vv_{\rm row} + \vv_{\rm null} = \vv_{\rm row}' + \vv_{\rm null}'$, then $\vv_{\rm row} - \vv_{\rm row}' = \vv_{\rm null}' - \vv_{\rm null}$ lies in both subspaces, so it is perpendicular to itself and equals $\vzero$. Check of the example: $\vn\cdot\vv = (1 - 2 - 3)/\sqrt3 = -4/\sqrt3$, and $\vv_{\rm row}\cdot\vn = (7/3 - 2/3 - 5/3)/\sqrt3 = 0$. Hou and Mason (2021, Sec. V) use this relation to turn a statement about null spaces into a statement about row spaces, which a rank computation can test (next two slides).

Null-space inclusion and reversed row-space inclusion

For $A$ and $B$ with $n$ columns: $\Null(A) \subseteq \Null(B)$ $\iff$ $\Row(B) \subseteq \Row(A)$ $\iff$ $\rank\begin{bmatrix} A \\ B \end{bmatrix} = \rank A$.
  1. If every row of $B$ is a combination of rows of $A$, then $A\vv = \vzero$ gives $B\vv = \vzero$.
  2. If $\Null(A) \subseteq \Null(B)$, every row of $B$ is perpendicular to $\Null(A)$, so it lies in the complement $\Row(A)$.
  3. Stacking $B$ under $A$ keeps the rank exactly when the new rows already lie in $\Row(A)$.

$A$ as on the previous slide. $B = (2, 1, 1)$ = row 1 + row 2: ranks 2 and 2, $B\vn = 0$. $B = (0, 0, 1)$: ranks 2 and 3, $B\vn = -0.5774$.

The figure shows $\Null(A)$ as a red line and $\Null(B)$, the plane of vectors perpendicular to the single row $B$, in yellow. Inclusion holds when the line lies in the plane. Step 1 is the direction ROW $\subseteq$ ROW $\Rightarrow$ NULL $\subseteq$ NULL: if $B = KA$ for some matrix $K$, then $B\vv = K(A\vv) = \vzero$. Step 2 is the reverse direction and uses the orthogonal complement of the previous slide: a vector perpendicular to all of $\Null(A)$ has zero null-space part, so it equals its row-space part. Step 3 turns the row-space inclusion into a count: $\Row\begin{bmatrix} A \\ B \end{bmatrix}$ contains $\Row(A)$, and the two are equal exactly when their dimensions are equal. The rank test needs no null-space basis and no solving, which is why Hou and Mason use it. In floating point the ranks come from the SVD with the tolerance $\max(m, n)\,\varepsilon\,\sigma_1$ (slide svd-statement); $B\vn$ is the same test done with the null-space basis, and it is $0$ up to rounding exactly when $B$ passes.

Eigenvalues, quadratic forms, and positive definite matrices

Directions a matrix only stretches, the bowls and saddles of $\tfrac12\vz^\T A\vz$, and the Cholesky factor that exists exactly when the bowl has a bottom.

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a matrix

  • An eigenvector of a square matrix $A$ is a nonzero $\vv$ with $A\vv = \lambda\vv$. The number $\lambda$ is its eigenvalue.
  • $A$ keeps the direction of $\vv$ and scales its length by $\lambda$; $\lambda < 0$ flips it.
  • $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$: $A(1, 1) = (3, 3)$, so $\lambda = 3$; $A(1, -1) = (1, -1)$, so $\lambda = 1$.
  • A shear has one eigenvector direction. A $90^\circ$ rotation has none.
Most vectors change direction under $A$; the figure shows the angle between $\vv$ and $A\vv$ as $\vv$ moves around the circle. Along an eigenvector the angle is $0^\circ$ (or $180^\circ$ for a negative eigenvalue) and $A$ acts as multiplication by a number. Any nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is again an eigenvector with the same eigenvalue, so an eigenvector is really a direction; the slides scale them to length 1 with the largest entry positive. The presets: the symmetric matrix has perpendicular eigenvector directions (slide spectral-theorem); $[[4, 1], [2, 3]]$ has eigenvalues 5 and 2 on directions $71.57^\circ$ apart; the shear $[[1, 1], [0, 1]]$ has the double eigenvalue 1 but only the $x$-axis as eigenvector direction; the rotation by $90^\circ$ turns every vector, and its eigenvalues are the complex pair $\pm i$; the diagonal matrix flips the $y$-axis ($\lambda = -0.5$). An eigenvalue $\lambda = 0$ means $A\vv = \vzero$: the eigenvectors of 0 are the nonzero vectors of $\Null(A)$.

Eigenvalues of a $2\times 2$ matrix from the characteristic equation

$\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$: $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = (\lambda - 5)(\lambda - 2)$. Eigenvectors $(0.7071, 0.7071)$ and $(-0.4472, 0.8944)$.
  1. $A\vv = \lambda\vv$ is the same as $(A - \lambda I)\vv = \vzero$.
  2. A nonzero solution $\vv$ exists only when $A - \lambda I$ is singular: $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.
  3. For $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$: $(a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc = \lambda^2 - (\tr A)\,\lambda + \det A = 0$.
  4. $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$: $\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 1)$, so $\lambda = 3$ and $1$. $A - 3I = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ has null space along $(1, 1)$; $A - I$ along $(1, -1)$.

$\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = \tr A$ and $\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \det A$.

Step 2 uses deck 01: a square matrix sends a nonzero vector to $\vzero$ exactly when its determinant is zero, because then it squashes the plane onto a line and has no inverse. The characteristic polynomial $\det(A - \lambda I)$ has degree $n$ for an $n\times n$ matrix, so $A$ has $n$ eigenvalues counted with multiplicity, some possibly complex. In the $2\times 2$ case the quadratic formula gives $\lambda = \tfrac12\tr A \pm \sqrt{\tfrac14(\tr A)^2 - \det A}$; the function d02_eigen_2x2 computes exactly this. When the quantity under the root is negative the eigenvalues are complex, as for the rotation. Expanding $(\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2) = \lambda^2 - (\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)\lambda + \lambda_1\lambda_2$ and matching coefficients gives the sum and product rules. Check of the second example: $\tr = 7 = 5 + 2$, $\det = 12 - 2 = 10 = 5\cdot 2$; for $\lambda = 2$, $A - 2I = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ has null space along $(-1, 2)$, which normalizes to $(-0.4472, 0.8944)$. For a triangular matrix the eigenvalues are the diagonal entries.

Symmetric matrices and the spectral theorem

  • A symmetric matrix ($A^\T = A$) has real eigenvalues: for $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & d \end{bmatrix}$, $\tfrac14(\tr A)^2 - \det A = \bigl(\tfrac{a - d}{2}\bigr)^2 + b^2 \ge 0$.
  • Eigenvectors of different eigenvalues are perpendicular:
  1. $\lambda_1\,\vv_1\cdot\vv_2 = (A\vv_1)\cdot\vv_2 = \vv_1\cdot(A^\T\vv_2)$
  2. $\phantom{\lambda_1\,\vv_1\cdot\vv_2} = \vv_1\cdot(A\vv_2) = \lambda_2\,\vv_1\cdot\vv_2$
  3. so $(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\,\vv_1\cdot\vv_2 = 0$ and $\vv_1\cdot\vv_2 = 0$.
Spectral theorem. A symmetric $A$ is $V\Lambda V^\T$ with $V^\T V = I$ and $\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$.

$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = V\diag(3, 1)V^\T$ with $V = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$. For $\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$: $\vv_1\cdot\vv_2 = 0.3162$, $71.57^\circ$ apart.

The first bullet expands $\tfrac14(a + d)^2 - (ad - b^2) = \tfrac14(a^2 + 2ad + d^2) - ad + b^2 = \tfrac14(a - d)^2 + b^2$, a sum of squares, so the eigenvalues $\tfrac12\tr A \pm \sqrt{\cdot}$ are real. The proof of perpendicularity uses the transpose rule $(A\vx)\cdot\vy = \vx\cdot(A^\T\vy)$ of deck 01 and then $A^\T = A$. It needs $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$; when an eigenvalue repeats, its eigenvectors form a subspace, and Gram-Schmidt picks an orthonormal basis of it. Collect the unit eigenvectors as the columns of $V$. Then $AV = V\Lambda$ (column $i$ reads $A\vv_i = \lambda_i\vv_i$), and $V^{-1} = V^\T$ gives $A = V\Lambda V^\T = \lambda_1\vv_1\vv_1^\T + \dots + \lambda_n\vv_n\vv_n^\T$: a sum of projections onto the eigenvector directions, each scaled by its eigenvalue. The figure shows the consequence: a symmetric matrix maps the circle to an ellipse whose axes are its eigenvectors. A non-symmetric matrix also maps the circle to an ellipse, but its axes are not its eigenvectors; the singular vectors of Part 3 are.

Quadratic forms as bowls, saddles, and valleys

$J(\vz) = \tfrac12\vz^\T A\vz$ with $A = V\diag(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)V^\T$ and the first eigenvector at angle $\theta$. Both $\lambda_i > 0$: a bowl. Opposite signs: a saddle. One zero: a valley with a line of minimizers. Both negative: a cap. Right: level sets $J = 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2$ (solid) and $J < 0$ (dashed).



The tex (line 1133) calls $J(\vz) = \tfrac12\vz^\T A\vz$ the model problem of optimization: near a minimizer $\vz^\star$ a smooth function is its value there plus a quadratic form in $\vz - \vz^\star$ whose matrix is the Hessian (deck 03 builds the Taylor expansion, deck 05 uses it). The sliders set the eigenvalues and the direction of the first eigenvector; the matrix is rebuilt as $V\diag(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)V^\T$ by d02_quadratic_from_eigen. Along the eigenvector $\vv_i$ the surface is the parabola $\tfrac12\lambda_i t^2$, so each eigenvalue is the curvature in its direction: a large eigenvalue gives a steep wall, a small one a shallow floor. A bowl has a single minimizer at $\vzero$; a valley ($\lambda_2 = 0$) is flat along $\vv_2$, and every point of that line is a minimizer; a saddle goes up along one eigenvector and down along the other, so $\vzero$ is neither a minimum nor a maximum. The readout shows the matrix, the shape, the semi-axes of the level set $J = 1$ (next slide) and $\kappa = \lambda_1/\lambda_2$ for a bowl. Gradient descent on a bowl with $\kappa = 10$ or more zig-zags across the narrow direction (deck 05).

Level sets of a quadratic form in eigenvector coordinates

  1. Eigenvector coordinates: $\vz = V\vy$, that is $\vy = V^\T\vz$.
  2. $\vz^\T A\vz = \vy^\T V^\T(V\Lambda V^\T)V\vy = \vy^\T\Lambda\vy$.
  3. $\vy^\T\Lambda\vy = \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \lambda_2 y_2^2$: no cross term.
  4. For $\lambda_1, \lambda_2 > 0$, $J = c$ is the ellipse $\dfrac{y_1^2}{2c/\lambda_1} + \dfrac{y_2^2}{2c/\lambda_2} = 1$, semi-axis $\sqrt{2c/\lambda_i}$ along $\vv_i$.
$c = 1$, $\lambda = 3, 1$: $\sqrt{2/3} = 0.8165$ along $(1, 1)$ and $\sqrt2 = 1.4142$ along $(1, -1)$. Axis ratio $\sqrt3 = \sqrt\kappa$, where $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min} = 3$.
Step 2 uses $V^\T V = I$ twice. Step 3 is the reason eigenvectors matter for optimization: in the rotated coordinates $\vy$ the quadratic splits into independent one-dimensional parabolas, one per eigenvalue, which the tex states at line 1145. Step 4 divides $\tfrac12(\lambda_1 y_1^2 + \lambda_2 y_2^2) = c$ by $c$. The semi-axis along $\vv_i$ is proportional to $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$: the steep direction (large $\lambda$) has the short axis. The ratio of the long axis to the short one is $\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}} = \sqrt\kappa$, where $\kappa$ is the condition number of $A$ (tex definition box at line 1153; slide condition-number defines it for every matrix). For $\kappa = 1$ the level sets are circles; for $\kappa = 100$ they are ellipses ten times longer than wide. If an eigenvalue is zero or negative the level sets are not ellipses: two parallel lines for a valley, hyperbolas for a saddle. The tex (line 1149) states the ellipsoid shape without the condition $A \succ 0$; the errata cell of the notebook records this.

Positive definite and positive semidefinite matrices

  • Symmetric $A$ is positive definite, $A \succ 0$, when $\vz^\T A\vz > 0$ for every $\vz \ne \vzero$; positive semidefinite, $A \succeq 0$, when $\vz^\T A\vz \ge 0$; indefinite when both signs occur.
  • Eigenvalue test: $\vz^\T A\vz = \sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2$, so $A \succ 0$ exactly when every $\lambda_i > 0$, and $A \succeq 0$ when every $\lambda_i \ge 0$.
  • $2\times 2$ test: $A \succ 0$ exactly when $a_{11} > 0$ and $\det A > 0$.
The eigenvalue test follows from the previous slide: with $\vy = V^\T\vz$, $\vz \ne \vzero$ exactly when $\vy \ne \vzero$, and $\sum_i\lambda_i y_i^2 > 0$ for every nonzero $\vy$ exactly when every $\lambda_i > 0$ (take $\vy = \ve_i$ to see that each $\lambda_i$ must be positive). The $2\times 2$ test: $\det A = \lambda_1\lambda_2 > 0$ means the eigenvalues have the same sign, and $a_{11} = \ve_1^\T A\ve_1 > 0$ rules out both negative. The three panels are the three cases. $[[2, 1], [1, 2]]$ has eigenvalues 3 and 1: a bowl. $[[1, 1], [1, 1]]$ has eigenvalues 2 and 0: a valley, with $\vz^\T A\vz = (z_1 + z_2)^2$, zero along $(1, -1)$. $[[1, 2], [2, 1]]$ has eigenvalues 3 and $-1$: a saddle, and $\vz = (1, -1)$ gives $1 - 2 - 2 + 1 = -2$. Deck 07 uses one more fact: sums of PSD matrices and nonnegative multiples of them are PSD, so the PSD matrices form a convex cone. Decks 08 and 09 need $\succ 0$ for the cost matrices of LQR and for the $Q_{uu}$ block of iLQR.

Gram matrices and the Delassus matrix of a four-legged table

  • For any $m\times n$ matrix $A$: $\vz^\T(A^\T A)\vz = (A\vz)\cdot(A\vz) = \norm{A\vz}^2 \ge 0$, so $A^\T A \succeq 0$.
  • $A^\T A \succ 0$ exactly when $A\vz = \vzero$ only for $\vz = \vzero$: the columns of $A$ are independent.
  • Table top on legs at $(\pm 1, \pm 1)$ m, velocity $(v_z, \omega_x, \omega_y)$. Leg $i$ moves up at $v_z + \omega_x y_i - \omega_y x_i$, so row $i$ of $J_c$ is $[1\ \ y_i\ \ {-x_i}]$.
  • With $M = I$: $J_c^\T J_c = 4I$, and the Delassus matrix $G = J_c M^{-1}J_c^\T$ has eigenvalues 4, 4, 4, 0.
$G(1, -1, 1, -1) = \vzero$: this pattern of leg forces moves nothing.
$A^\T A$ is called the Gram matrix of the columns of $A$: entry $(i, j)$ is $\va_i\cdot\va_j$. It is symmetric, and the one-line computation on the slide shows it is PSD. It is singular exactly when some nonzero $\vz$ has $A\vz = \vzero$. The Delassus matrix $G = J_c M^{-1}J_c^\T$ (tex line 1168; deck 19) has the same structure with $A = L^\T J_c^\T$, where $M^{-1} = LL^\T$ is a Cholesky factorization of the inverse mass matrix (next slide). Here $J_c$ is $4\times 3$: four legs, three motions of the top that move the legs vertically. Its four rows live in $\R^3$, so they are dependent, and $G = J_cJ_c^\T$ ($4\times 4$) has rank at most 3. The null vector $(1, -1, 1, -1)$ is a set of leg forces with zero sum and zero moment about both axes: it squeezes the table without moving it. This is the statical indeterminacy of the four-legged table (deck 13; deck 01, the four-legged table): rigid-body statics cannot tell how the load is shared, and the tex calls the configuration hyperstatic with $\kappa = \infty$. Slide table-forces picks one distribution.

Cholesky factorization of a $2\times 2$ matrix

Cholesky: $A = LL^\T$ with $L$ lower triangular and $L_{ii} > 0$.

$$LL^\T = \begin{bmatrix} L_{11}^2 & L_{11}L_{21} \\ L_{11}L_{21} & L_{21}^2 + L_{22}^2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Match the entries of $A$:

  1. $L_{11} = \sqrt{a_{11}}$
  2. $L_{21} = a_{21}/L_{11}$
  3. $L_{22} = \sqrt{a_{22} - L_{21}^2}$
$A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$: $L_{11} = 2$, $L_{21} = 1$, $L_{22} = \sqrt2$.

Solve $A\vz = \vb$, $\vb = (2, 1)$, by two triangular solves:

  1. Forward, $L\vw = \vb$: $2w_1 = 2$, $w_1 = 1$; $w_1 + \sqrt2\,w_2 = 1$, $w_2 = 0$.
  2. Back, $L^\T\vz = \vw$: $\sqrt2\,z_2 = 0$, $z_2 = 0$; $2z_1 + z_2 = 1$, $z_1 = 0.5$.
  3. Check: $A(0.5, 0) = (2, 1)$.
For $n\times n$: $n^3/3$ flops to factor (LU: $2n^3/3$), $n^2$ per triangular solve.
This is the worked example of the tex (lines 1211-1231), with each entry matched separately. Entry $(1, 1)$ of $LL^\T$ is $L_{11}^2$; entry $(2, 1)$ is $L_{21}L_{11}$; entry $(2, 2)$ is $L_{21}^2 + L_{22}^2$. Entry $(1, 2)$ repeats $(2, 1)$ because $LL^\T$ is symmetric, so a symmetric $A$ gives three equations in three unknowns, solved in order. For $n\times n$ the same matching runs column by column: $L_{jj} = \sqrt{a_{jj} - \sum_{k < j} L_{jk}^2}$ and $L_{ij} = (a_{ij} - \sum_{k < j}L_{ik}L_{jk})/L_{jj}$ for $i > j$. A triangular system is solved one unknown at a time: the forward solve starts at the top row, the back solve at the bottom. The tex's rule (line 1196) is to factor and substitute instead of forming $A^{-1}$: the factor costs $n^3/3$ once, and each new right-hand side costs $2n^2$, which is why ADMM and iLQR reuse the factor of $G + \rho I$ or $Q_{uu}$ across iterations (decks 09 and 19). Num.chol and Num.cholSolve do the same in the browser.

Existence of the Cholesky factor and positive definiteness

The last entry: $L_{22}^2 = a_{22} - \dfrac{a_{21}^2}{a_{11}} = \dfrac{\det A}{a_{11}}$.

The Cholesky factor exists exactly when $A \succ 0$.
  • If $L$ exists: $\vz^\T A\vz = \vz^\T LL^\T\vz = \norm{L^\T\vz}^2 > 0$ for $\vz \ne \vzero$.
  • If $A \succ 0$: $a_{11} = \ve_1^\T A\ve_1 > 0$, and $\vz = (-a_{21}/a_{11},\ 1)$ gives $\vz^\T A\vz = L_{22}^2 > 0$.
  • $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 + \mu & 2 \\ 2 & 1 + \mu \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $3 + \mu$ and $\mu - 1$. $L_{22}^2 = -3$ at $\mu = 0$, $0$ at $\mu = 1$, $0.9$ at $\mu = 1.5$.
$L_{22}^2 = a_{22} - (a_{21}/\sqrt{a_{11}})^2$, and multiplying by $a_{11}$ gives $a_{11}a_{22} - a_{21}^2 = \det A$. The first bullet proves that a factor forces $A \succ 0$: $L^\T$ has positive diagonal, so it is invertible and $L^\T\vz \ne \vzero$ for $\vz \ne \vzero$. The second proves the converse for $2\times 2$: both square roots of the factorization have positive arguments. With $\vz = (-a_{21}/a_{11}, 1)$: $\vz^\T A\vz = a_{21}^2/a_{11} - 2a_{21}^2/a_{11} + a_{22} = a_{22} - a_{21}^2/a_{11}$. The $n\times n$ proof is the same argument applied column by column (the pivot $L_{jj}^2$ is a ratio of leading determinants). So an attempted Cholesky factorization is also the standard PD test (tex line 1206): iLQR tries to factor $Q_{uu}$, and on failure adds $\mu I$ and tries again (deck 09). The slider does that to $[[1, 2], [2, 1]]$: adding $\mu I$ raises both eigenvalues by $\mu$, and the factorization succeeds for every $\mu > 1$. At $\mu = 1$ the matrix is only PSD and $L_{22} = 0$, which a solver treats as failure.

Singular values and conditioning

The ellipse a matrix makes of the unit circle, the condition number as its aspect ratio, and a drawer on a rail as the example Hou and Mason care about.

Image of the unit circle under a $2\times 2$ matrix

  • Every $2\times 2$ matrix maps the unit circle onto an ellipse, flat when $\det A = 0$.
  • Its semi-axes $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge 0$ are the singular values, along unit vectors $\vu_1, \vu_2$; they are images of perpendicular unit vectors: $A\vv_i = \sigma_i\vu_i$.
  • $\norm{A\vv}^2 = \vv^\T A^\T A\vv$, so the $\vv_i$ are eigenvectors of $A^\T A$ and $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A^\T A)}$.
  • $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$: $A^\T A = \begin{bmatrix} 25 & 20 \\ 20 & 25 \end{bmatrix}$, eigenvalues 45 and 5, $\sigma = 6.708, 2.236$.
The length of $A\vv$ for a unit vector $\vv$ is $\sqrt{\vv^\T(A^\T A)\vv}$, a quadratic form in the symmetric PSD matrix $A^\T A$ (slide gram-psd). By Part 2 its largest value over unit vectors is the largest eigenvalue, reached at the top eigenvector, and its smallest value is the smallest eigenvalue. So the longest and shortest images are $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{\lambda_1}$ and $\sigma_2 = \sqrt{\lambda_2}$, reached at the perpendicular eigenvectors $\vv_1, \vv_2$ of $A^\T A$. Their images are perpendicular too: $(A\vv_1)\cdot(A\vv_2) = \vv_1^\T A^\T A\vv_2 = \lambda_2\,\vv_1\cdot\vv_2 = 0$. For the example, $\vv_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt2$ gives $A\vv_1 = (3, 9)/\sqrt2 = 6.708\,(0.3162, 0.9487)$, and $\vv_2 = (1, -1)/\sqrt2$ gives $A\vv_2 = (3, -1)/\sqrt2 = 2.236\,(0.9487, -0.3162)$. The area of the ellipse is $\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2 = \pi\abs{\det A}$: here $6.708 \times 2.236 = 15 = \det A$. Drag a column onto the line of the other to make $\sigma_2 = 0$; the ellipse collapses onto a segment and the readout reports rank 1.

Singular value decomposition of an $m\times n$ matrix

  • $A = U\Sigma V^\T = \sigma_1\vu_1\vv_1^\T + \dots + \sigma_r\vu_r\vv_r^\T$ for every $m\times n$ matrix: $U$ ($m\times m$) and $V$ ($n\times n$) orthogonal, $\Sigma$ ($m\times n$) zero except $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$ on its diagonal.
  • $\rank A$ = the number of nonzero $\sigma_i$. Rows 1 to $r$ of $V^\T$ are an orthonormal basis of $\Row(A)$; rows $r + 1$ to $n$ are one of $\Null(A)$.
$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$: $AA^\T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, so $\sigma = \sqrt3 = 1.7321$ and $1$; rank 2. $\Row(A)$: $(2, 1, 1)/\sqrt6$ and $(0, 1, -1)/\sqrt2$. $\Null(A)$: $(1, -1, -1)/\sqrt3$.
The strip shows the three factors of $A = [[3, 0], [4, 5]]$ acting in turn on the unit circle: $V^\T$ rotates (or reflects) the right singular vectors $\vv_i$ onto the axes $\ve_i$, $\Sigma$ stretches axis $i$ by $\sigma_i$, and $U$ rotates the axes onto the left singular vectors $\vu_i$. Every matrix is rotation, stretch, rotation. For a wide or tall matrix $\Sigma$ is rectangular: $\sigma_i$ sits at $(i, i)$ and the extra rows or columns are zero. Why $V^\T$ holds the four-subspace bases: $A\vv_i = \sigma_i\vu_i$, which is $\vzero$ for $i > r$, so $\vv_{r+1}, \dots, \vv_n$ are orthonormal vectors in $\Null(A)$, and there are $n - r$ of them, its dimension. The first $r$ are perpendicular to those, so they span the complement $\Row(A)$. Numerically, rank counts the singular values above $\max(m, n)\,\varepsilon\,\sigma_1$ with $\varepsilon = 2.2\times 10^{-16}$, the default of numpy and lib/num.js. Hou and Mason's Null(·) and Row(·) (2021, Sec. V) are exactly these blocks of $V^\T$; the notebook's d02_svd_subspaces returns them with the largest entry of each vector made positive.

Condition number and error amplification

$\cond A = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ (Hou and Mason 2021, eq. 8); $\cond A = \infty$ when $A$ is singular.

For $A\vz = \vb$ it bounds how a relative error in $\vb$ grows in $\vz$:

  1. $A\,\Delta\vz = \Delta\vb$, and $A^{-1}$ stretches by at most $1/\sigma_{\min}$: $\norm{\Delta\vz} \le \norm{\Delta\vb}/\sigma_{\min}$.
  2. $\vb = A\vz$ and $A$ stretches by at most $\sigma_{\max}$: $\norm{\vb} \le \sigma_{\max}\norm{\vz}$.
  3. Multiply the first by $1/\norm{\vz} \le \sigma_{\max}/\norm{\vb}$: $\dfrac{\norm{\Delta\vz}}{\norm{\vz}} \le \cond A\,\dfrac{\norm{\Delta\vb}}{\norm{\vb}}$.

$\diag(1, 0.01)$: $\cond = 100$. $\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$: $6.708/2.236 = 3$. Symmetric $A \succ 0$: $\cond A = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min} = \kappa$.

$A^{-1} = V\Sigma^{-1}U^\T$ has singular values $1/\sigma_i$, so its largest stretch is $1/\sigma_{\min}$: the figure shows the unit disk of possible $\Delta\vb$ and the ellipse of the resulting $\Delta\vz$. Step 3 multiplies the two inequalities of steps 1 and 2. The bound is reached: take $\vb$ along $\vu_1$ (so $\vz$ is as short as possible for its $\vb$) and $\Delta\vb$ along $\vu_{\min}$ (so $\Delta\vz$ is as long as possible). Hou and Mason write $\cond(A) = \norm{A}_2\norm{A^\dagger}_2$, where $\norm{A}_2 = \sigma_{\max}$ is the largest stretch and $A^\dagger$ is the pseudoinverse of Part 4, whose largest stretch is $1/\sigma_{\min}$. For a symmetric PD matrix the singular values are the eigenvalues, and the condition number is the $\kappa$ of the tex's definition box (line 1153) and of slide quadratic-derivation. A rule of thumb from floating point: solving a system with $\cond A = 10^k$ loses about $k$ of the 16 significant digits. Deck 05 shows that $\kappa$ also sets how many gradient-descent steps a bowl needs.

Linear systems with nearly parallel rows

  • Change the second right side to $2.02$: solution $(0, 2)$.
  • The rows are $0.2851^\circ$ apart and $\cond = 402.01$. $\vb$ changed by 0.353 %, $\vz$ by 100 %: a factor 283.55, under the bound 402.01.
  • Two unit rows $\varphi$ apart: $\cond = \cot(\varphi/2)$.

Each equation of a $2\times 2$ system is a line; the solution is where the lines cross. When the lines cross at a small angle, moving one of them a little slides the crossing a long way along the thin wedge between them. The amount is the geometry of the figure: moving a line by $\Delta b$ perpendicular to itself moves the crossing by $\Delta b/\sin\varphi$ along the other line. The singular values say the same thing: for unit rows $\va_1, \va_2$ at angle $\varphi$, $AA^\T = [[1, \cos\varphi], [\cos\varphi, 1]]$ has eigenvalues $1 \pm \cos\varphi = 2\cos^2(\varphi/2)$ and $2\sin^2(\varphi/2)$, so $\cond A = \cot(\varphi/2)$, about $2/\varphi$ for small $\varphi$ in radians. The worked example uses the rows $(1, 1)$ and $(1, 1.01)$, whose relative change of $\vb$ is $0.01/\norm{(2, 2.01)} = 0.00353$ and of $\vz$ is $\norm{(-1, 1)}/\norm{(1, 1)} = 1$. The study plan's “ill-conditioned systems” video covers this case. At $\varphi = 0$ the lines are parallel: either no crossing or the same line, the subject of the next slide.

Ill-conditioned and inconsistent systems

  • Fragile (ill-conditioned): one solution exists, but $\cond A$ is large, so small errors in $A$ or $\vb$ move it far. In (b) a change of 0.1 moves it 2.87.
  • Infeasible (inconsistent): $\rank[A\,|\,\vb] > \rank A$, and no $\vz$ satisfies every row. Least squares finds the closest miss, 0.0707 in (c).
The two failure modes look alike in the picture, two lines that are nearly or exactly parallel, and they are different problems. A fragile system still has one solution, and a solver returns it; the solution is untrustworthy because it depends strongly on the data, by the factor $\cond A$ of slide condition-number (here 57.29 for $\varphi = 2^\circ$, and the shift of 2.87 is 28.7 times the change of 0.1). An infeasible system has no solution at all: the augmented matrix $[A\,|\,\vb]$ has larger rank than $A$ (deck 01, consistency), so $\vb$ is outside $\Col(A)$. Panel (d) is the third outcome of parallel rows, a consistent system with a whole line of solutions. Control-axis matrix $T$ for the drawer

  • Hou and Mason pick the control axes as the rows of an orthonormal $T$. Drawer: $T = \begin{bmatrix} \sin\theta & -\cos\theta \\ \cos\theta & \sin\theta \end{bmatrix}$.
  • $\vw = T\vv$ and $\veta = T\vf$: velocity and force along the axes. The last row is the velocity command $C$; row 1 is force-controlled.
  • $T^\T T = I$: $\vv = T^\T\vw$, $\cond T = 1$, and $\veta\cdot\vw = \vf^\T T^\T T\vv = \vf\cdot\vv$.
  • $\theta = 30^\circ$: $\vv = (1.1547, 0)$ gives $\vw = (0.5774, 1)$.
This answers the study plan's question “what does $T$ do”. $T$ is a rotation of coordinates: it rewrites the generalized velocity $\vv$ and force $\vf$ in a new orthonormal frame whose axes the controller chooses. Along each axis the robot controls one thing: the force along the force-controlled axes, the velocity along the velocity-controlled ones. Hou and Mason 2019 write $T = \diag(I_u, R_a)$: the unactuated coordinates keep their identity rows and $R_a$ rotates the actuated ones; the 2021 paper requires $R_a$ orthonormal so that force and velocity axes are perpendicular (reciprocal). Because $T$ is orthonormal, it loses nothing ($\vv = T^\T\vw$) and it keeps power, force times velocity. For the drawer the best choice is $\theta = 0$: velocity control along the rail and force control against the rail normal, where the rail itself supplies whatever force is needed. The readout shows $T$, and $\vw$ for the velocity $\vv = (1/\cos\theta, 0)$ that a command $b = 1$ produces; the second entry of $\vw$ is always $b$. Deck 21 builds $T$ for the block-tilting task.

The condition number of a matrix depends on how its rows are written, but the controller only cares about the set of velocities the rows allow. Multiplying the equation $\vc\cdot\vv = b$ by 10 changes nothing physical and changes $\cond$ from 2.4142 to 14.2132. Hou and Mason (2021, Sec. IV-A) remove both artifacts before measuring. For the contact rows $J$, which may be redundant when a contact is modeled by several points, they replace $J$ by $\hat J = \operatorname{Row}(J)$, an orthonormal basis of its rows, which has $\cond = 1$ and describes the same constraint. For the command rows $C$ they divide each row by its length, the standard remedy for artificial ill-conditioning. The result, eq. 9, is the crashing index: it is 1 when the commands are perpendicular to the constraints and to each other, and it grows without bound as a command approaches a constraint. With a redundant $J$ the raw number 1.4142 is not even the right kind of warning: the command is ideal, and the index says so. d02_crashing_index computes both columns.

Least squares, minimum-norm solutions, and the Schur complement

The best answer when a system has no solution, the shortest answer when it has many, and block elimination for the systems of later decks.

Least-squares fit of a line to four points

  • Points $(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 2), (3, 4)$, line $y = c + d\,t$: four equations $c + d\,t_i = y_i$ in two unknowns.
  • As a system $A\vx = \vy$: row $i$ of $A$ is $[1\ \ t_i]$, $\vx = (c, d)$. No exact solution.
  • Least squares minimizes $\sum_i e_i^2 = \norm{\vy - A\vx}^2$.
  • Normal equations (next slide): $\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}\vx = \begin{bmatrix} 9 \\ 18 \end{bmatrix}$, so $c = d = 0.9$. Residuals $(0.1, 0.2, -0.7, 0.4)$, sum of squares 0.7.
A line through four points that do not lie on one line must miss some of them. Least squares chooses the line that makes the sum of squared vertical misses smallest. Squares are used because they make the problem a quadratic with a closed-form answer, because they penalize large misses more than small ones, and because they are the right criterion when the measurement noise is Gaussian (deck 04). They also give outliers a strong pull: drag one point far from the others to see the line follow it. The entries of the normal equations are sums over the data: $A^\T A = \begin{bmatrix} n & \sum t_i \\ \sum t_i & \sum t_i^2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}$ and $A^\T\vy = (\sum y_i, \sum t_i y_i) = (9, 18)$. Cramer's rule with $\det = 56 - 36 = 20$: $c = (14\cdot 9 - 6\cdot 18)/20 = 0.9$ and $d = (4\cdot 18 - 6\cdot 9)/20 = 0.9$. The residuals sum to zero, which is the first normal equation. The tex (lines 1234-1266) lists inverse kinematics, model fitting and the Gauss-Newton step as uses of the same computation.

Normal equations from the orthogonality of the residual

  1. $A\vx$ runs over $\Col(A)$. The closest point $\vp = A\hat\vx$ to $\vb$ has an error $\ve = \vb - A\hat\vx$ perpendicular to $\Col(A)$.
  2. Perpendicular to every column $\va_j$: $\va_j\cdot\ve = 0$, that is $A^\T(\vb - A\hat\vx) = \vzero$.
  3. Normal equations: $A^\T A\,\hat\vx = A^\T\vb$.
  4. Any other $\vx$ is worse: $\vb - A\vx = \ve + A(\hat\vx - \vx)$, two perpendicular parts, so $\norm{\vb - A\vx}^2 = \norm{\ve}^2 + \norm{A(\hat\vx - \vx)}^2$.
$A^\T A$ is invertible exactly when the columns of $A$ are independent; then $\hat\vx = (A^\T A)^{-1}A^\T\vb$.

Line fit: $A^\T\ve = (0.1 + 0.2 - 0.7 + 0.4,\ 0.2 - 1.4 + 1.2) = (0, 0)$.

This is projection onto a subspace (slide projection-subspace) with a basis that is not orthonormal: the columns of $A$. The condition “error perpendicular to the subspace” is still the right one, and it becomes the normal equations; they are called normal because the error is normal (perpendicular) to $\Col(A)$. Step 4 is the Pythagoras argument of slide projection-derivation in $\R^m$: $A(\hat\vx - \vx)$ lies in $\Col(A)$ and $\ve$ is perpendicular to it. The invertibility statement is slide gram-psd: $A^\T A \succ 0$ exactly when the columns are independent. Calculus gives the same equations: the gradient of $\tfrac12\norm{A\vx - \vb}^2$ is $A^\T(A\vx - \vb)$ (deck 03), and setting it to zero is step 3; the tex (line 1238) takes this route. In practice the normal equations square the condition number, $\cond(A^\T A) = \cond(A)^2$, so libraries solve least squares with QR or the SVD instead. Gauss-Newton (deck 05) solves one such system per iteration for the linearized residual.

Least squares as projection onto the column space

  • $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $\vb = (6, 0, 0)$: a line fit to $(0, 6), (1, 0), (2, 0)$.
  • $A^\T A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$, $A^\T\vb = (6, 0)$, $\hat\vx = (5, -3)$.
  • $\vp = A\hat\vx = (5, 2, -1)$; $\ve = (1, -2, 1)$, $\norm{\ve} = 2.4495$, $A^\T\ve = \vzero$.
  • $P = A(A^\T A)^{-1}A^\T = \tfrac16\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 & -1 \\ 2 & 2 & 2 \\ -1 & 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$, and $P\vb = \vp$.
This example is Strang's (Introduction to Linear Algebra, Sec. 4.3; MIT 18.06 lecture 16, which the study plan lists for week 5). The data $\vb$ has three entries and the model has two parameters, so $\Col(A)$ is a plane in $\R^3$ and $\vb$ is off it. Solving the normal equations: $\det(A^\T A) = 15 - 9 = 6$, $\hat x_1 = (5\cdot 6 - 3\cdot 0)/6 = 5$, $\hat x_2 = (3\cdot 0 - 3\cdot 6)/6 = -3$. So the best line is $y = 5 - 3t$, with fitted values $5, 2, -1$ at $t = 0, 1, 2$ and residuals $1, -2, 1$. The error is perpendicular to both columns: $(1, 1, 1)\cdot(1, -2, 1) = 0$ and $(0, 1, 2)\cdot(1, -2, 1) = 0$. The projection matrix has the two properties of slide projection-derivation, $P^2 = P$ and $P^\T = P$, and its trace is 2, the dimension of the plane it projects onto (its eigenvalues are 1, 1, 0). Minimum-norm solution of an underdetermined system

  • $x_1 + x_2 = 2$ has the solutions $(1, 1) + t(1, -1)$: one equation, two unknowns.
  • $\norm{\vx}^2 = (1 + t)^2 + (1 - t)^2 = 2 + 2t^2$, smallest at $t = 0$: $\vx^+ = (1, 1)$, $\norm{\vx^+} = 1.4142$.
  • $\vx^+$ lies in $\Row(A)$, perpendicular to $\Null(A)$. Any solution is $\vx = \vx^+ + \vn$ with $\vn \in \Null(A)$, so $\norm{\vx}^2 = \norm{\vx^+}^2 + \norm{\vn}^2$.
Wide $A$ with independent rows: $\vx^+ = A^\T(AA^\T)^{-1}\vb$, the minimum-norm solution.
A system with more unknowns than independent equations has a whole solution set: one particular solution plus the null space (deck 01, solution sets). The minimum-norm solution picks the point of that set closest to the origin. Geometrically it is the foot of the perpendicular from $\vzero$ to the solution line, so it is perpendicular to the line's direction, which spans $\Null(A)$; by the orthogonal complement (slide orthogonal-complement) it lies in $\Row(A)$. That gives the formula: a vector in $\Row(A)$ is $\vx = A^\T\vy$ for some $\vy$, and $A\vx = \vb$ becomes $AA^\T\vy = \vb$, solvable when the rows are independent ($AA^\T \succ 0$). Here $A = [1\ 1]$, $AA^\T = 2$, $\vy = 1$ and $\vx^+ = (1, 1)$. The Pythagoras identity in the third bullet shows why every other solution is longer: its extra piece $\vn$ is perpendicular to $\vx^+$. At $t = 0.6$ the readout shows $\norm{\vx}^2 = 2 + 2(0.36) = 2.72$. Deck 06 derives the same formula as an equality-constrained QP with Lagrange multipliers.

Minimum-norm leg forces of a four-legged table

  • $M\vf = (100, 20, 10)$: three equations, four legs. $MM^\T = 4I$, so $\vf^+ = \tfrac14 M^\T(100, 20, 10) = (32.5, 22.5, 17.5, 27.5)$ N.
  • All solutions: $\vf^+ + t(1, -1, 1, -1)$. Every leg pushes for $-17.5 \le t \le 22.5$ N, and $\norm{\vf}^2 = \norm{\vf^+}^2 + 4t^2$.

The rows of $M$ are the force balance and the moment balances about the two horizontal axes; leg $i$ at $(x_i, y_i)$ contributes $f_i$, $f_i x_i$ and $f_i y_i$. The rows are perpendicular with length 2, so $MM^\T = 4I$ and $\vf^+ = \tfrac14 M^\T\vr$: $f_i^+ = (100 + 20x_i + 10y_i)/4$, which gives $130/4$, $90/4$, $70/4$, $110/4$ for the legs at $(1, 1), (-1, 1), (-1, -1), (1, -1)$. The null vector $(1, -1, 1, -1)$ is the squeeze of slide gram-psd. Rigid-body statics cannot choose among the solutions; a real table picks one through the stiffness of its legs and floor (deck 13, statical indeterminacy; deck 13). At $t = 10$ the forces are $(42.5, 12.5, 27.5, 17.5)$ N with norm $\sqrt{2625 + 400} = 55.0$ N. Outside $[-17.5, 22.5]$ some leg must pull on the floor, which a table cannot do.

Pseudoinverse from the SVD

  • $A^+ = V_r\Sigma_r^{-1}U_r^\T = \sum_{i \le r}\tfrac{1}{\sigma_i}\vv_i\vu_i^\T$ (first $r$ singular vectors).
  • $\vx^+ = A^+\vb$ is the least-squares solution of smallest norm. It is $A^{-1}\vb$, $(A^\T A)^{-1}A^\T\vb$ or $A^\T(AA^\T)^{-1}\vb$ when those exist.
  • $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = 5\,\vu\vu^\T$, $\vu = (1, 2)/\sqrt5$, $\sigma = (5, 0)$: $A^+ = \tfrac15\vu\vu^\T = \tfrac1{25}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.
  • $\vb = (1, 1)$: $\vx^+ = (0.12, 0.24)$, and $A\vx^+ = (0.6, 1.2)$ is the projection of $\vb$ onto $\Col(A)$.

Tikhonov: $(A^\T A + \mu I)^{-1}A^\T\vb$ is $(0.1154, 0.2308)$ at $\mu = 1$ and $(0.1200, 0.2399)$ at $\mu = 0.01$; it tends to $\vx^+$ as $\mu \to 0$.

The pseudoinverse undoes $A$ where it can: it maps $\vu_i$ back to $\vv_i/\sigma_i$ for every nonzero singular value, and sends the directions $A$ cannot reach to zero. The result solves both problems of this part at once. Of all $\vx$, $A\vx^+$ is the point of $\Col(A)$ closest to $\vb$ (least squares); of all $\vx$ with that closest image, $\vx^+$ is the shortest (it has no $\Null(A)$ part). For the rank-one example, the least-squares solutions are the line $x_1 + 2x_2 = 0.6$, and the shortest point of it is $0.6\,(1, 2)/5 = (0.12, 0.24)$. Hou and Mason's $\cond(A) = \norm{A}_2\norm{A^\dagger}_2$ uses the same matrix, written $A^\dagger$. Tikhonov regularization (tex lines 1259-1266) solves $\min \norm{A\vx - \vb}^2 + \mu\norm{\vx}^2$: adding $\mu I$ to $A^\T A$ makes it PD even when $A$ is rank-deficient, at the price of a slightly shorter, biased answer. In inverse kinematics this is damped least squares. As $\mu \to 0$ the solution tends to $\vx^+$: at $\mu = 0.1$ it is $(0.1195, 0.2390)$ and at $\mu = 0.001$ $(0.1200, 0.2400)$.

Block elimination and the Schur complement

$\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ u \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$: $S = 4 - 2\cdot 2/2 = 2$, $Sx = 6 - 2\cdot 2/2 = 4$, so $x = 2$ and $u = (2 - 2\cdot 2)/2 = -1$.

$\begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ B^\T & C \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} \vx \\ \vu \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \vf \\ \vg \end{bmatrix}$ with $C$ invertible.

  1. Second block row: $B^\T\vx + C\vu = \vg$, so $\vu = C^{-1}(\vg - B^\T\vx)$.
  2. Substitute into the first: $A\vx + BC^{-1}(\vg - B^\T\vx) = \vf$.
  3. Collect $\vx$: $(A - BC^{-1}B^\T)\,\vx = \vf - BC^{-1}\vg$.
$S = A - BC^{-1}B^\T$ is the Schur complement of $C$.

Used for KKT systems (deck 06), the Riccati recursion (deck 08) and the iLQR backward pass (deck 09).

Block elimination is Gaussian elimination with matrices in place of numbers: solve the second block row for $\vu$, substitute, and a smaller system in $\vx$ alone remains. Its matrix $S$ is the Schur complement. Read with scalar blocks $a, b, c$, the formula is $s = a - b^2/c$, the pivot left after eliminating one variable: for the example, subtracting $2/2 = 1$ times row 2 from row 1 gives $(4 - 2)x = 6 - 2$, the same $2x = 4$. Check of the solution: $4\cdot 2 + 2\cdot(-1) = 6$ and $2\cdot 2 + 2\cdot(-1) = 2$. Two facts used later: $\det K = \det C\cdot\det S$, so $K$ is invertible when $C$ and $S$ are; and for symmetric $K$ with $C \succ 0$, $K \succ 0$ exactly when $S \succ 0$ (next slide). In the $2\times 2$ Cholesky factorization, $L_{22}^2 = a_{22} - a_{21}^2/a_{11}$ is the Schur complement of $a_{11}$. The KKT matrix of an equality-constrained QP (deck 06) has the block form with $C = 0$, so it is eliminated the other way round, through the Schur complement $-BA^{-1}B^\T$ of $A$.

Minimizing a quadratic over some of its variables

  • $f(x, u) = \tfrac12(ax^2 + 2bxu + cu^2)$. For $c > 0$, complete the square in $u$: $f = \tfrac12 c\bigl(u + \tfrac bc x\bigr)^2 + \tfrac12\bigl(a - \tfrac{b^2}{c}\bigr)x^2$.
  • Minimum over $u$: $u^* = -\tfrac bc x$, value $\tfrac12 s x^2$ with $s = a - b^2/c$, the Schur complement of $c$.
  • $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{bmatrix} \succ 0$ exactly when $c > 0$ and $s > 0$. $a = 4$, $c = 2$: $b = 2$ gives $s = 2$, gain $-1$; $b = 3$ gives $s = -0.5$.
$c > 0$ is not enough. $a = 0$, $b = 1$, $c = 1$ gives $s = -1$: the minimum over $u$ is $-\tfrac12x^2$.

Expanding the completed square checks it: $\tfrac12c(u + \tfrac bcx)^2 = \tfrac12cu^2 + bxu + \tfrac12\tfrac{b^2}{c}x^2$, and adding $\tfrac12(a - \tfrac{b^2}{c})x^2$ gives $f$. The first term is at least 0 and is 0 exactly at $u = -\tfrac bcx$, so for each fixed $x$ the best $u$ is a linear function of $x$ (a feedback gain $-b/c$) and the best value is a quadratic in $x$ alone with coefficient $s$. This is the tex's statement (line 1284): minimizing a quadratic over a subset of variables leaves the Schur complement in the rest. In LQR and iLQR, $x$ is the state and $u$ the control; the backward pass minimizes out $u$ at each time step, and $V_{xx} = Q_{xx} - Q_{xu}Q_{uu}^{-1}Q_{ux}$ is this $s$. The warning box corrects the tex (line 1298-1300), which says $Q_{uu} \succ 0$ preserves $V_{xx} \succ 0$: it needs the whole block matrix PD, or $Q_{xx}$ large enough. On the purple line the contours are tangent to vertical lines, which is the condition $\partial f/\partial u = 0$. Set $c \le 0$ to see that no minimum over $u$ exists.
