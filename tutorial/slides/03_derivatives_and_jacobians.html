Part 1

Derivatives of functions of one variable

Slopes of the quartic $J(z) = z^4 - 4z^2 + z + 4$, the rules that compute them, curvature, and polynomial models of the quartic near a point.

Secant lines and the tangent line of the quartic

  • $J(1) = 2$ and $J(1.5) = 1.5625$.
  • The secant line through these two points has slope $\dfrac{1.5625 - 2}{0.5} = -0.875$.
  • Shrink $h$: the secant turns toward the tangent line, which touches the graph at $z_0$ with slope $-3$.


The slope of a line is rise over run. A curve has no single slope, but the straight line through two of its points does: the secant through $(z_0, J(z_0))$ and $(z_0 + h, J(z_0 + h))$ has slope $\big(J(z_0 + h) - J(z_0)\big)/h$, the average rate of change of $J$ over the interval. With $z_0 = 1$ and $h = 0.5$ this is $-0.875$. As $h$ shrinks toward 0 from either side, the second point slides toward the first and the secant settles on one line, the tangent line, whose slope is the derivative $J'(z_0)$. At $z_0 = 1$ that slope is $-3$ (next slide). The slope carries units: if $z$ were in meters and $J$ in joules, $J'$ would be in joules per meter. The value $h = 0$ itself gives $0/0$, which is why the derivative is defined by a limit instead of by plugging in $h = 0$. Drag $z_0$ to the three places where the tangent is flat; deck 05 looks for exactly those points.

Notation: $J'(z) = \dfrac{\dd J}{\dd z}$, the derivative of $J$ with respect to $z$.

The limit means: the difference quotient can be made as close to $J'(z_0)$ as we like by taking every $h \ne 0$ close enough to 0, on both sides. The table shows this for the quartic at $z_0 = 1$; the gap to $-3$ is exactly $2h + 4h^2 + h^3$, which the slide taylor-polynomials explains. For small $h$ the $2h$ term dominates, so the gap is about $2h$ and falls tenfold when $h$ does. For $z^2$ the algebra is exact: after cancelling $z^2$, every remaining term has a factor $h$, the division removes one factor, and the $h$ that is left goes to 0. A function can fail to have a derivative: for $\abs{z}$ at $z = 0$ the quotient is $+1$ for $h > 0$ and $-1$ for $h < 0$, so it has no single limit, and the graph has a corner. Every function in this deck is smooth (it has as many derivatives as we need). Leibniz's notation $\dd J/\dd z$ reads as "a small change in $J$ per small change in $z$"; Lagrange's $J'(z)$ is shorter. The two mean the same.

Derivation of the power rule

Claim: $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd z} z^n = n z^{n-1}$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$

  1. Binomial expansion: $(z + h)^n = z^n + n z^{n-1}h + \binom{n}{2} z^{n-2}h^2 + \dots + h^n$.
  2. Subtract $z^n$: every remaining term has at least one factor $h$.
  3. Divide by $h$: $n z^{n-1} + \binom{n}{2} z^{n-2}h + \dots + h^{n-1}$.
  4. Let $h \to 0$: every term after the first carries a factor $h$ and vanishes, leaving $n z^{n-1}$.
Example. $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd z}z^3 = 3z^2$, so the slope at $z = 2$ is $12$. Check: $(2.001^3 - 2^3)/0.001 = 12.006$.
For $n = 3$ the expansion is $(z + h)^3 = z^3 + 3z^2h + 3zh^2 + h^3$, so the difference quotient is $3z^2 + 3zh + h^2$, which tends to $3z^2$. The coefficient $\binom{n}{2} = n(n-1)/2$ is a binomial coefficient; its value does not matter here, only that the term carries $h^2$. Two special cases: $n = 1$ gives $\frac{\dd}{\dd z}z = 1$ (a line of slope 1), and a constant ($z^0$) has derivative 0 because it does not change. The rule holds for every real exponent, for example $\frac{\dd}{\dd z}z^{-1} = -z^{-2}$, but this deck only needs whole numbers. The check in the example is a forward difference with $h = 0.001$; its error $0.006$ is about $h$ times the curvature term, the pattern Part 4 of this deck studies.

The sum rule holds because the difference quotient of $f + g$ is the sum of the two difference quotients, and the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits. With the power rule, any polynomial can be differentiated term by term: $4z^3$ from $z^4$, $-8z$ from $-4z^2$, $1$ from $z$, and $0$ from the constant 4. The product rule

$(fg)' = f'g + fg'$.

  • A rectangle has sides $f(z)$ and $g(z)$. When $z$ grows by $h$, the sides grow by $\Delta f$ and $\Delta g$.
  • New area minus old: $g\,\Delta f + f\,\Delta g + \Delta f\,\Delta g$.
  • Divide by $h$ and let $h \to 0$: $\Delta f/h \to f'$, $\Delta g/h \to g'$, and the corner $\Delta f\,\Delta g/h \approx f' g' h \to 0$.
  • Check: $J = z^2(z^2 - 4) + z + 4$ gives $J' = 2z(z^2 - 4) + z^2\cdot 2z + 1$; at $z = 1$: $-6 + 2 + 1 = -3$.
The algebra behind the picture: $f(z+h)g(z+h) - f(z)g(z) = \big(f(z+h) - f(z)\big)g(z+h) + f(z)\big(g(z+h) - g(z)\big)$, which is the old area subtracted from the new one, split into a strip of width $\Delta f$ (including the corner) and a strip of height $\Delta g$. Divide by $h$. The first term tends to $f'(z)g(z)$ because $g(z+h) \to g(z)$ (a differentiable function is continuous), and the second tends to $f(z)g'(z)$. The check writes the quartic as a product plus a line and recovers $J'(1) = -3$. The product rule reappears three times in the series: in the proof that the gradient is perpendicular to a circle's radius (next slides), in the second derivatives of compositions that deck 09 needs for the DDP Q-function (tex lines 1829-1833), and in deck 17, where differentiating $R^\T R = I$ shows that $R^\T\dot R$ is skew-symmetric.

The chain rule for a composition

For $J(z) = g(f(z))$: $\quad\dfrac{\dd J}{\dd z} = g'(f(z))\,f'(z)$.

  1. The quartic is $J(z) = (z^2 - 2)^2 + z$. Inner function $f(z) = z^2 - 2$, $f'(z) = 2z$. Outer function $g(y) = y^2$, $g'(y) = 2y$.
  2. $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd z}(z^2 - 2)^2 = 2(z^2 - 2)\cdot 2z$, so $J'(z) = 4z(z^2 - 2) + 1 = 4z^3 - 8z + 1$.
  3. At $z = 1$: $f = -1$, $g'(f) = -2$, $f' = 2$, and $J'(1) = (-2)(2) + 1 = -3$.
Rates multiply: $y = f(z)$ changes $f'(z)$ times as fast as $z$, and $g(y)$ changes $g'(y)$ times as fast as $y$, so $g(f(z))$ changes $g'(y)\,f'(z)$ times as fast as $z$.
Expanding $(z^2 - 2)^2 = z^4 - 4z^2 + 4$ confirms that this is the same quartic. The chain rule follows from the definition by splitting the difference quotient: $\frac{g(f(z+h)) - g(f(z))}{h} = \frac{g(f(z+h)) - g(f(z))}{f(z+h) - f(z)}\cdot\frac{f(z+h) - f(z)}{h}$. As $h \to 0$, the second factor tends to $f'(z)$ and the first, a difference quotient of $g$ at $y = f(z)$ with step $f(z+h) - f(z) \to 0$, tends to $g'(f(z))$. (The split fails when $f(z+h) = f(z)$ for small $h$; the first-order argument on the slide chain-rule-jacobians avoids the division and covers that case.) The chain rule is the most used rule in the series: it differentiates a rotation angle that changes in time (next slide), a cost evaluated along a trajectory (deck 09), and a constraint that holds along a motion (slide constraint-time-derivative).

Derivatives of sine, cosine, and a planar rotation

  • $\vp(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ moves on the unit circle; with $\theta$ in radians the arc length is $\theta$, so the speed is 1.
  • The velocity is tangent to the circle: $\vp'(\theta) = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$. So $\cos' = -\sin$ and $\sin' = \cos$.
  • Entry by entry, $R(\theta) = \begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{bmatrix}$ has $\dfrac{\dd R}{\dd\theta} = \begin{bmatrix}-\sin\theta & -\cos\theta\\ \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\end{bmatrix} = R(\theta)\,S$, $S = \begin{bmatrix}0 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$.
  • In time (chain rule): $\dot R = R\,S\,\dot\theta$, with angular velocity $\omega = \dot\theta$.
Why the velocity is perpendicular to the radius: $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$ for every $\theta$, and differentiating both sides with the chain rule gives $2\cos\theta\,\cos'\theta + 2\sin\theta\,\sin'\theta = 0$, that is $\vp\cdot\vp' = 0$ (a dot product, deck 01's dot-product). The velocity has length 1 because an arc of angle $\Delta\theta$ radians on a unit circle has length $\Delta\theta$. The two facts fix $\vp' = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$, the radius turned by $+90°$. In degrees the derivatives pick up a factor $\pi/180$, which is why calculus uses radians. The readout checks both rules with a central difference of step $10^{-5}$ (Part 4). $S$ is the $90°$ rotation of deck 01's slide linearity; $R(\theta)S$ rotates by $\theta + 90°$, which is where each column of $R$ moves as $\theta$ grows. Deck 17 builds the 3D version, $\dot R = R\,[\vomega]_\times$ with a skew-symmetric matrix of the angular velocity; the planar $S$ is that matrix for a unit rotation rate about the $z$-axis.

The second derivative as curvature

  • $J''(z) = 12z^2 - 8$ is the slope of $J'$.
  • $J'' > 0$: the slope increases and the graph bends up. $J'' < 0$: it bends down. The bend changes at the inflection points $z = \pm\sqrt{2/3} = \pm 0.8165$.
  • $J' = 0$ at $z = -1.473$, $0.126$, $1.347$. There $J'' = 18.04$, $-7.81$, $13.77$: a minimum, a maximum, a minimum.
The three panels show $J$, $J'$ and $J''$ over the same $z$. Where $J'' > 0$ the slope $J'$ is increasing, so the tangent line turns counter-clockwise as $z$ grows and the graph lies above its tangent lines (it bends up, or is locally convex); where $J'' < 0$ it bends down. A point with $J' = 0$ is a stationary point. If $J'' > 0$ there, the graph bends up on both sides and the point is a local minimum; if $J'' < 0$, a local maximum. This is the second-derivative test; the slide taylor-polynomials shows why it works. With $J'' = 0$ the test says nothing: $z^4$ has a minimum at 0 and $z^3$ has neither. The roots of the cubic $J'$ need a numerical method; d03_stationary_points uses Newton's method on $J'$ (deck 05). The values: $J(-1.473) = -1.444$ (the global minimum), $J(0.126) = 4.063$, $J(1.347) = 1.381$. The tex (line 257) gives $J(-1.47) \approx 0.26$ and $J(1.35) \approx 1.28$; both are wrong and recorded in the notebook's errata cell.

Taylor polynomials of the quartic

Goal: a polynomial in $\delta = z - z_0$ that has the value, slope, curvature, … of $J$ at $z_0$.

  1. $T(\delta) = c_0 + c_1\delta + c_2\delta^2 + c_3\delta^3 + c_4\delta^4$ has $T^{(k)}(0) = k!\,c_k$. Matching $T^{(k)}(0) = J^{(k)}(z_0)$ gives $c_k = J^{(k)}(z_0)/k!$.
  2. At $z_0 = 1$: $J = 2$, $J' = -3$, $J'' = 4$, $J''' = 24$, $J'''' = 24$, so $$J(1 + \delta) = 2 - 3\delta + 2\delta^2 + 4\delta^3 + \delta^4.$$
  3. Keep two terms: the linear model $2 - 3\delta$ (the tangent line). Keep three: the quadratic model $2 - 3\delta + 2\delta^2$.
$\delta = 0.1$: $J(1.1) = 1.7241$; linear model $1.7$; quadratic model $1.72$.
Step 1: differentiating $\delta^k$ $k$ times gives $k(k-1)\cdots 1 = k!$, a constant. Lower powers have already become 0 after fewer than $k$ derivatives, and higher powers still carry a factor $\delta$ that is 0 at $\delta = 0$. So the $k$-th derivative of $T$ at 0 picks out $k!\,c_k$ alone. For a polynomial of degree 4 the degree-4 Taylor polynomial is the function itself, only rewritten around $z_0$; expanding $(1+\delta)^4 - 4(1+\delta)^2 + (1+\delta) + 4$ gives the same five coefficients. For $\sin z$ or $e^z$ the series never stops and truncating it leaves an error. Two uses on earlier slides: the secant error at $z_0 = 1$ is $\big(J(1+h) - 2 + 3h\big)/h = 2h + 4h^2 + h^3$ (slide derivative-as-limit), and at a stationary point the linear term vanishes, so near it $J(z_0 + \delta) \approx J(z_0) + \tfrac12 J''(z_0)\delta^2$, which is above $J(z_0)$ on both sides when $J'' > 0$: the second-derivative test.

Linear and quadratic models of the quartic

  • The linear model is the tangent line. The quadratic model is the parabola with the same value, slope and curvature at $z_0$.
  • At $z_0 = 1$ the quadratic model $2 - 3\delta + 2\delta^2$ opens upward; its minimum is at $\delta = 0.75$.
  • At $z_0 = 0$ it is $4 + \delta - 4\delta^2$, which opens downward.

The models are polynomials in $\delta = z - z_0$ with the coefficients $c_k = J^{(k)}(z_0)/k!$ of the previous slide, computed by d03_taylor_poly. Near $z_0$ each model hugs the quartic; farther away it drifts off, the linear model first. The quadratic model's stationary point solves $c_1 + 2c_2\delta = 0$, that is $\delta = -J'(z_0)/J''(z_0)$. Newton's method (deck 05) jumps there. From $z_0 = 1$ the jump is $\delta = 0.75$, to $z = 1.75$, past the minimum at $1.347$. From $z_0 = 0$ the model opens downward ($J''(0) = -8$), so its stationary point $\delta = 1/8$ is a maximum, and Newton's method heads for the local maximum of $J$ at $0.126$. Gradient descent uses only the linear model, which has no minimum; it steps a fixed distance downhill along it. The tex insight box (lines 157-175) names these two choices as the difference between the two methods. The quartic model (degree 4) lies exactly on $J$.

Gradients and Hessians

Functions of several variables: slices, the gradient and its level curves, curvature along a line, the multivariable Taylor expansion, and the gradients of linear, quadratic and least-squares functions.

A function of two variables as a surface and a contour map

$J(z_1, z_2) = z_1^4 - 4z_1^2 + z_1 + 4 + 2(z_2 - z_1)^2$: the quartic along $z_1$ plus a valley term, zero on the line $z_2 = z_1$. $J(0, 1) = 6$, $J(1, 0) = 4$. Minima: $(-1.473, -1.473)$ with $J = -1.444$ and $(1.347, 1.347)$ with $J = 1.381$. Saddle: $(0.126, 0.126)$, $J = 4.063$.

A function of two variables takes a point $\vz = (z_1, z_2)$ of the plane and returns a number. Two pictures show it. The surface (left) plots the height $J$ above each point. The contour map (right) is a topographic map: each curve joins the points where $J$ has one value, the level curve $J(\vz) = c$, here for $c = -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 13, 17$. Contours close together mean a steep slope. On the line $z_2 = z_1$ the valley term vanishes and $J$ is the quartic of Part 1, so the two minima and the saddle lie on that line at the stationary points of the quartic; off the line $J$ rises as $2(z_2 - z_1)^2$. The saddle is a maximum along the line and a minimum across it, and the contour map shows it as a crossing of level curves. Partial derivatives as slopes of slices

$\dfrac{\partial J}{\partial z_1}$: hold $z_2$ fixed and differentiate in $z_1$. $\dfrac{\partial J}{\partial z_2}$: hold $z_1$ fixed. For the valley function, $\dfrac{\partial J}{\partial z_1} = 4z_1^3 - 8z_1 + 1 - 4(z_2 - z_1)$ and $\dfrac{\partial J}{\partial z_2} = 4(z_2 - z_1)$; at $(0, 1)$ they are $-3$ and $4$.

A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative of a one-variable slice. Freezing $z_2 = 1$ leaves $t \mapsto J(t, 1) = t^4 - 4t^2 + t + 4 + 2(1 - t)^2$, the red curve in the upper right panel; its slope at $t = 0$ is $\partial J/\partial z_1(0, 1)$. Freezing $z_1 = 0$ leaves $t \mapsto J(0, t) = 4 + 2t^2$, the blue curve; its slope at $t = 1$ is $4t = 4$. The rules of Part 1 apply with the frozen variable treated as a constant. The term $2(z_2 - z_1)^2$ needs the chain rule: in $z_1$ its derivative is $2\cdot 2(z_2 - z_1)\cdot(-1) = -4(z_2 - z_1)$, and in $z_2$ it is $+4(z_2 - z_1)$. The readout checks both partial derivatives with central differences of step $10^{-6}$ (d03_partial_slices); they agree to about $10^{-10}$. The symbol $\partial$ (a rounded d) marks a derivative with the other variables held fixed.

The gradient vector

  • The gradient collects the partial derivatives: $\nabla J(\vz) = \left(\dfrac{\partial J}{\partial z_1},\ \dfrac{\partial J}{\partial z_2}\right)$. In $\R^n$ it has $n$ entries.
  • Valley function: $\nabla J = \big(4z_1^3 - 8z_1 + 1 - 4(z_2 - z_1),\ 4(z_2 - z_1)\big)$.
  • $\nabla J(0, 1) = (-3, 4)$, length 5. $\nabla J(1, 0) = (1, -4)$. $\nabla J(1, 1) = (-3, 0)$.
  • The arrows point uphill, cross the contours at right angles, and are longest where the contours crowd.
The gradient is a vector in the same space as $\vz$, and each point has its own gradient, so the gradient is a vector field: an arrow attached to every point of the plane. The tex (line 1305) defines it by $(\nabla J)_i = \partial J/\partial z_i$. The grid arrows are scaled by one common factor so the longest fits in its cell; their lengths compare with each other but not with the axes. At a minimum, a maximum or a saddle every partial derivative is zero, so the arrows shrink to nothing near the three stationary points on the line $z_2 = z_1$. The next three slides prove the three observations in the last bullet: the gradient gives the rate of change in every direction, it points in the direction of fastest increase, and it is perpendicular to the level curve through its base point. Hand check at $(1, 1)$: $4 - 8 + 1 - 0 = -3$ and $4(1 - 1) = 0$, so on the line $z_2 = z_1$ the gradient points along $z_1$.

Directional derivative as the dot product of gradient and direction

Move from $\vz$ along a unit vector $\vd$: $g(t) = J(\vz + t\vd)$. The rate of change of $J$ in direction $\vd$ is $g'(0)$.

  1. $g(t) = J(z_1 + t d_1,\ z_2 + t d_2)$: both arguments move with $t$.
  2. Change one argument at a time: $g'(0) = \dfrac{\partial J}{\partial z_1}d_1 + \dfrac{\partial J}{\partial z_2}d_2$.
  3. $g'(0) = \nabla J(\vz)\cdot\vd$.

At $(0, 1)$ with $\vd = (0.6, 0.8)$: $(-3)(0.6) + (4)(0.8) = 1.4$.

Step 2 splits the change of $J$ into two moves, first along $z_1$, then along $z_2$: $J(z_1 + td_1, z_2 + td_2) - J(z_1, z_2) = \big[J(z_1 + td_1, z_2 + td_2) - J(z_1, z_2 + td_2)\big] + \big[J(z_1, z_2 + td_2) - J(z_1, z_2)\big]$. Divided by $t$, the first bracket is a difference quotient in $z_1$ with step $td_1$ and tends to $\frac{\partial J}{\partial z_1}d_1$ (this needs the partial derivatives to be continuous, which holds for every function in this deck); the second tends to $\frac{\partial J}{\partial z_2}d_2$. The sum of products is the dot product of deck 01 (dot-product). Special cases: $\vd = (1, 0)$ gives $\partial J/\partial z_1 = -3$ and $\vd = (0, 1)$ gives $\partial J/\partial z_2 = 4$ at $(0, 1)$. The readout compares $\nabla J\cdot\vd$ with the central difference $\big(J(\vz + t\vd) - J(\vz - t\vd)\big)/2t$ at $t = 10^{-6}$. For a direction that is not a unit vector the same formula gives the rate per unit of $t$, which is $\norm{\vd}$ times the rate per unit length.

Steepest ascent and descent directions

  • For a unit $\vd$: $\nabla J\cdot\vd = \norm{\nabla J}\cos\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between $\vd$ and $\nabla J$.
  • Largest, $+\norm{\nabla J}$, at $\alpha = 0$: $\vd = \nabla J/\norm{\nabla J}$, the steepest ascent direction.
  • Smallest, $-\norm{\nabla J}$, at $\vd = -\nabla J/\norm{\nabla J}$: steepest descent. Gradient descent (deck 05) steps this way.
  • Zero at $\alpha = \pm 90^\circ$: along the level curve.
  • At $(0, 1)$: ascent at $126.87^\circ$ with rate 5; descent at $-53.13^\circ$; level at $36.87^\circ$ and $-143.13^\circ$.
The first bullet is the angle form of the dot product, $\va\cdot\vb = \norm{\va}\norm{\vb}\cos\alpha$, from deck 01 (dot-product-angle), with $\norm{\vd} = 1$. The cosine ranges over $[-1, 1]$, so the directional derivative ranges over $[-\norm{\nabla J}, \norm{\nabla J}]$, and the curve in the figure is $5\cos(\varphi - 126.87^\circ)$. The tex states the same argument (lines 224-236) and adds that the result holds for step lengths measured in the Euclidean norm. With the length of a step measured as $\sqrt{\vdelta^\T H\vdelta}$ instead, the steepest direction becomes $-H^{-1}\nabla J$, Newton's direction; the tex omits that this needs $H$ positive definite (deck 02, positive-definite). At $(0, 1)$ the valley Hessian is indefinite, so there it fails; the errata cell of the notebook records it. Rescaling one coordinate changes which direction is steepest, which is why gradient descent crawls on ill-conditioned problems (deck 05).

Orthogonality of the gradient to level curves

  1. A level curve $\vz(s)$ keeps $J(\vz(s)) = c$ for every $s$.
  2. Differentiate in $s$ with the previous two slides: $\nabla J(\vz(s))\cdot\vz'(s) = 0$.
  3. So the tangent $\vz'(s)$ is perpendicular to $\nabla J$.

At $(0, 1)$: $J = 6$, $\nabla J = (-3, 4)$, unit tangent $(-0.8, -0.6)$, and $(-3)(-0.8) + (4)(-0.6) = 0$. A step of $0.001$ along the tangent changes $J$ by $-2.5\times 10^{-6}$, a second-order change.

Step 2 is the directional derivative with the direction $\vz'(s)$: the rate of change of $J$ along the curve is $\nabla J\cdot\vz'(s)$, and $J$ does not change along a level curve. A level curve passes through every point where $\nabla J \ne \vzero$ (the implicit function theorem, used here without proof); at the saddle $(0.126, 0.126)$ two level curves cross and the gradient is zero. The tangent in the readout is $\nabla J$ turned by $+90°$ and divided by its length. Walking $0.001$ along the tangent leaves $J$ unchanged to first order; the change that remains is $\tfrac12(0.001)^2\,\vt^\T H\vt = -2.48\times 10^{-6}$, the curvature term of the slide multivariable-taylor. That is the Lagrange multiplier condition.

The Hessian matrix of second partial derivatives

$$\nabla^2 J(\vz) = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial^2 J}{\partial z_1^2} & \dfrac{\partial^2 J}{\partial z_1\,\partial z_2}\\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial^2 J}{\partial z_2\,\partial z_1} & \dfrac{\partial^2 J}{\partial z_2^2}\end{bmatrix},\qquad \left(\nabla^2 J\right)_{ij} = \frac{\partial}{\partial z_i}\frac{\partial J}{\partial z_j}.$$
  1. Differentiate $\dfrac{\partial J}{\partial z_1} = 4z_1^3 - 8z_1 + 1 - 4(z_2 - z_1)$: in $z_1$, $12z_1^2 - 8 + 4 = 12z_1^2 - 4$; in $z_2$, $-4$.
  2. Differentiate $\dfrac{\partial J}{\partial z_2} = 4(z_2 - z_1)$: in $z_1$, $-4$; in $z_2$, $4$.
  3. $\nabla^2 J = \begin{bmatrix} 12z_1^2 - 4 & -4\\ -4 & 4\end{bmatrix}$. At $(1, 0)$: $\begin{bmatrix} 8 & -4\\ -4 & 4\end{bmatrix}$. At $(0, 1)$: $\begin{bmatrix} -4 & -4\\ -4 & 4\end{bmatrix}$.

The two mixed partial derivatives are equal: the Hessian is symmetric.

The Hessian (written $\nabla^2 J$, or $H$ when the point is clear) is the $n\times n$ matrix of all second partial derivatives, as the tex defines it at line 1306. Row $i$ is the gradient of $\partial J/\partial z_i$, so the Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient (Part 3). The order of differentiation does not matter when the second partial derivatives are continuous (Schwarz's theorem, also called Clairaut's), which makes the Hessian symmetric; here both mixed partials are $-4$. A symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors (deck 02, spectral-theorem), and the next slide reads them as the largest and smallest curvature. This Hessian does not depend on $z_2$, because $J$ is quadratic in $z_2$. The function d03_valley_hess returns it at any point.

Curvature along a line and the Hessian eigenvalues

Along $g(t) = J(\vz + t\vd)$: $g'(t) = \nabla J(\vz + t\vd)\cdot\vd$ and, differentiating each entry of $\nabla J$ the same way, $g''(0) = \vd^\T\nabla^2 J(\vz)\,\vd$.

Step by step: $g'(t) = \sum_j \frac{\partial J}{\partial z_j}(\vz + t\vd)\,d_j$ by the previous slides. Each $\partial J/\partial z_j$ is itself a function of two variables, and its rate of change along $\vd$ is $\sum_i \frac{\partial^2 J}{\partial z_i\partial z_j}d_i$. So $g''(0) = \sum_i\sum_j d_i H_{ij} d_j = \vd^\T H\vd$. At $(1, 0)$, where $H = [8, -4; -4, 4]$, the curvature is $8$ at $0°$, $2$ at $45°$, $4$ at $90°$ and $10$ at $135°$. Over all unit directions the largest and smallest values of $\vd^\T H\vd$ are the eigenvalues of $H$, $10.47$ along $(0.851, -0.526)$ and $1.53$ along $(0.526, 0.851)$ (deck 02, quadratic-forms). Both are positive, so every line through $(1, 0)$ curves up. At $(0, 1)$ the eigenvalues are $\pm 5.66$: some lines curve up and others down, the local shape of a saddle. Taylor expansion in several variables

  1. Restrict $J$ to the line from $\vz_0$ through $\vz_0 + \vdelta$: $g(t) = J(\vz_0 + t\vdelta)$, so $J(\vz_0 + \vdelta) = g(1)$.
  2. One-variable Taylor at $t = 0$: $g(1) \approx g(0) + g'(0) + \tfrac12 g''(0)$.
  3. Substitute $g'(0) = \nabla J(\vz_0)^\T\vdelta$ and $g''(0) = \vdelta^\T\nabla^2 J(\vz_0)\,\vdelta$:
$$J(\vz_0 + \vdelta) \approx J(\vz_0) + \nabla J(\vz_0)^\T\vdelta + \tfrac12\,\vdelta^\T\nabla^2 J(\vz_0)\,\vdelta.$$
At $\vz_0 = (1, 0)$, $\vdelta = (0.1, 0.1)$: $J = 3.7241$. Linear model: $4 + (1)(0.1) + (-4)(0.1) = 3.7$. Quadratic: $\vdelta^\T H\vdelta = 0.01(8 - 8 + 4) = 0.04$, so $3.7 + 0.02 = 3.72$.

Gradients of $\vb^\T\vx$ and $\vx^\T A\vx$

Linear. $f(\vx) = \vb^\T\vx = \sum_i b_i x_i$.

  • Only the term $i = k$ contains $x_k$: $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_k} = b_k$.
  • $\nabla(\vb^\T\vx) = \vb$. Example: $\vb = (2, -1)$, $f = 2x_1 - x_2$, $\nabla f = (2, -1)$ at every $\vx$.

Quadratic. $f(\vx) = \vx^\T A\vx = \sum_i\sum_j x_i A_{ij} x_j$.

  1. Terms with $i = k$: derivative $\sum_j A_{kj}x_j = (A\vx)_k$.
  2. Terms with $j = k$: derivative $\sum_i x_i A_{ik} = (A^\T\vx)_k$.
  3. $\nabla(\vx^\T A\vx) = (A + A^\T)\vx$, which is $2A\vx$ for symmetric $A$. Hessian: $A + A^\T$.
$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2\\ 0 & 3\end{bmatrix}$, $\vx = (1, 1)$: $f = x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2 = 6$, and by hand $\nabla f = (2x_1 + 2x_2,\ 2x_1 + 6x_2) = (4, 8)$ $= (A + A^\T)\vx$ with $A + A^\T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2\\ 2 & 6\end{bmatrix}$.
Both identities are the one-variable rules $\frac{\dd}{\dd x}(bx) = b$ and $\frac{\dd}{\dd x}(ax^2) = 2ax$ in several variables, proved component by component. In the double sum, $x_k$ appears in the terms of row $k$ ($i = k$) and of column $k$ ($j = k$); the diagonal term $A_{kk}x_k^2$ belongs to both, and its derivative $2A_{kk}x_k$ is split between steps 1 and 2, one $A_{kk}x_k$ each, so nothing is counted twice. Only the symmetric part of $A$ matters: $\vx^\T A\vx = \vx^\T\tfrac12(A + A^\T)\vx$, since $\vx^\T A\vx$ is a number and equals its own transpose $\vx^\T A^\T\vx$. The Hessian follows by applying the linear identity to each entry of $(A + A^\T)\vx$. Deck 06 uses the linear identity on the constraint term of a Lagrangian: $\vnu^\T(A\vx - \vb) = (A^\T\vnu)^\T\vx - \vnu^\T\vb$ has gradient $A^\T\vnu$ in $\vx$. The functions d03_grad_linear and d03_grad_quadratic check the example with central differences.

Jacobians, linearization, and constraints

Vector-valued functions and their Jacobians, the chain rule as a product of matrices, linearized pendulum dynamics, and the velocity constraint $J_\Phi\dot q = 0$ of Hou and Mason.

The Jacobian matrix of a vector-valued function

$\vf:\R^n\to\R^m$ has $m$ component functions $f_1, \dots, f_m$ of $n$ variables. Its Jacobian is the $m\times n$ matrix $J_\vf = \dfrac{\partial\vf}{\partial\vx}$ with entries $\dfrac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}$: row $i$ is $\nabla f_i^\T$, column $j$ is the rate of change of $\vf$ along $x_j$. To first order, $\vf(\vx + \vdelta) \approx \vf(\vx) + J_\vf\,\vdelta$.

Two-link arm, $l_1 = l_2 = 1$ m, joint angles $\vtheta = (\theta_1, \theta_2)$: $\vp(\vtheta) = \big(\cos\theta_1 + \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2),\ \sin\theta_1 + \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)\big)$.

$$J_\vp = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\theta_1 - \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) & -\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)\\ \cos\theta_1 + \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) & \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)\end{bmatrix}$$

At $\vtheta = (0, 90^\circ)$: $\vp = (1, 1)$ m and $J_\vp = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -1\\ 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}$ m/rad.

The shape rule: one row per output, one column per input. With $m = 1$ the Jacobian is a single row, the gradient laid on its side ($\nabla f^\T$); with $n = 1$ it is a single column, the velocity of a curve. The first-order formula is the multivariable Taylor expansion of each component, cut after the linear term. The arm's tip is the sum of the two link vectors, each a rotated unit vector, and each entry of $J_\vp$ comes from the rules of Part 1: $\theta_1$ appears in both links, $\theta_2$ only in the second. Deck 01 (linearity) showed that column $j$ of a matrix is the image of $\ve_j$; here column $j$ is the tip velocity when joint $j$ turns at 1 rad/s with the other joint held still. At $(0, 90°)$ column 1 is $(-1, 1)$: turning the shoulder moves the tip $(1, 1)$ perpendicular to the line from the shoulder. Column 2 is $(-1, 0)$: turning the elbow at $(1, 0)$ moves the tip perpendicular to the second link. d03_arm_jacobian computes both.

The arm Jacobian as a linear map of joint velocities

  • Tip velocity by the chain rule in time: $\dot\vp = J_\vp(\vtheta)\,\dot\vtheta$.
  • The circle of unit joint rates maps to an ellipse (deck 02, svd-picture).
  • $\det J_\vp = l_1 l_2\sin\theta_2$: zero when the arm is straight or folded. The ellipse then flattens to a segment and the tip cannot move along the arm.
  • First-order check at $(0, 90^\circ)$, $\dd\vtheta = (0.01, 0.02)$ rad: true $\Delta\vp = (-0.030045, 0.009550)$ m, $J_\vp\,\dd\vtheta = (-0.03, 0.01)$ m. For $(0.1, 0.2)$ rad: $(-0.3005, 0.0552)$ vs $(-0.3, 0.1)$.
$\vp(\vtheta(t))$ is a composition of the arm map with the joint trajectory, and the chain rule of the next slide gives $\dot\vp = J_\vp\dot\vtheta$. For each $\vtheta$ the Jacobian is a $2\times 2$ matrix, a linear map from joint rates to tip velocities, and deck 02 showed that such a map sends the unit circle to an ellipse whose semi-axes are the singular values (listed in the readout). The determinant: $J_{11}J_{22} - J_{12}J_{21} = l_1l_2(\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)\cos\theta_1 - \sin\theta_1\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)) = l_1 l_2\sin\theta_2$. At $\theta_2 = 0$ both columns point perpendicular to the straight arm, so no joint motion moves the tip along it; the arm is at a singular configuration. Near it, the smallest singular value is small and a tip velocity along the arm needs very large joint rates (deck 02's condition number). The first-order check shows the linear prediction improving as the step shrinks: at $\dd\vtheta = (0.01, 0.02)$ the error is about $5\times 10^{-4}$ m, at ten times the step about $4.5\times 10^{-2}$ m, a hundred times larger, as an $O(\norm{\dd\vtheta}^2)$ error should be. Deck 17 uses the transpose of this matrix for forces, $\vtau = J^\T\vf$.

The chain rule as a product of Jacobians

$\vx\in\R^n \;\xrightarrow{\ \vf\ }\; \vy = \vf(\vx)\in\R^m \;\xrightarrow{\ \vg\ }\; \vw = \vg(\vy)\in\R^p$.

  1. $\vf(\vx + \vdelta) \approx \vf(\vx) + J_\vf\,\vdelta$, so $\vy$ moves by $\Delta\vy \approx J_\vf\vdelta$.
  2. $\vg(\vy + \Delta\vy) \approx \vg(\vy) + J_\vg\,\Delta\vy \approx \vg(\vy) + J_\vg J_\vf\,\vdelta$.
  3. $J_{\vg\circ\vf}(\vx) = J_\vg(\vf(\vx))\,J_\vf(\vx)$: sizes $(p\times m)(m\times n) = p\times n$.
Arm distance $w(\vtheta) = \tfrac12\norm{\vp(\vtheta) - \vp^\star}^2$, $\vp^\star = (0.5, 1.5)$, at $\vtheta = (0, 90^\circ)$: outer Jacobian $(\vp - \vp^\star)^\T = [0.5\ \ {-0.5}]$ ($1\times 2$), inner $J_\vp$ ($2\times 2$). Gradient $\nabla w = J_\vp^\T(\vp - \vp^\star) = (-1, -0.5)$.

Along a path $\vz(t)$: $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd t}J(\vz(t)) = \nabla J(\vz)^\T\dot\vz$.

Entry by entry, $\frac{\partial w_i}{\partial x_j} = \sum_k \frac{\partial g_i}{\partial y_k}\frac{\partial y_k}{\partial x_j}$: every path from $x_j$ to $w_i$ through an intermediate $y_k$ contributes the product of its two rates. Deck 01 showed that composing linear maps multiplies their matrices (composition); the chain rule says the first-order approximations compose the same way. The tex states the rule at lines 1316-1319. For a scalar output ($p = 1$) the Jacobian is a row, and the gradient is its transpose: $\nabla(g\circ\vf) = J_\vf^\T\nabla g$. In the example, $J_\vp^\T = [-1, 1; -1, 0]$ times $(0.5, -0.5)$ gives $(-1, -0.5)$, which d03_arm_distance_gradient confirms with central differences. Deck 09 uses the same identity for the DDP Q-function, $Q_\vx = c_\vx + \vf_\vx^\T V'_\vx$ (tex eq. Qx, line 1802). The path formula is the case $n = 1$: the path $t\mapsto\vz(t)$ has the column $\dot\vz$ as Jacobian. It differentiates $J$ along a trajectory and, on the slide constraint-time-derivative, a constraint along a motion.

Linearization of discrete-time dynamics into $A$ and $B$

Dynamics $\vx_{k+1} = \vf(\vx_k, \vu_k)$. Near a nominal state and control $(\bar\vx, \bar\vu)$: $\delta\vx_{k+1} \approx A\,\delta\vx_k + B\,\delta\vu_k$ with $A = \dfrac{\partial\vf}{\partial\vx}$, $B = \dfrac{\partial\vf}{\partial\vu}$ at $(\bar\vx, \bar\vu)$.

Pendulum: $\vx = (\theta, \omega)$, torque $u$, $m = 1$ kg, $l = 1$ m, $g = 9.81$ m/s$^2$, time step $h = 0.01$ s.

  1. $a = -\tfrac{g}{l}\sin\theta + \tfrac{u}{ml^2}$; $\ \omega_{k+1} = \omega_k + h a$; $\ \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + h\omega_{k+1} = \theta_k + h\omega_k + h^2 a$.
  2. $\dfrac{\partial a}{\partial\theta} = -\tfrac{g}{l}\cos\theta = -k$ and $\dfrac{\partial a}{\partial u} = \tfrac{1}{ml^2} = b$.
  3. $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - h^2k & h\\ -hk & 1\end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} h^2 b\\ hb\end{bmatrix}$.
  4. Hanging down ($\bar\theta = 0$, $k = 9.81$): $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.999019 & 0.01\\ -0.0981 & 1\end{bmatrix}$, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 0.0001\\ 0.01\end{bmatrix}$, $\det A = 1$.
Row 1 of $A$ differentiates $\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + h\omega_k + h^2 a$: $\partial/\partial\theta_k = 1 + h^2(-k)$ and $\partial/\partial\omega_k = h$. Row 2 differentiates $\omega_{k+1} = \omega_k + ha$: $-hk$ and 1. $B$ collects the derivatives in $u$. The step updates the velocity first and then moves the angle with the new velocity (semi-implicit Euler), which is the update of MuJoCo's default integrator, mjINT_EULER. Its $\det A = (1 - h^2k) + h^2k = 1$ for every $\theta$: the step preserves area in the $(\theta, \omega)$ plane, as the exact pendulum does. Upside down ($\bar\theta = \pi$) the sign of $k$ flips and $A = [1.000981, 0.01; 0.0981, 1]$. The nominal point need not be an equilibrium: along a trajectory $(\bar\vx_k, \bar\vu_k)$ each step has its own $A_k$ and $B_k$. Deck 08 designs LQR controllers for $\delta\vx_{k+1} = A\delta\vx_k + B\delta\vu_k$, and deck 09 (iLQR) relinearizes around each new trajectory. d03_pendulum_AB computes the matrices.

Linearized and nonlinear pendulum trajectories

  • Both start at rest, $\Delta\theta_0$ away from the nominal angle, with $u = 0$.
  • Hanging down, the largest gap over 2 s: $0.011^\circ$ from $5^\circ$, $0.73^\circ$ from $20^\circ$, $8.44^\circ$ from $45^\circ$, $71.0^\circ$ from $90^\circ$.
  • Upside down, the linear model grows like $e^{3.13t}$. From $1^\circ$ the gap is $0.011^\circ$ after 1 s and $69^\circ$ after 2 s.

The linear model replaces $\sin\theta$ by its tangent line at $\bar\theta$. Hanging down, $\sin\theta = \theta - \theta^3/6 + \dots$, so the dropped term is third order and the gap grows like the cube of the amplitude: from $5°$ to $20°$ the amplitude grows 4 times and the gap $0.011° \to 0.73°$ about $4^3 = 64$ times. Upside down, the eigenvalues of $A$ are about $1 \pm 0.0313$, so the linear model multiplies the deviation by about $1.0318$ per step, which is $e^{3.13t}$ with $3.13 \approx \sqrt{g/l}$. The true pendulum falls and swings through the bottom, and after 2 s it has nothing to do with the exponential prediction. The numbers come from d03_pendulum_rollouts (1 s windows for the upside-down figures in the notebook, 2 s on the plot). A linear model is only as good as the region the state stays in: LQR (deck 08) holds the pendulum up because the feedback keeps $\delta\vx$ small, and iLQR (deck 09) relinearizes along the current trajectory for the same reason.

Time derivative of a holonomic constraint

  • A constraint $\Phi(q) = 0$ holds at every instant of a motion $q(t)$.
  • Chain rule: $\dfrac{\dd}{\dd t}\Phi(q(t)) = J_\Phi(q)\,\dot q = 0$.
  • Bead on the unit circle: $\Phi(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - 1$, $J_\Phi = [\,2x\ \ 2y\,]$. At $(0.6, 0.8)$: $J_\Phi = [\,1.2\ \ 1.6\,]$.
  • $\dot q = (-0.8, 0.6)$: $J_\Phi\dot q = 0$, along the circle. $\dot q = (1, 0)$: $1.2$, off the circle.
  • Hou and Mason 2019, eq. 3: $J_\Phi(q)\,\dot q = J_\Phi(q)\,\Omega(q)\,v = 0$.
A holonomic constraint restricts the configuration $q$ itself, as an equation $\Phi(q) = 0$ (Hou 2019, eq. 2). If it holds at every time, its time derivative is zero, and the chain rule for a path (slide chain-rule-jacobians) turns that into a linear equation on the velocity: $J_\Phi\dot q = 0$, with $J_\Phi = \partial\Phi/\partial q$. The allowed velocities form the null space of $J_\Phi$ (deck 01, null-space). For the bead, $J_\Phi^\T = (2x, 2y)$ points along the radius and the allowed velocities are tangent to the circle. The same computation for a unit quaternion, $\Phi(q) = q^\T q - 1$, gives $2q^\T\dot q = 0$: the derivative of a unit quaternion is perpendicular to it (deck 17). Hou and Mason write $\dot q = \Omega(q)v$ with a generalized velocity $v$ (deck 17 builds $\Omega$), so the constraint becomes $Nv = 0$ with $N = J_\Phi\Omega$, their natural constraint (deck 21). A simulator that steps $q_{k+1} = q_k + h\dot q_k$ with $J_\Phi\dot q_k = 0$ still drifts off the circle: from $(0.6, 0.8)$ with $\dot q = (-0.8, 0.6)$ and $h = 0.01$, $\norm{q_1}^2 - 1 = h^2 = 10^{-4}$.

Velocity constraint of a block pivoting on its corner

  • $q = (x, y, \theta)$: centre and angle of a $0.2\times 0.1$ m block. Corner in the block frame: $\vc = (-a, -b)$, $a = 0.1$ m, $b = 0.05$ m.
  • Pinned corner: $\Phi(q) = \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y\end{bmatrix} + R(\theta)\vc = \vzero$ (2 equations).
  • $J_\Phi = \big[\, I_2 \ \big|\ R'(\theta)\vc\,\big]$ ($2\times 3$). At $\theta = 0$: $J_\Phi = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0.05\\ 0 & 1 & -0.1\end{bmatrix}$.
  • $\Null J_\Phi$ is spanned by $(-0.05, 0.1, 1)$: rotation about the corner. Sliding right, $(1, 0, 0)$, moves the corner at $(1, 0)$ m/s.

$\Phi(q)$ is the world position of the corner, the centre plus the rotated corner offset, minus the pin at the origin. Its Jacobian has the $2\times 2$ identity in the $(x, y)$ columns, because moving the centre moves the corner by the same amount, and $R'(\theta)\vc = R(\theta)S\vc$ in the $\theta$ column (slide sine-cosine-derivatives). At $\theta = 0$, $S\vc = (b, -a) = (0.05, -0.1)$; at $30°$ the column is $(0.0933, -0.0616)$ (d03_pivot_jacobian). $J_\Phi\dot q$ is the velocity of the corner point, so the constraint says that the corner does not move; a Jacobian that maps $\dot q$ to the velocity of a contact point is a contact Jacobian (deck 18). The null vector with $\dot\theta = 1$ moves the centre at $(-0.05, 0.1)$, perpendicular to the corner-to-centre vector $(0.1, 0.05)$: a pure rotation about the corner. Hou and Mason (2019, eqs. 31-33) write their sticking contacts in this form, a rotated body-frame point plus the body position equal to a fixed world point. For planar $(x, y, \theta)$ coordinates $\dot q$ is the generalized velocity, so $\Omega = I$ and $N = J_\Phi$. Deck 21 tilts a block about an edge with this constraint.

Part 4

Finite differences

Derivatives from function values alone: truncation error, roundoff, and the finite-difference Jacobians that MuJoCo and MuJoCo MPC use.

Forward and central difference formulas

  1. Taylor: $J(z \pm h) = J(z) \pm hJ'(z) + \tfrac{h^2}{2}J''(z) \pm \tfrac{h^3}{6}J'''(z) + \dots$
  2. Forward difference: $\dfrac{J(z + h) - J(z)}{h} = J'(z) + \tfrac{h}{2}J''(z) + \dots$: error $O(h)$.
  3. Central difference: subtract the two expansions; the even terms cancel. $\dfrac{J(z + h) - J(z - h)}{2h} = J'(z) + \tfrac{h^2}{6}J'''(z) + \dots$: error $O(h^2)$.
$z = 1$, $h = 0.1$, $J'(1) = -3$: $J(1.1) = 1.7241$, $J(1) = 2$, $J(0.9) = 2.3161$.
Forward: $(1.7241 - 2)/0.1 = -2.759$, error $0.241$.
Central: $(1.7241 - 2.3161)/0.2 = -2.96$, error $0.04$.
A finite difference computes a derivative from values of the function alone, which is what a program can do with a simulator whose equations it cannot differentiate by hand. Step 2 subtracts $J(z)$ from the expansion of $J(z + h)$ and divides by $h$; the first term left over is $\tfrac{h}{2}J''$, so halving $h$ halves the error. Step 3 subtracts the expansion of $J(z - h)$ from that of $J(z + h)$: the $J$ and $J''$ terms cancel and the $J'$ and $J'''$ terms double, and dividing by $2h$ leaves an error of $\tfrac{h^2}{6}J'''$. For the quartic at $z = 1$ the errors are exact polynomials: $2h + 4h^2 + h^3$ forward and $4h^2$ central (the fifth derivative is zero), so $0.241$ and $0.04$ at $h = 0.1$. The central difference costs two new evaluations per derivative, the forward difference one when $J(z)$ is already known. Both are options of MuJoCo's mjd_transitionFD (argument flg_centered), which deck 10 uses.

Roundoff error in floating-point subtraction

  • A double-precision number carries 53 binary digits, about 16 decimal digits. Near 1 the spacing of doubles is $\varepsilon = 2^{-52} \approx 2.2\times 10^{-16}$.
  • $1 + 10^{-12}$ is stored as $1 + 4504\,\varepsilon$, so the computer's $(1 + 10^{-12}) - 1$ is $1.000089\times 10^{-12}$: relative error $8.9\times 10^{-5}$.
  • $J(1 + 10^{-12})$ computes to $1.9999999999969997$ and $J(1) = 2$: their difference keeps about 4 correct digits.
  • The forward difference at $h = 10^{-12}$ has error $2.7\times 10^{-4}$, larger than at $h = 10^{-4}$ ($2.0\times 10^{-4}$).
Each value of $J$ carries an error of about $\varepsilon\abs{J}$. A difference divided by $h$ carries about $\varepsilon\abs{J}/h$, which grows as $h$ shrinks.
A double stores a number as a 53-bit binary fraction times a power of two, so between 1 and 2 the representable numbers are spaced $2^{-52}$ apart. $10^{-12}/\varepsilon = 4503.6$, and the nearest representable number to $1 + 10^{-12}$ is $1 + 4504\varepsilon = 1 + 1.000089\times 10^{-12}$. The subtraction itself is exact, but it exposes the rounding of the input: the result has only about four correct digits. This loss of digits when two nearly equal numbers are subtracted is called cancellation. The forward difference subtracts $J(z)$ from $J(z + h)$, two numbers that agree in their first 12 digits when $h = 10^{-12}$, then divides by $h$, which enlarges the rounding error by $10^{12}$. The truncation error of the previous slide shrinks with $h$ and the roundoff error grows as $h$ shrinks; the next slide plots both. The values are from d03_fd_forward and Python's own arithmetic; JavaScript uses the same IEEE 754 doubles, and the twin tests check that the slide's error curve matches the notebook's bit for bit.

Error of finite differences against the step size

  • Right branch: truncation error, slope 1 (forward) and slope 2 (central) on log-log axes.
  • Left branch: roundoff error, about $\varepsilon\abs{J}/h$.
  • At $z_0 = 1$ the forward error is smallest near $h = 10^{-8}$ (about $10^{-8}$); the central error near $h = 10^{-6}$ to $10^{-5}$ (about $10^{-11}$).
  • Rule of thumb for $\abs{z_0}$ near 1: $h \approx \sqrt\varepsilon = 1.5\times 10^{-8}$ forward, $h \approx \varepsilon^{1/3} = 6\times 10^{-6}$ central.

The total error of the forward difference is about $\tfrac{h}{2}\abs{J''} + \varepsilon\abs{J}/h$. The first term falls as $h$ shrinks and the second rises, so the sum is smallest where the two are comparable, at $h$ of order $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ for a function whose value and curvature are of order 1; the smallest error is then also of order $\sqrt\varepsilon$, about $10^{-8}$: half of the 16 digits are lost. The central difference balances $\tfrac{h^2}{6} \abs{J'''}$ against the same roundoff, which gives $h$ of order $\varepsilon^{1/3}$ and an error of order $\varepsilon^{2/3} \approx 10^{-11}$. On the grid of d03_fd_steps the smallest measured errors at $z_0 = 1$ are $5.5\times 10^{-9}$ at $h = 7\times 10^{-9}$ (forward) and $2.5\times 10^{-11}$ at $h = 2\times 10^{-6}$ (central). The left branch is jagged because rounding errors depend on the exact bits of each value. At $h = 10^{-15}$ the forward error is $0.55$: the difference $J(1 + h) - J(1)$ is almost all rounding. Where $J'' = 0$ (the inflection points) the forward difference loses its $O(h)$ term and behaves like the central one.
