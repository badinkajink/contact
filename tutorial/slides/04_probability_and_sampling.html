This deck builds the probability that sampling-based optimization uses. Part 1 defines outcomes, events, random variables, the mean and the variance on dice, where every number can be checked by counting. Part 2 moves to continuous distributions, the Gaussian density, and the way a computer draws random numbers (a seeded generator and the Box-Muller transform). Part 1

Probability and random variables

Outcomes and events, random variables, the mean and the variance, and what happens to the average of many repeated trials. Every number comes from counting dice.

Outcomes, events, and probability

  • An experiment has a set $\Omega$ of outcomes. One roll of a die: $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$.
  • An event is a set of outcomes. “Even” is $A = \{2, 4, 6\}$.
  • With equally likely outcomes, $P(A) = \abs{A} / \abs{\Omega}$, where $\abs{A}$ counts the outcomes in $A$: $P(\text{even}) = 3/6$.
  • Rules: $0 \le P(A) \le 1$; $P(\Omega) = 1$; $P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)$; $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)$ when $A$ and $B$ share no outcome.
  • With overlap, subtract the shared part once: $P(\text{even or} \ge 5) = \tfrac36 + \tfrac26 - \tfrac16 = \tfrac46$.
Probability starts with a list of everything that can happen, the set $\Omega$ of outcomes, and assigns each set of outcomes (each event) a number between 0 and 1. The rules on the slide are Kolmogorov's axioms in words, plus two consequences: the complement rule follows from $A$ and “not $A$” sharing no outcome and together making $\Omega$, so $P(A) + P(\text{not } A) = 1$. The overlap rule follows from counting: in $\abs{A} + \abs{B}$ every outcome in both sets is counted twice, so $\abs{A \text{ or } B} = \abs{A} + \abs{B} - \abs{A \text{ and } B}$. For “even or at least 5” the shared outcome is 6, and the union is $\{2, 4, 5, 6\}$, four outcomes. “Equally likely” is a modeling assumption about a fair die, not a consequence of the rules; a loaded die obeys the same rules with other numbers. The select box computes the event and its probability by counting the yellow faces.

Equally likely outcomes of two dice

  • Roll two dice. An outcome is the ordered pair $(a, b)$: 36 outcomes, each with probability $1/36$.
  • $(3, 4)$ and $(4, 3)$ are different outcomes: the first die shows 3 in one and 4 in the other.
  • The sum 7 comes from 6 outcomes, $(1, 6), (2, 5), \dots, (6, 1)$: $P(\text{sum} = 7) = 6/36 = 0.1667$. The sum 2 comes from $(1, 1)$ alone: $1/36$.
Treating the dice as distinguishable (a red die and a blue die, say) makes the 36 outcomes equally likely, which is what lets probabilities be computed by counting. If the pairs were unordered, $\{3, 4\}$ would be one outcome and $\{3, 3\}$ another, and these two would not be equally likely: $\{3, 4\}$ happens in two ways out of 36 and $\{3, 3\}$ in one. The counts of the sums form a triangle, $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1$ for sums 2 to 12, which add to 36. The count for sum $s$ is $6 - \abs{s - 7}$: the diagonal of the grid on which $a + b = s$ is longest through the middle of the square. Move the slider to see each diagonal. d04_two_dice_counts in the notebook counts the same cells by looping over all 36 pairs.

Independent events and repeated trials

  • Events $A$ and $B$ are independent when $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)\,P(B)$. Two dice: $P(\text{two sixes}) = \tfrac16\cdot\tfrac16 = \tfrac{1}{36}$, one cell of the grid.
  • At least one six in $k$ rolls: the complement is “no six in any roll”, with probability $(5/6)^k$, so $$P(\text{at least one six}) = 1 - (5/6)^k.$$
  • $k = 4$: $1 - 0.4823 = 0.5177$. $k = 12$: $0.8878$.
  • With success probability $p$ per trial: $P(\text{at least one success in } k) = 1 - (1 - p)^k$.
Independence means that knowing whether $B$ happened does not change the chances of $A$. For two dice, the first die's face does not affect the second's, and every event about the first die is independent of every event about the second: of the 36 cells, the six with $a = 6$ contain exactly one with $b = 6$, a fraction $1/6$ of them, the same as the fraction $6/36$ of all cells with $b = 6$. For repeated independent trials the product rule extends to $k$ factors: $P(\text{no six in } k \text{ rolls}) = (5/6)^k$. Counting “at least one” directly would need the cases “exactly one”, “exactly two”, and so on; the complement needs one line. The Chevalier de Méré bet on at least one six in four rolls and won slightly more than half the time, 0.5177. The last formula, $1 - (1 - p)^k$, is the success probability of $k$ random restarts of an optimizer (slide random-restarts) and the chance that a failure mode of probability $p$ shows up in $k$ random test problems (slide random-test-problems).

Random variables and probability mass functions

  • A random variable is a number computed from the outcome. For two dice, $X = a + b$ turns each of the 36 outcomes into a sum from 2 to 12.
  • Its probability mass function (pmf) lists $p(k) = P(X = k)$: $$p(k) = \frac{6 - \abs{k - 7}}{36}, \quad k = 2, \dots, 12.$$
  • The values of a pmf are at least 0 and add to 1: $(1 + 2 + \dots + 6 + \dots + 1)/36 = 36/36$.
  • Events about $X$ add pmf values: $P(X \le 4) = p(2) + p(3) + p(4) = \tfrac{1 + 2 + 3}{36} = \tfrac16$.
Capital letters ($X$, $Y$) name random variables and lowercase letters ($x$, $k$) the values they take, so $P(X = k)$ reads “the probability that the sum equals $k$”. A random variable is a function from outcomes to numbers; many outcomes can give the same value, and the pmf collects their probabilities: $p(7)$ is the total probability of the six outcomes on the diagonal $a + b = 7$ of the previous slide. Once the pmf is known the outcomes can be forgotten: every question about $X$ alone is answered by adding pmf values over the values in question. The same outcomes support other random variables: the larger of the two faces, $\max(a, b)$, has $p(k) = (2k - 1)/36$ for $k = 1, \dots, 6$, because the cells with maximum $k$ form an L-shaped strip of $2k - 1$ cells in the corner of the grid (next slide).

The mean as a probability-weighted average

$$\E[X] = \sum_k k\,p(k)$$
  • Each value is weighted by its probability. One die: $\E[X] = (1 + 2 + \dots + 6)\cdot\tfrac16 = 3.5$.
  • The mean is the balance point of the bars: a beam loaded with weight $p(k)$ at position $k$ balances on a support at $\E[X]$.
  • The mean need not be a possible value: no roll shows 3.5.
  • Larger of two dice, $p(k) = (2k - 1)/36$: $\E = \tfrac{1\cdot1 + 2\cdot3 + 3\cdot5 + 4\cdot7 + 5\cdot9 + 6\cdot11}{36} = \tfrac{161}{36} = 4.472$.

We write the mean $m = \E[X]$; $\mu$ is the friction coefficient in this series.

The mean (or expected value) $\E[X]$ is the average of the values of $X$, each counted as often as it is likely. It is a property of the distribution, not of any single roll. The balance-point picture is the center of mass: the torque of weight $p(k)$ at distance $k - m$ from the support is $p(k)(k - m)$, and the torques cancel exactly when $\sum_k p(k)(k - m) = \E[X] - m = 0$, using $\sum_k p(k) = 1$. For the sum of two dice the triangle is symmetric about 7, so the mean is 7. For the larger of two dice the bars grow to the right and the support moves to $161/36 = 4.472$, right of the middle value 3.5. The mean of a function of $X$ uses the same weights: $\E[g(X)] = \sum_k g(k)\,p(k)$, which the next slide applies to $g(X) = (X - m)^2$.

Variance and standard deviation

$$\Var X = \E\bigl[(X - m)^2\bigr], \qquad \sigma = \sqrt{\Var X}$$

  • One die, $m = 3.5$. The deviations $x - m$ average to 0, so their squares measure the spread.
  • $\Var X = 35/12 = 2.9167$ and $\sigma = 1.7078$, in the units of $X$.
  • Shortcut: $\Var X = \E[X^2] - m^2 = 15.1667 - 12.25 = 2.9167$.
The deviations from the mean always average to zero, $\E[X - m] = \E[X] - m = 0$, so the plain average deviation says nothing about spread. Squaring makes every deviation count positively and counts large deviations more. The variance is in squared units (dots squared, or m$^2$ for a position), so the standard deviation $\sigma = \sqrt{\Var X}$ is used to state spreads: a die is typically about 1.7 away from 3.5. The shortcut comes from expanding the square inside the mean: $(X - m)^2 = X^2 - 2mX + m^2$, and the mean of a sum is the sum of the means, so $\E[(X - m)^2] = \E[X^2] - 2m\,\E[X] + m^2 = \E[X^2] - 2m^2 + m^2 = \E[X^2] - m^2$. The shortcut is convenient by hand; in floating point it loses digits when $m$ is large compared with $\sigma$ (it subtracts two large, nearly equal numbers), which is why the notebook computes $\E[(X - m)^2]$ directly.

Rules for shifted, scaled, and summed random variables

Shift and scale ($a$, $b$ numbers):

$$\E[aX + b] = a\,\E[X] + b, \qquad \Var(aX + b) = a^2 \Var X.$$

The shift $b$ cancels in $(aX + b) - (am + b) = a(X - m)$.

Sum of $X$ and $Y$, with means $m_X$, $m_Y$:

Two dice: $\Var = \tfrac{35}{12} + \tfrac{35}{12} = \tfrac{35}{6} = 5.8333$, $\sigma = 2.4152$, not $2 \times 1.7078 = 3.4157$.
  1. $\Var(X + Y) = \E\bigl[\bigl((X - m_X) + (Y - m_Y)\bigr)^2\bigr]$
  2. $= \E[(X - m_X)^2] + 2\,\E[(X - m_X)(Y - m_Y)] + \E[(Y - m_Y)^2]$
  3. $= \Var X + 2\Cov(X, Y) + \Var Y$, with the covariance $\Cov(X, Y) = \E[(X - m_X)(Y - m_Y)]$.
  4. Independent $X$, $Y$: $\Cov(X, Y) = 0$, so $\Var(X + Y) = \Var X + \Var Y$.
Step 1 uses $\E[X + Y] = m_X + m_Y$ (the mean of a sum is the sum of the means: the sum over outcomes of $X + Y$ splits into two sums), so the deviation of $X + Y$ is the sum of the two deviations. Step 2 expands $(u + v)^2 = u^2 + 2uv + v^2$ and takes the mean term by term. Step 3 names the three terms. Step 4 needs one fact about independent variables: $\E[UV] = \E[U]\,\E[V]$. For discrete variables, $\E[UV] = \sum_{u, v} uv\,P(U = u, V = v) = \sum_{u, v} uv\,P(U = u)P(V = v) = \bigl(\sum_u u P(U = u)\bigr)\bigl(\sum_v v P(V = v)\bigr)$. With $U = X - m_X$ and $V = Y - m_Y$, both of mean zero, $\Cov(X, Y) = 0$. So variances of independent variables add, standard deviations do not: $\sigma_{X + Y} = \sqrt{\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2}$. The same rule for $N$ independent copies gives $\Var(X_1 + \dots + X_N) = N\sigma^2$, the fact behind the $1/\sqrt N$ error of averages (slide monte-carlo-error).

Histograms of repeated samples

Repeat the experiment $N$ times, independently. The results $x_1, \dots, x_N$ are samples of $X$.

$$\bar x = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^N x_i, \qquad s^2 = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - \bar x)^2$$

$\bar x$ and $s^2$ are the sample mean and sample variance. Sums of two dice, seed 1: $N = 100$ gives $6.68$, $5.998$; $N = 10^4$ gives $6.976$, $5.867$; $N = 10^5$ gives $6.996$, $5.802$. The exact values are 7 and 5.833.

A histogram counts how often each value appears among the samples. Divided by $N$, the counts are fractions, and the bars are drawn on the same scale as the pmf (red dots). With 10 samples the bars are ragged; by $10^4$ they sit within a few thousandths of the pmf. The sample mean and variance are the mean and variance of the samples themselves, each sample counted with weight $1/N$. Some texts divide the sample variance by $N - 1$ instead of $N$: the deviations are measured from $\bar x$, which sits closer to the samples than the true mean $m$ does, so $\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2$ is on average $(N - 1)\sigma^2$, not $N\sigma^2$. The difference is 1 % at $N = 100$ and does not matter for the large $N$ used in sampling. The cross-entropy method refits with $1/N$ (slide elite-fit). The samples are the rolls $1 + \lfloor 6u \rfloor$ of the generator of slide pseudo-random-generators, two uniforms per sum.

The law of large numbers

  • The sample mean of $N$ independent samples approaches the mean $m$ as $N$ grows: $\bar x_N \to m$.
  • Seed 1: $\bar x_N = 4.1$, $3.36$, $3.444$, $3.4856$ at $N = 10$, $10^2$, $10^3$, $10^4$.
  • The typical error shrinks like $\sigma/\sqrt N$ (derived on slide monte-carlo-error). Band half-width $2\sigma/\sqrt N$: 0.342 at $N = 100$, 0.108 at $N = 1000$.
  • At $N = 1000$, 193 of 200 seeds (96.5 %) lie inside the band.
The law of large numbers says that the average of $N$ independent samples of the same random variable converges to its mean. It does not say that a run of low rolls is later “corrected” by high ones: the early rolls are never undone, they are diluted by the many later ones. How fast the dilution works is the useful part. The variance of the average of $N$ independent samples is $\sigma^2/N$ (the sum rule of slide variance-rules gives $N\sigma^2$ for the sum; dividing by $N$ multiplies the variance by $1/N^2$), so its standard deviation is $\sigma/\sqrt N$ with $\sigma = 1.7078$ for a die. Two standard deviations cover about 95 % of a Gaussian (slide rule-68-95-997), and the fraction of 200 seeds inside the band, 96.5 %, is close to that. The slider picks which five seeds are drawn; the curves wander early and settle inside the narrowing band.

Part 2

Continuous distributions and random number generation

Densities, the uniform and Gaussian distributions, and how a computer turns a seed into uniform and Gaussian samples.

The uniform distribution and probability density

  • A continuous random variable can take any value in an interval. A single value has probability 0; an interval has a positive probability.
  • A density $p(x) \ge 0$ gives probabilities as areas: $P(a \le X \le c)$ is the area under $p$ from $a$ to $c$, written $\int_a^c p(x)\,\dd x$. The total area is 1.
  • Uniform on $[0, b]$: $p(x) = 1/b$ on the interval, 0 outside. $P(0.2 \le X \le 0.5) = 0.3/b$.
  • Mean $b/2$, variance $b^2/12$. For $U[0, 1]$: $1/12 = 0.0833$, $\sigma = 0.2887$.
  • A density can exceed 1: $U[0, 0.5]$ has $p(x) = 2$.
For a continuous variable, probabilities live on intervals: the chance that a uniform number on $[0, 1]$ equals exactly 0.3 is zero, but the chance that it lies in $[0.2, 0.5]$ is 0.3. The density is probability per unit length, so it has units of 1/(units of $X$) and can exceed 1 when the interval is shorter than 1. The integral sign is notation for area: cut $[a, c]$ into many thin strips of width $\Delta x$, and the area is the limit of $\sum_k p(x_k)\,\Delta x$. Means use the same weights as in the discrete case, with sums becoming areas: $\E[X] = \int x\,p(x)\,\dd x$. For $U[0, 1]$ the mean is $1/2$ by symmetry. For $\E[X^2]$ use the strip sum on the grid $x_k = k/n$: $\tfrac1n\sum_{k=1}^n (k/n)^2 = \tfrac{(n + 1)(2n + 1)}{6n^2}$, which is 0.385 for $n = 10$, 0.33835 for $n = 100$, and tends to $1/3$. So $\Var X = 1/3 - 1/4 = 1/12$. For $U[0, b]$, $X = bU$ with $U$ uniform on $[0, 1]$, and the scale rule gives mean $b/2$ and variance $b^2/12$.

The Gaussian density

$$p(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(x - m)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr)$$
  • Written $X \sim \Nrm(m, \sigma^2)$: mean $m$, variance $\sigma^2$. The standard normal $\Nrm(0, 1)$ has $m = 0$, $\sigma = 1$.
  • Peak $1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$ at $x = m$: 0.3989 for $\sigma = 1$, 0.7979 for $\sigma = 0.5$. Halving $\sigma$ doubles the peak; the area stays 1.
  • Symmetric about $m$; steepest at $m \pm \sigma$.
$\exp(t)$ means $e^t$. The exponent is $-\tfrac12$ times the squared distance from $m$ measured in units of $\sigma$, so the curve depends on $(x - m)/\sigma$ only: changing $m$ slides it, changing $\sigma$ stretches it sideways and squeezes it vertically by the same factor, which keeps the area at 1. The constant $\sqrt{2\pi}$ is the area under $e^{-x^2/2}$, proved on slide box-muller-radius. That $m$ is the mean follows from the symmetry about $m$; that $\sigma^2$ is the variance is a computation the notebook checks numerically. Gaussians appear whenever a quantity is the sum of many small independent effects (the central limit theorem): the pmf of the sum of two dice is already a triangle, and the sum of ten dice is close to a bell. Sampling-based controllers perturb their inputs with Gaussian noise because it is cheap to draw, fixed by two numbers per coordinate (or a mean vector and a covariance matrix), and has no hard edges. The readout checks the area by a strip sum with width 0.01.

Probability within $k$ standard deviations

  • $Z = (X - m)/\sigma$ is standard normal (next slide), so $P(\abs{X - m} \le k\sigma) = P(\abs{Z} \le k)$ depends on $k$ alone.
  • $\Phi(z) = P(Z \le z)$ is the area left of $z$. By symmetry, $P(\abs{Z} \le k) = 2\Phi(k) - 1$.
  • $k = 1, 2, 3$: 0.6827, 0.9545, 0.9973. One tail beyond $2\sigma$: 0.0228.
  • A gripper's position error $e \sim \Nrm(0, (2\text{ mm})^2)$: $P(\abs{e} > 4\text{ mm}) = 1 - 0.9545 = 4.55\,\%$.
  • 95.0 % of the probability lies within $1.96\sigma$.
Standardizing converts every Gaussian question into a question about $\Nrm(0, 1)$: subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation. $\Phi$ has no formula in terms of elementary functions; it is computed from the error function, $\Phi(z) = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + \operatorname{erf}(z/\sqrt2)\bigr)$, which numerical libraries provide (math.erf in Python; lib/algo/04_sampling.js sums a series). The symmetry step: the tails beyond $-k$ and beyond $k$ have equal areas $1 - \Phi(k)$, so the middle is $1 - 2(1 - \Phi(k)) = 2\Phi(k) - 1$. The three numbers 0.6827, 0.9545 and 0.9973 are worth memorizing as 68 %, 95 % and 99.7 %. In the gripper example the error exceeds 4 mm, two standard deviations, in about 1 grasp of 22. The value 1.96 makes the middle exactly 95 % and sets the width of the usual 95 % confidence interval of a Monte Carlo estimate: estimate $\pm 1.96\,\sigma_f/\sqrt N$ (slide monte-carlo-error). These numbers hold for one Gaussian coordinate; in two dimensions the fractions inside ellipses are smaller (slide covariance-ellipse).

Shifting and scaling a standard normal

  • If $\epsilon \sim \Nrm(0, 1)$, then $x = m + \sigma\epsilon \sim \Nrm(m, \sigma^2)$.
  • Mean and variance by the rules of slide variance-rules: $\E[x] = m + \sigma\cdot 0 = m$, $\Var x = \sigma^2\cdot 1$.
  • $\epsilon = (0.5, -1.2, 2.0)$, $m = 3$, $\sigma = 0.5$: $x = (3.25, 2.4, 4.0)$.
  • 2000 draws, seed 11, $m = 3$, $\sigma = 0.5$: sample mean 2.995, standard deviation 0.4971.
$x = m + \sigma\epsilon$ is the one-dimensional case of $\vx = \vm + L\veps$ with $LL^\T = \Sigma$ (slide cholesky-sampling).
Every Gaussian sample in this series is made from standard normals this way, so a generator only needs to produce $\Nrm(0, 1)$. The shape stays Gaussian because the map is a shift and a stretch: $P(x \le t) = P(\epsilon \le (t - m)/\sigma)$, so the density of $x$ at $t$ is the density of $\epsilon$ at $(t - m)/\sigma$ divided by $\sigma$ (stretching the axis by $\sigma$ divides the heights by $\sigma$ to keep the area 1). Written out, that is exactly the formula of slide gaussian-pdf. Standardizing, $\epsilon = (x - m)/\sigma$, is the same map run backwards. The figure keeps the 2000 draws of $\epsilon$ fixed (seed 11) and moves only $m$ and $\sigma$, so the blue histogram is the gray one slid and stretched; the readout recomputes the sample mean and standard deviation, which track $m$ and $\sigma$ with errors of about $\sigma/\sqrt{2000} = 0.022\sigma$.

Pseudo-random number generators and seeds

  • A pseudo-random number generator (PRNG) is a deterministic rule whose outputs look independent and uniform. The seed is its starting state: the same seed gives the same stream.
  • This series uses mulberry32 (lib/num.js): the $k$-th draw of seed $s$ is $u_k = h(s + k c \bmod 2^{32}) / 2^{32}$, with $c = \texttt{0x6D2B79F5}$ and $h$ a fixed mix of shifts, XORs and multiplications.
  • Die roll $1 + \lfloor 6u \rfloor$; uniform on $[a, b]$: $a + (b - a)u$.
A computer running a program has no dice, so “random” numbers come from a deterministic function whose outputs pass statistical tests of uniformity and independence. Seed 1 of mulberry32 gives 0.62707, 0.00274, 0.52745, 0.98105, 0.96838, 0.28110, so its first six die rolls are 4, 1, 4, 6, 6, 2. The counter $a = s + kc$ steps by an odd constant $c = 1831565813$, so it returns to its start only after $2^{32}$ draws, the period of the generator. The scatter of successive pairs fills the square without visible structure; old linear generators failed this kind of test (IBM's RANDU put all triples of successive draws on 15 planes in the unit cube). Seeds make experiments reproducible: a figure drawn with seed 1 is the same on every load, a failing random test problem can be rerun, and a paper's random test set can be regenerated. numpy's default generator is PCG64, a different stream; the notebook class D04Rng reproduces mulberry32 and its Box-Muller normals, so every sample statistic on these slides is recomputed in Python for the same seed (tools/twins/04.json checks the two agree bit for bit).

The Box-Muller transform

$$r = \sqrt{-2\ln u_1}, \quad \theta = 2\pi u_2$$ $$z_1 = r\cos\theta, \quad z_2 = r\sin\theta$$

Two independent uniforms give two independent standard normals. $u_2$ sets the angle; $u_1$ sets the radius, which is large when $u_1$ is small.

$u = (0.3, 0.6)$: $r = 1.5518$, $\theta = 216^\circ$, $\vz = (-1.2554, -0.9121)$.
G. E. P. Box and M. E. Muller published the transform in 1958. It turns a point of the unit square into a point of the plane in polar coordinates: the angle is $2\pi u_2$, uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$, and the radius is $\sqrt{-2\ln u_1}$. Because $\ln u_1 < 0$ for $u_1 < 1$, the square root is real; $u_1$ near 1 gives a radius near 0, and $u_1 = e^{-2} = 0.1353$ gives radius 2. The next slide shows why this radius makes the two coordinates independent standard normals. The worked example: $\ln 0.3 = -1.2040$, $r = \sqrt{2.4079} = 1.5518$; $\theta = 0.6 \times 360^\circ = 216^\circ$; $\cos 216^\circ = -0.8090$ and $\sin 216^\circ = -0.5878$. In Num.rng the first uniform of a pair is replaced by $1 - u$ so that it lies in $(0, 1]$ and $\ln u_1$ is finite, and the second normal of each pair is kept for the next call. The colors show that each quarter of the $u_2$ range fills one quadrant of the plane.

The radius of a two-dimensional standard normal sample

  • For $\vz = (z_1, z_2)$ with independent standard normal entries, the fraction of draws beyond radius $r$ is $$P(R > r) = e^{-r^2/2}.$$
  • $r = 1, 2, 3$: 0.6065, 0.1353, 0.0111. 2000 draws: 0.6085, 0.1110, 0.0095.
  • Box-Muller sets $e^{-R^2/2} = u_1$, so $R = \sqrt{-2\ln u_1}$ and $P(R > r) = P(u_1 < e^{-r^2/2}) = e^{-r^2/2}$.
  • The density $\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2}$ depends on the radius only, so the angle is uniform: $\theta = 2\pi u_2$.
The joint density of two independent standard normals is the product $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z_1^2/2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z_2^2/2} = \tfrac{1}{2\pi}e^{-\rho^2/2}$ with $\rho^2 = z_1^2 + z_2^2$. A thin ring of radius $\rho$ and width $\dd\rho$ has area $2\pi\rho\,\dd\rho$, so it holds probability $\rho e^{-\rho^2/2}\,\dd\rho$. The function $-e^{-\rho^2/2}$ has slope $\rho e^{-\rho^2/2}$ (chain rule), so adding up the rings from $r$ outward gives $0 - (-e^{-r^2/2}) = e^{-r^2/2}$. At $r = 0$ this is 1: the rings hold all the probability, which proves that $1/(2\pi)$ is the right constant in two dimensions and, taking the square root, that $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ is the right one in one dimension. The step line is the inverse-transform idea: to make $R$ with $P(R > r) = G(r)$, set $G(R) = u$ for a uniform $u$, because $P(u < t) = t$. In two dimensions only 39.35 % of the draws lie within radius 1, against 68.27 % of one coordinate within one standard deviation.

Part 3

Random vectors, covariance, and Monte Carlo estimates

Covariance matrices and their ellipses, sampling a Gaussian vector with a Cholesky factor, and the error of averages of samples.

Covariance and correlation

  • $\Cov(X, Y) = \E[(X - m_X)(Y - m_Y)]$ (slide variance-rules) is positive when $X$ and $Y$ tend to lie above their means together, negative when one tends to lie above while the other lies below.
  • Correlation: $\corr(X, Y) = \Cov(X, Y) / (\sigma_X\sigma_Y)$, a number in $[-1, 1]$ without units.
Points $(1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5), (4, 4), (5, 6)$: means $(3, 4)$; products of deviations $4, 1, 0, 0, 4$; covariance $9/5 = 1.8$; variances 2 and 2; correlation $1.8/2 = 0.9$.
The sign of each product $(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)$ says whether a sample sits in the upper-right or lower-left quadrant around the mean (positive) or in one of the other two (negative); the covariance is their average. Its units are the units of $X$ times those of $Y$, which is why the correlation divides by both standard deviations. The sample covariance is a dot product: with the deviation vectors $\vd_x = (-2, -1, 0, 1, 2)$ and $\vd_y = (-2, -1, 1, 0, 2)$, $\Cov = \vd_x\cdot\vd_y/5$ and $\corr = \vd_x\cdot\vd_y/(\norm{\vd_x}\norm{\vd_y}) = 9/10$, the cosine of the angle between them (deck 01, dot-product-angle). A cosine lies in $[-1, 1]$, and equals $\pm 1$ only when the deviations are proportional, that is when $Y = aX + b$ exactly. Independent variables have correlation 0; the converse fails ($X$ uniform on $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and $Y = X^2$ are uncorrelated but $Y$ is fixed by $X$). The figure draws its pairs with the Cholesky method of slide cholesky-sampling, from the same 1000 standard normals for every $r$.

The covariance matrix

A random vector $\vx = (x_1, x_2)$ has mean $\vm = \E[\vx]$ and covariance matrix

$$\Sigma = \E\bigl[(\vx - \vm)(\vx - \vm)^\T\bigr] = \begin{bmatrix} \Var x_1 & \Cov(x_1, x_2) \\ \Cov(x_1, x_2) & \Var x_2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

Variance along a unit vector $\va$, with $\vd = \vx - \vm$:

  1. $\Var(\va^\T\vx) = \E\bigl[(\va^\T\vd)^2\bigr]$
  2. $\phantom{\Var(\va^\T\vx)} = \E[\va^\T\vd\,\vd^\T\va] = \va^\T\,\E[\vd\vd^\T]\,\va$
  3. $\phantom{\Var(\va^\T\vx)} = \va^\T\Sigma\,\va \ge 0$
The outer product $\vd\vd^\T$ is the $2\times 2$ matrix with entries $d_i d_j$ (deck 01, transpose); its mean has the variances on the diagonal and the covariance off it, and $\Sigma$ is symmetric. Step 1 uses $\E[\va^\T\vx] = \va^\T\vm$, so the deviation of $\va^\T\vx$ is $\va^\T\vd$, the shadow of $\vd$ on $\va$ (deck 01, dot-product). Step 2 writes the square of the number $\va^\T\vd$ as $(\va^\T\vd)(\vd^\T\va)$ and moves the fixed $\va$ outside the mean. Step 3 is the result: the variance in direction $\va$ is the quadratic form $\va^\T\Sigma\va$ of deck 02 (quadratic-forms), and because a variance is never negative, every covariance matrix is positive semidefinite (positive-definite). For $\Sigma = [[4, 2], [2, 3]]$ the variance is 4 along $x$, 3 along $y$, $\tfrac12(4 + 2\cdot 2 + 3) = 5.5$ at $45^\circ$ and $\tfrac12(4 - 4 + 3) = 1.5$ at $-45^\circ$. Its largest and smallest values over all directions are the eigenvalues of $\Sigma$ (slide covariance-ellipse). The readout compares $\va^\T\Sigma\va$ with the sample variance of the 1000 projections (seed 3).

The multivariate Gaussian density

$$p(\vx) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^n\det\Sigma}}\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac12(\vx - \vm)^\T\Sigma^{-1}(\vx - \vm)\Bigr), \qquad \vx \in \R^n$$
  • Written $\vx \sim \Nrm(\vm, \Sigma)$, with $\Sigma$ positive definite. For $n = 1$ and $\Sigma = \sigma^2$ it is the density of slide gaussian-pdf.
  • $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$: $\det\Sigma = 8$, $\Sigma^{-1} = \tfrac18\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, peak $1/(2\pi\sqrt8) = 0.05627$.
  • Level sets are the ellipses $(\vx - \vm)^\T\Sigma^{-1}(\vx - \vm) = k^2$; $k$ is the Mahalanobis distance from $\vm$.
  • Diagonal $\Sigma$: the density is a product of 1D densities, and the entries are independent.
The exponent is the one-dimensional $-(x - m)^2/(2\sigma^2)$ with the division by $\sigma^2$ replaced by the inverse matrix: it measures the distance from $\vm$ in units of the spread, differently in every direction. The factor $\det\Sigma$ in the constant plays the role of $\sigma^2$: a wider cloud has a lower peak, and the total volume under $p$ stays 1. The figure is a heat map of $p$ for $\vm = \vzero$ with the ellipses $k = 1, 2, 3$; at the Mahalanobis distance $k$ the density is $e^{-k^2/2}$ times the peak. For a diagonal $\Sigma = \diag(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2)$ the inverse is $\diag(1/\sigma_1^2, 1/\sigma_2^2)$, the exponent is $-x_1^2/(2\sigma_1^2) - x_2^2/(2\sigma_2^2)$, the exponential of a sum is a product, and $\det\Sigma = \sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2$ splits the constant: $p(\vx) = p_1(x_1)\,p_2(x_2)$. For Gaussian vectors, uncorrelated entries are therefore independent; for other distributions that is false (slide covariance). The inverse of a $2\times 2$ matrix swaps the diagonal, negates the off-diagonal and divides by the determinant (deck 01, identity-and-inverse).

Covariance ellipses from eigenvectors


  • Spectral theorem (deck 02, spectral-theorem): $\Sigma = V\diag(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2)V^\T$, orthonormal eigenvectors $\vv_1$, $\vv_2$ in the columns of $V$.
  • The $k$-$\sigma$ ellipse $(\vx - \vm)^\T\Sigma^{-1}(\vx - \vm) = k^2$ has semi-axes $k\sigma_1$ along $\vv_1$ and $k\sigma_2$ along $\vv_2$.
  • $\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$: $\sigma_1^2 = 5.5616$, $\sigma_2^2 = 1.4384$; $\sigma_1 = 2.3583$, $\sigma_2 = 1.1994$; $\vv_1$ at $37.98^\circ$.
In two dimensions, $P(\text{inside the } k\text{-}\sigma\text{ ellipse}) = 1 - e^{-k^2/2}$: 0.3935, 0.8647, 0.9889 for $k = 1, 2, 3$.
Rotate coordinates with $\vy = V^\T(\vx - \vm)$. Then $(\vx - \vm)^\T\Sigma^{-1}(\vx - \vm) = \vy^\T\diag(1/\sigma_1^2, 1/\sigma_2^2)\vy = y_1^2/\sigma_1^2 + y_2^2/\sigma_2^2$, and setting this to $k^2$ gives an ellipse with semi-axes $k\sigma_1$ and $k\sigma_2$ along the eigenvectors. The eigenvalues of a symmetric $2\times 2$ matrix are $\tfrac{a + d}{2} \pm \sqrt{\bigl(\tfrac{a - d}{2}\bigr)^2 + b^2} = 3.5 \pm \sqrt{4.25} = 3.5 \pm 2.0616$ (deck 02, eigen-2x2). They are written $\sigma_i^2$ here because they are the largest and smallest directional variances $\va^\T\Sigma\va$ of the previous slide; $\lambda$ is kept for other uses in this series. The sliders build $\Sigma$ from $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$ and the angle of $\vv_1$ and draw 1000 points $\vx = V\diag(\sigma_1, \sigma_2)\veps$ from fixed standard normals $\veps$ (seed 7). For each point $(\vx - \vm)^\T\Sigma^{-1}(\vx - \vm) = \norm{\veps}^2$, so it lies inside the $k$-$\sigma$ ellipse exactly when $\norm{\veps} \le k$, which has probability $1 - e^{-k^2/2}$ by slide box-muller-radius. The fractions in the readout (0.394, 0.888, 0.993) therefore do not change as the sliders move. These are not the one-dimensional 68-95-99.7 numbers: a 2-$\sigma$ ellipse holds 86 %, not 95 %.

Sampling a Gaussian vector with a Cholesky factor

Goal: samples of $\Nrm(\vm, \Sigma)$ from a vector $\veps$ of independent standard normals.

$\vx = \vm + L\veps$ with $LL^\T = \Sigma$ ($L$ the Cholesky factor, deck 02, cholesky).
  1. $\E[\vx] = \vm + L\,\E[\veps] = \vm$.
  2. $\vx - \vm = L\veps$, so $\Cov\vx = \E[L\veps\veps^\T L^\T] = L\,\E[\veps\veps^\T]\,L^\T$.
  3. $\E[\veps\veps^\T] = I$: $\E[\epsilon_i^2] = 1$, and $\E[\epsilon_i\epsilon_j] = 0$ for $i \ne j$.
  4. $\Cov\vx = LIL^\T = \Sigma$.

$\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$: $L = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & \sqrt2 \end{bmatrix}$.

$\veps = (0.5, -1)$: $L\veps = (1,\ 0.5 - 1.4142) = (1, -0.9142)$.

$\vm = (1, 2)$: $\vx = (2, 1.0858)$.

Any $L$ with $LL^\T = \Sigma$ works, for example $V\diag(\sigma_1, \sigma_2)$ of the previous slide. Cholesky costs about $n^3/3$ operations for $n\times n$ and is the usual choice.

Step 1 uses linearity of the mean: $\E[L\veps] = L\,\E[\veps]$, because each entry of $L\veps$ is a fixed combination of the $\epsilon_j$. Step 2 uses $(L\veps)^\T = \veps^\T L^\T$ (deck 01, transpose of a product) and moves the fixed matrices out of the mean. Step 3 is the covariance of independent standard normals: unit variances on the diagonal and zero covariances off it (slide variance-rules). In one dimension $L = \sigma$ and the recipe is $x = m + \sigma\epsilon$ (slide shift-and-scale). Matching the mean and covariance is not yet the whole claim: a linear map of a Gaussian vector is again Gaussian (substituting $\veps = L^{-1}(\vx - \vm)$ into the density of $\veps$ gives the formula of slide multivariate-gaussian, with $\norm{L^{-1}(\vx - \vm)}^2 = (\vx - \vm)^\T\Sigma^{-1}(\vx - \vm)$ and $\det L = \sqrt{\det\Sigma}$), so $\vx \sim \Nrm(\vm, \Sigma)$. The factor exists only for a positive definite $\Sigma$ (deck 02, cholesky-exists). Num.rng(seed).mvnormal(m, S) in lib/num.js implements this recipe, and the cross-entropy method samples this way in every iteration (deck 10).

Correlated samples from independent ones

At the default $\Sigma = [[4, 2], [2, 3]]$ the sample mean of the 500 draws is $(1.009, 1.994)$ and the sample covariance $[[3.733, 1.892], [1.892, 3.134]]$: close to $\vm$ and $\Sigma$, with errors of the size $1/\sqrt{500} \approx 0.045$ relative to the spread (slide monte-carlo-error). No random vector has such a covariance, because some direction would have negative variance (slide covariance-matrix). Monte Carlo estimates of expectations

$$\E[f(X)] \approx \hat f_N = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N f(x_i)$$
  • $x_1, \dots, x_N$ are independent samples of $X$; the law of large numbers makes $\hat f_N \to \E[f(X)]$.
  • A probability is the mean of an indicator: $P(A) = \E[\ind_A]$, where $\ind_A = 1$ when $A$ happens and 0 otherwise.
  • $\pi/4$ is the probability that a uniform point of the unit square lands in the quarter disk $u_1^2 + u_2^2 \le 1$, so $\pi \approx 4 \times$ (fraction inside).
  • Seed 1: 3.28 ($N = 100$), 3.188 ($10^3$), 3.1364 ($10^4$), 3.14264 ($10^5$).
Monte Carlo replaces a sum or an area that is hard to compute by an average over random samples. The name comes from the casino in Monaco, given by Metropolis and Ulam (1949) to the method they used for neutron transport at Los Alamos. The estimate is itself random: another seed gives another number. For the quarter disk the exact fraction is $\pi/4 = 0.7854$ (the area of a quarter of the unit disk), so four times the fraction estimates $\pi$. In sampling-based model predictive control (deck 10) $X$ is a random perturbation of the control inputs and $f$ the cost of the rollout it produces; the average cost of a distribution of inputs is estimated by exactly this formula. Each point uses two uniforms from Num.rng(seed), first $u_1$ then $u_2$, and the notebook function d04_mc_pi counts the same points.

The $1/\sqrt N$ error of Monte Carlo estimates

$\sigma_f^2 = \Var f(X)$; the samples $x_i$ are independent.

  1. $\E[\hat f_N] = \frac1N\sum_i\E[f(x_i)] = \E[f(X)]$: no bias.
  2. $\Var\hat f_N = \frac{1}{N^2}\sum_i\Var f(x_i)$: variances of independent terms add, and the factor $\frac1N$ is squared.
  3. $\Var\hat f_N = \frac{N\sigma_f^2}{N^2} = \frac{\sigma_f^2}{N}$, standard deviation $\sigma_f/\sqrt N$.
  • $\pi$: $f = 4\cdot\ind$, $\sigma_f = 4\sqrt{p(1 - p)} = 1.6422$ with $p = \pi/4$.
  • RMS error over 200 seeds: 0.1531 at $N = 100$ (predicted 0.1642), 0.01569 at $N = 10^4$ (predicted 0.01642).
Four times the samples halve the error; each extra correct digit costs 100 times the samples.
Step 2 combines the two rules of slide variance-rules: $\Var(aY) = a^2\Var Y$ with $a = 1/N$, and the variance of a sum of independent terms is the sum of their variances. An indicator takes the value 1 with probability $p$ and 0 otherwise, so its mean is $p$, its mean square is also $p$, and its variance is $p - p^2 = p(1 - p)$; multiplying by 4 multiplies the variance by 16. The root-mean-square (RMS) error is the square root of the average squared error over many seeds, an estimate of the standard deviation of $\hat f_N$; the measured values sit about 5 % below the prediction because 200 seeds give only a rough estimate of a standard deviation. The derivation never used the dimension of $X$: the error of an average falls as $1/\sqrt N$ in one dimension or in a thousand, with only the constant $\sigma_f$ depending on the problem. Finding a small region of low cost is a different matter, and in high dimension it is hard (tex lines 2406-2416, deck 10). On log-log axes the power law $N^{-1/2}$ is a straight line of slope $-1/2$.

Smoothing a step cost by averaging over noise

  • Step cost: $J(y) = 1$ for $y < 0$, $J(y) = 0$ for $y \ge 0$. Its slope is 0 wherever it exists, so a gradient gives no direction.
  • Smoothed cost: $J_\sigma(z) = \E[J(z + \sigma\epsilon)] = P(\epsilon < -z/\sigma) = \Phi(-z/\sigma)$.
  • Slope at $z = 0$: $-1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$, which is $-1.330$ for $\sigma = 0.3$.
  • Monte Carlo with $N = 200$ (seed 1), $\sigma = 0.3$: 0.935, 0.44, 0.025 at $z = -0.5, 0, 0.5$; exact 0.9522, 0.5, 0.0478.
  • Large $\sigma$: smooth and wide. Small $\sigma$: close to the step. Annealing starts with a large $\sigma$ and shrinks it (DIAL-MPC, deck 10).
A cost that jumps (a grasp that holds or drops, a contact that is made or lost) has zero slope almost everywhere, so gradient methods (deck 05) see nothing. Averaging the cost over random perturbations of the input turns the jump into a ramp: $z + \sigma\epsilon$ falls below 0 with probability $\Phi(-z/\sigma)$, which slides smoothly from 1 to 0 over a width of a few $\sigma$. The slope at 0 is the Gaussian density at 0, $1/\sqrt{2\pi} = 0.3989$, divided by $\sigma$ and negated (chain rule, deck 03). Sampling-based optimizers never form $J_\sigma$ explicitly; they evaluate $J$ at perturbed inputs, which amounts to optimizing the smoothed cost. The width $\sigma$ trades bias against shape: a large $\sigma$ gives a useful slope far from the jump but moves the smoothed cost away from $J$; a small one keeps $J_\sigma$ close to $J$ but flat almost everywhere. DIAL-MPC and other annealed methods (tex lines 2359-2388) run rounds with decreasing $\sigma$. Deck 22 applies the same averaging to contact. With few samples the red estimates are noisy, with standard deviation $\sqrt{J_\sigma(1 - J_\sigma)/N}$ (slide monte-carlo-error).
